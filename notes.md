## Part A — The big structural fixes

### A1. The rule for time gradients (new, planted early, used everywhere)

v1 never tells the reader *where* time gradients form, so every boundary (the blister wall, the
Shear, the Carousel seam) arrives exactly where the plot needs it. That's the root of the "Ben's
box" problem. v2 gives the story one physical rule the reader can learn in Part One and then use
to predict what will happen:

> **Time behaves like pressure, and matter resists it.**
> 1. In open air, a fast region thins out smoothly with distance (roughly like the square of the
>    distance). Stand a few metres from a filament and you're nearly at ordinary time.
> 2. Solid matter resists the difference, so where fast and slow time meet across something solid,
>    **the whole difference ("the drop") bunches up inside the solid** — a wall, a door, a pane of
>    glass. Denser material holds more. Keelstone holds the most (that is what the Lattice is *for*).
> 3. So a filament inside an **open** room makes a fast patch a few metres wide. A filament inside a
>    **sealed** room brings the *whole room* up to its speed and the walls hold the entire drop.
>    That's a **blister**.
> 4. Open a door in a blister and the drop has nowhere to go but through the doorway — and through
>    whoever is in it. It then sweeps outward as a front. You can't vent a blister from inside, and
>    you can't open it from outside, without the front going through someone.
> 5. You can't build a road *around* a drop; you can only stretch it out. Nobody built a
>    "gradient-proof stair" into the Vault because the Draw was designed to run at ordinary time — the
>    Lattice was meant to hold a drop of a few seconds a day. That's also why the placard says
>    DO NOT ATTEMPT TO TRAVERSE THE LATTICE.

Why this works for the book:
- It uses the user's idea (resistance through a medium + falloff with distance) but avoids the two
  traps: suits don't become gradients (a drop needs *different* times on the two sides; a centimetre
  of suit in uniform time holds nothing), and there's no emergency staircase (see 5).
- Ben (an electronics engineer) gets to name the analogy: *it's a voltage divider* — the biggest drop
  sits across the biggest resistance.
- It explains things the story already had but never justified: why Holdfast exists at all (the
  Vault is one giant blister and the Lattice is its glass), why the Shear sits in the conduit where it
  passes through the Lattice (a hole through the dam), why the storeroom thread only aged things a
  metre or two around it (the storeroom's doors were open), why the Crown filled with dust (a
  keelstone room with a thread in it), and why Section 9 says what it says.

**Where it's planted (v2):**
- Ch 1 — the drone's clock now ran near-normal for the whole flight *except* for ninety seconds near a
  thread in an open hall, when it gained ten hours. Cas explains open-air falloff and the Lattice as a
  dam for time.
- Ch 2 — Cas's "Shear" prediction now follows from the rule (the Lattice will hold the whole drop).
- Ch 3 — at the storeroom, the doors are propped open and Cas notes it; Ben plants his knowledge of
  the "Hammond" containment locks; a new short scene at a **sealed locker room with a thread inside it**
  shows a blister before B-7: the wired glass rippling, dust and ruin inside, Ben's pole jumping at the
  glass. Ben names it. The team states the rule out loud: *never open a room with a thread in it, never
  shut yourself in one.*
- Ch 5 — at the Shear Cas recognises the Vault as one enormous blister.

### A2. Ben's blister (Ch 3 end – Ch 4) — rebuilt so it's earned

**v1 problem.** The bulkhead is "on the manual lock… must have sealed when the power went funny";
Ben runs off to cycle it; at the moment he touches the lever the thread moves *and* every door in the
hall slams *and* the half-open bulkhead reverses; the boundary happens to sit exactly at the glass.
Four coincidences in a row, and the reason for opening the door is thrown at the reader in the same
breath as the danger.

**Brainstorm (options considered):**
1. *Keep the accident, add a rule* — the rule alone explains why the boundary is at the glass, but the
   slamming doors and the reason for going in are still convenient.
2. *Ben causes it* (cycling the door changes the stresses the filament follows) — clever, but it makes
   Ben's death his own fault in a way that sours eleven years of him being lovely.
3. *They walk into a known blister on purpose* — no; the reader would ask why nobody stopped them.
4. **A known, weighed risk taken for a good reason** — chosen. The reader knows the rule, sees the
   danger coming, understands why the choice is still reasonable, and watches it go wrong in the one
   way everyone said it could. It also rhymes with the Prologue: Cas does the sum and thinks *it'll
   probably be fine* — Ruth's sentence — without knowing it's hers. The reader knows.

**How it now plays:**
- B-7 is a bioscrubber hall — **sealed by design** (algae containment). The filament is running down
  the *inside* of the far wall "like a vein under skin"; the wall is holding it, so the hall is only
  fast near that end (the near tanks look normal, the far tanks are aged). The team says out loud what
  happens if the thread comes off the wall into the air of a *sealed* hall.
- Crossing the hall means spending ten minutes inside a sealed room with a thread in its wall — the
  exact thing they've just agreed never to do. So the safe move is to **vent it first**: open the main
  bulkhead so the hall becomes part of the open Works. If the thread comes out after that, it only
  makes a fast patch at the far end, like the storeroom.
- The Hammond containment bulkhead can only be opened **from inside** (so nobody can breach
  containment from the corridor), and the personnel lock beside it is interlocked — one door at a time.
  So one person has to go in, alone, with the hall sealed behind them, for about five minutes, to bleed
  the hydraulics and crank the bulkhead. Ben knows the lock (planted earlier). Teo offers; Ben is
  faster. The long way round is three hours through gravity weather that eats keel spin they'll need
  at the Shear.
- Cas is the technical lead and makes the call. He does the arithmetic on how often threads jump and
  thinks *it'll probably be fine.*
- While Ben is bleeding the lock (bulkhead still closed), the thread jumps off the wall, across the
  ceiling and into the floor. No doors slam — they were already shut, which is the point. The gallery
  glass visibly ripples like heat haze and stays that way: the drop is sitting in it, as they saw at
  the locker room.
- Ben's first message says why he can't come out: opening *any* door puts the whole drop through the
  doorway and sends a front down the corridor and up the stair — through them — and it would go
  through him on the way. He's safe as long as the glass holds, and he can't leave. The reader
  already knows every piece of this.
- Aftermath: in Ch 5 Cas's grief-arithmetic now includes *I said yes*; Teo answers it. In Ch 13 the
  confession ties the pen to the same sentence he thought at the top of the stair.

### A3. The time model — making "more time, closer to the core" clear

The idea is great and the v1 world bible has a solid model — **R(t) ≈ 250,000 · e^(−t/5.6 h)** —
but the prose rarely *spends* it where the reader can see it, and several numbers drift. v2 makes the
advantage explicit at the key moments:

- Ch 3 — Ben: "we've got more time than we thought." v1 Cas: "We've got the same amount of time. We're
  just spending more of it." That line reads as wrong. v2: the deadline (the Settlement) is on the
  *outside* clock, so yes, every hour in here costs the world forty minutes — **but the air, the suit
  power and the keel are on ours.** And it gets better further in.
- Ch 8 — Perpetua: an Outside Hour now takes "about a month" (v1 said *four months*; the model gives
  28 days). Cas then does the conversion at the sighting tube: forty-odd rounds of Holdfast is about
  two outside hours — hour ~36, not the two or three days he told Rask — and the only reason anyone
  can do anything about it is that they're living at seven hundred to one.
- Ch 10 — the banner (ROUND 48) is converted for the reader: two and a bit hours outside.
- Ch 12 — the climb: the time rate rises smoothly with height (open air, rule 1), so the city below
  slows from lazy smoke at 2 km to a photograph at 3 km. Numbers retuned so the shutter-wave, the
  last Bell and the morning shutter all agree (see B-table).

## Part D — Second pass: dialogue and prose

A slower re-read of v2 done one chapter at a time, listening for lines that don't sound like a person
talking, or sentences that trip. The first pass was mostly about physics and numbers. This one is about
the ear. Where a fix only worked by rewriting the lines around it, I rewrote the passage instead of
patching the line.

### The drills scene (Ch 2, *Keel*) — rewritten

**The problem.** Ben says "Just think about touching it", and Teo answers "I'm thinking about touching
you, Farr." Whatever it was meant to be, it reads as a come-on. It isn't something a frustrated person
says, and Ben's "that's fair" doesn't rescue it. A few lines later Teo says "Sounds like my marriage"
about the radios, and she's a widow, which is an odd joke for her to reach for. The Lees also quotes
that line back later, and puts it in the canteen when it was actually said in the hangar.

**Options I considered:**
1. *Keep the shape and swap Teo's reply* ("I'm thinking about throwing it at you"). It's the smallest
   change, but the scene would still be a joke with no job, and the new line is still a bandaid.
2. *Cut the exchange.* This loses the only light moment before the Works, and Cas's first laugh a page
   later would come from nowhere.
3. *Let the grip-assist actually do something.* Teo tries exactly what Ben says, and the gauntlet folds
   the spanner in half. The joke comes from the machine, not from innuendo. It also plants the
   grip-assist, which pays off in Ch 13, when Cas's gauntlet crushes the fibres of Ione's rope as he
   catches her. And it gives Ben a moment of nervous oversharing, which is exactly his voice.
4. *Make the spanner a running gag through the drills.* This was too much. It would crowd the radio
   line and Cas's laugh.

**Chosen:** option 3. Teo gently closes her hand and the spanner folds "like a sandwich". Ben says "…Or
that." Teo asks "Is this coming out of my pay?" Ben says the Ministry isn't counting spanners today, then adds: "Everybody does one. I did a coffee mug.
And a technician's hand, a bit. He was fine. He was mostly fine." Then Teo picks up the next spanner
like an egg, and it stays a spanner.

The radio joke now becomes "Sounds like working for the Commonweal". That's a dry line about a
government that runs on not saying things, and it's said in front of a man who signed a report and
couldn't talk about it for fifteen years. That's why it's Cas who laughs. The Lees callback is updated
to the hangar, the spanner and the new line.
