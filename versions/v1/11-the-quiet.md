---
title: The Quiet
---

> Keep them calm. It is the only mercy I have left to give, and God knows I owe them one.
> — Private journal of Aldous Crane, Year 2 of the Watch (never published)

In the Quiet, there was nothing to do but count and think, and he was very good at both.

He counted the Bells. He'd lost the number the first time, in the dark, when the lid came down; so he started again from one. One. Two. He counted his breaths between them, to know the interval. About six hundred and forty. Eleven minutes, near enough. He counted the rivets he could reach on the curved steel wall of the tank by feel, running his fingers along the seams, and found there were two hundred and eight, and counted them again, and found two hundred and eight again, and felt a small, pathetic, animal comfort in that. He counted his heartbeats. He counted the drips from somewhere overhead that fell, every so often, into a puddle in the middle of the floor he couldn't see: one every forty breaths, give or take.

And he thought.

He thought about forty-seven.

He tried not to. He'd been trying not to since the well, and he was no better at not thinking about things than he'd ever been. He thought about the father on the ladder. *I'll come back for him. When I've seen it.* He thought about the sound that had come up the conduit, that long, slow, stretched lowing, and what it had actually been — what forty metres of Shear did to a body going down it without a keel — and he knew exactly what it had been, because he'd seen the body at the bottom of Conduit 14, and he'd done the arithmetic, and he could not stop doing it now. He thought about Seren, who'd been the cleverest Toller in two hundred years, and he thought about the Tollers' ledgers, the names in careful ink. He thought about Ione going down under the crowd at the doors.

He'd said it three times. *The Drop is not open.* Three times.

And they had heard *the Outside is alive, and he came up it*, and nothing else. Just as Perpetua had said. He'd known they would. He'd known it the way you know a sum. And he had said it anyway, because it was true, and because he had decided he was going to be a man who said true things, and forty-seven people had heard the true thing he said and climbed down a ladder toward it and been torn to pieces in the dark.

That was what truth did. That was what it had done, the first time he'd tried it in fifteen years.

He sat with his back against the steel and his knees up and the Bell coming up through the floor, and he found himself in a place he knew very well. He had lived in it for fifteen years. It was quite a small place, and quite a cold one, and it had a very simple shape. It went like this: *Everything I do adds to it.* The lie added to it. The truth added to it. Silence added to it; he'd tried silence, for fifteen years, and every day of silence had been another day of the Draw eating the future, another day of the Margin falling, another day of interest on interest on interest. There was no move that didn't make it bigger. There was no way to pay it down. There was only the debt, growing, and him inside it, and the only question that had ever mattered was how and when he could stop.

He'd come into the Draw thinking the Shear might settle it. Or the Crown. Or whatever was in the Crown. *At last*, he'd thought, at the window in the Collar. *Almost a relief.*

He thought it now, in the dark. He didn't argue with it.

Then he thought about Teo, in a chair, saying *oh no you don't*, and he put his forehead down on his knees and stayed like that for a long time.

* * *

He'd counted nine hundred and forty Bells — no. He'd lost it again. He'd counted something. It had been a long time. His mouth was dry. There was water in the puddle and he'd drunk from it, on his knees, lapping at it with his hand like a dog, and it had tasted of rust.

The lid opened.

Light came down. Not much — a lamp, a single flame — but after the dark it was like a knife in his eyes, and he threw his arm over his face. When he lowered it, there was a figure coming down the ladder. Slowly, carefully, an old man's descent, one rung at a time, with a lamp in one hand and something under his other arm.

Halloran Crane reached the bottom. He stood for a moment, getting his breath. Then he set the lamp down on the steel floor between them, and sat down beside it, cross-legged, with a small grunt, his long black coat pooling round him. He put the thing from under his arm in his lap. It was a book.

For a while he was silent. He looked at the lamp. Cas looked at him.

"I used to come down here as a boy," said Crane at last. "My father was Steward. He used to bring me. He'd lower me in, and shut the lid, and leave me for an hour. Sometimes two. He said a Steward had to know what the Quiet was, if he was going to put people in it." He looked round at the curved steel walls in the lamplight, the rust, the rivets. "He was right, I suppose. I've never put anyone in here without remembering it."

"Seren?"

Crane didn't flinch. He'd been expecting it.

"Eleven rounds," he said. "I came down every round. The way I've come down now. She wouldn't speak to me. On the eleventh round she said one thing. She said, *You know I'm right.* And I said yes." He looked at the lamp. "And then I signed the order, and the next round they took her up to Well Fourteen, and she went down it. She didn't look back. I was there. I made myself be there."

The Bell came. They both sat through it without moving.

"How many?" said Cas when it had gone. "Out there. Since."

"In the city? Nobody else has died." Crane's voice was very level. "There's been fighting. There was fighting in the Commons and there's been fighting in the lanes since. The Wardens have been — busy. There are people who want your head. There are more people who want mine. The riggers have taken the Drop District and won't let a Warden in it. The Tollers' banner is still hanging in the Commons. I haven't had it taken down." He paused. "Half the city is saying the Relief is a demon sent up from the Drop to lure the Watch to its death. And the other half is making lamps."

"Lamps?"

"Lamps. Oil lamps. Wicks, dishes. Every oil press in Holdfast has been running for two rounds. People are pressing algae in their kitchens." Crane looked at him. "They're getting ready for the dark, Relief. You told them the Lamp would go out. Some of them believed you."

Cas found that he couldn't speak.

"I didn't come down here to tell you that," said Crane. "I came down to ask you why."

"Why what?"

"Why you did it." The old man's face in the lamplight was very tired, and very intelligent, and entirely serious. "I asked you to be kind. You were kind, the first time. You gave them sixteen rounds of peace. Sixteen rounds of believing that someone had come for them. And then you stood up in the Commons and you took it away. And forty-seven of them are dead in the Drop. And I would very much like to understand what you thought you were doing."

* * *

It went on for a long time.

He'd think about it afterwards — how long, how quiet, the two of them in the bottom of a steel tank with a single flame between them and the Bell coming and going through the floor. He'd think that he had never in his life had a conversation like it. He'd think that it was the kind of conversation he should have had fifteen years ago, with someone, anyone, and never had.

"Truth," said Halloran Crane, "is a luxury."

He said it without contempt. The way you'd say that a coat was warm.

"It's a thing for people with futures. You can afford to tell people the truth when they have time to do something with it. When they can change their minds, and their lives, and fix what's broken. My ancestor stood under the Placard on the night of the Kindling in front of nineteen hundred people who had just watched their world end. Their families were gone. Their homes were gone. The sky was on fire. The ones who tried to leave had come back up the wells in — in pieces. What was the truth going to do for them? *We did this. It was my fault. We will all die here, and nobody is coming, and there is nothing beyond the Drop but a world that has forgotten us.* That was the truth, as far as he knew it. Do you know what nineteen hundred people do with that truth, in the dark, in the first week?"

"Tell me."

"They kill each other," said Crane. "It's in the Log. Ruth Tally's own Log. The Tollers think I haven't read it. I've read it ten times. There were riots in the first week. There was a man who took an axe to the scrubbers because he wanted it all to be over. Eleven people died. And my ancestor stood up under that sign and read it aloud and told them it was a promise. *You will not be forgotten.* And they stopped." He opened his hands. "His lie kept nineteen hundred terrified people alive for a hundred and sixty-eight years. It gave us children, and the Tanks, and the Crèche, and the Commons, and every rope and wall and lamp in this city. It gave us thirty-one thousand people. That is what a lie can do." He looked at Cas. "And your truth, Relief, in a single round? Forty-seven."

Cas didn't answer.

"It killed Seren," he said at last.

"Yes."

"And my — the people I work for, outside. They've been telling the same kind of lie for ninety years. *It's stable. It's safe. The future will pay.* I signed one of them. I signed my name under it. And that lie didn't keep anyone alive, it just — it just kept everything going. It kept the Draw going. It kept them feeding it. Right up until it broke and took nineteen hundred people with it and put them in here. That's what your ancestor was. He was one of us. He wasn't lying to keep your founders alive. He was lying because it's what we *do*. It's the only thing we know how to do."

Crane was quiet.

"Your lie kept them alive," said Cas. "Fine. It kept them alive so they could die calm. That's all it was ever going to do. It kept them in here. It kept anyone from ever trying — ever *once* trying — to go up and see if the thing could be closed. You had the Tollers' numbers. You had Seren's line. You had a girl who came down from the top of the world with the key in her hand. And you kept them calm. You gave them lamps to pray to and a board that counted the Bells and a banner that said the Quickening was glory. And in forty-eight rounds the Lace was going to touch the Rim and every one of them was going to die in the middle of a hymn." He heard his voice shaking. "I'd rather they died knowing. I'd rather they had forty-eight rounds of knowing, and could do something with it, even if all they do is make lamps. I'd rather *I* had. I'd rather I'd known, fifteen years ago, that it was all right to say it. That it wasn't going to kill anyone to say it."

"It did kill people."

"I know."

"Forty-seven."

"I *know*." It came out of him too loudly and rang off the steel walls, and he put his hands over his face. "I know. I know. I know."

The Bell came. They waited for it.

When it had gone, Crane spoke again, lower.

"I signed her order," he said. "Seren's. You know that. What you don't know is that I read her numbers, and I checked them myself, and I knew she was right, and I had the order drawn up before she ever painted the wall. I had it drawn up the round she came to me. Just in case." He looked at the lamp. "Just in case she was the kind of person who'd paint it on the wall. And she was. And I was the kind of person who'd already drawn up the order. That's what I've had to live with. Not that I did it. That I'd got it ready."

Cas lowered his hands.

The old man opened the book in his lap. It was small, bound in cracked black leather, its pages yellow and brittle. He turned them with great care until he found the one he wanted, and then held it out.

"Aldous Crane's journal," he said. "Private. It's passed from Steward to Steward. Nobody else has ever read it. This is from the second year."

Cas took it. He held it close to the lamp. The hand was a fine, sloping, educated hand, a Ministry hand, that had begun beautifully and gone to pieces.

*I did not give it to the Lamp. I will write that here where nobody will see it, once, so that it has been said. I did not give it. I held it. She asked me for it and I held it, because I was afraid of what they would say, and then the walkway went and it was gone. That is the truth. It is the only truth I have.*

*They sing about me now. They have painted me on a wall. Every time I walk past it I think I will go mad.*

*But they are calm. The riots have stopped. The children are being born. And so I will keep it, this lie, I will keep it until I die, and I will make my son keep it after me, because it is the only thing standing between them and the dark. Keep them calm. It is the only mercy I have left to give, and God knows I owe them one.*

Cas read it twice. Then he closed the book and held it in his lap and didn't give it back.

"He knew," he said.

"He knew." Crane took the book from him gently. "Every Steward has known. We've known it for a hundred and sixty-eight years, and every one of us has kept it, because he asked us to, and because it seemed like mercy." He stood up, slowly, his knees cracking. "I've been thinking, since the Commons, that perhaps it wasn't mercy he owed them. That perhaps mercy was just the thing he could afford."

He picked up the lamp. Then, after a moment, he put it down again on the floor.

"Can you close it?" he said. "Really? You and the Tally girl?"

"I don't know."

"You don't know."

"I know nobody's ever tried," said Cas.

Crane stood looking down at him in the flickering light.

"I let you finish," he said at last. "In the Commons. Do you know why?"

"No."

"Because my ancestor didn't let Ruth Tally finish." He turned toward the ladder. "I've wondered my whole life what she'd have said."

He climbed. It took him a long time. At the top he paused, and looked down, and for a moment Cas thought he was going to say something else. But he only looked. Then the lid came down.

He'd left the lamp.

* * *

It burned for a long time. Cas sat and watched it. It was the only light he'd ever been glad of in Holdfast — a little, yellow, living flame, not the Lamp's violet glare, just a wick in a dish of algae oil — and he watched it burn down, and made himself not count.

When it had nearly gone out, the lid opened again.

This time it opened fast. It banged back on its hinge and a big shape filled the round of light at the top and a voice like gravel said, "*Now*, Relief. Up. Quick as you can," and a rope came slithering down into the tank.

He looked up. Osk.

"Stack your bones," said Osk. "Come on."

He got up. He stacked his bones. He took the rope, and Osk hauled, and he came up out of the Quiet the way he'd come up out of the Drop — dragged over the lip like a fish — and lay on the stone floor at the top gasping, and there was a lamp, and faces over him.

Ione. Her left wrist was bound in a splint of wood and cord, and there was a line of black stitches over her right eye where the cut had been, and she looked at him lying on the floor with her unhurried grey-green eyes and said, "You're thinner."

"I've been in a tank."

"Eleven rounds. You'd be thinner." She held out her good hand. "Up."

Pim was there too, very dirty, with no rat, for once, on his shoulder. He was carrying the recorder in both hands like a precious thing. "I got it out of your room," he whispered. "Through the vent. There's a vent. It's very small. I got stuck. And the suit's in the lane, in the cart, under a tarp, it took four of us, and Brother Coll, and one of the riggers is sitting on it."

"Eleven rounds?" said Cas.

"Eleven," said Ione. "The same as my mother." Her face didn't change. "We had to wait till the Wardens changed at Well Four. Osk's the Warden at Well Four tonight."

Cas looked at Osk.

The big man was coiling the rope. He didn't look up.

"My brother," said Osk. "Tam. He went over. At Nine. The round you spoke." He finished the coil. "He heard you say the Outside was alive. He was always like that, Tam. Heard the first thing and ran. All his life." He hung the coil on his shoulder. "He didn't go because you told him the truth. He went because he'd been told a lie so long he couldn't wait another round to see if it was true." He looked at Cas then, finally. "I know which one killed him."

* * *

They went through the shuttered city in the violet dusk with the suit on the cart under a tarpaulin, Osk between the shafts and Pim and two riggers pushing, and Ione walking beside Cas with her good hand under his elbow.

It was different. He could feel it before he could see it. The lanes were as dim and still as they'd been every night — but there were lights in them now. Not the shrine lamps. Other lights. In windows, behind shutters that had been left open a crack. In doorways. On the steps of houses. Little clay lamps, and tin lamps, and lamps made of old instrument housings with a wick stuck in the top, burning, one or two in every lane. People had put them out. He didn't understand why at first. Then he did.

*They're getting ready for the dark.*

On the side of a scaffold tower near the Commons, someone had painted over part of the mural. Not the whole thing. Only the words. THE GIVING was still there, and the tall grieving man holding up his key. But under it, where it had said FOUNDER CRANE GAVE THE KEY TO THE LAMP, someone had painted, in rough white letters, crossing it out:

:: HE DROPPED IT

And under that, in a different hand, in yellow:

:: SEREN WAS RIGHT

They came to Toller's Tower by the back lanes. Perpetua was waiting in the doorway. She was standing — he'd never seen her stand — leaning on Tobiah's arm, very small and very bent, with a lamp in her other hand. She looked at Cas as he came up the step. She looked him up and down in about a second, as she had the first night.

"Eleven rounds," she said. "Hmph. You look worse than I do."

"I feel worse than you do."

"Nobody feels worse than I do. I'm a hundred and eight." She wasn't. She was eighty-three; Ione had told him. She jerked her head at the room behind her. "In. Quick. The Wardens'll be at the Quiet in half a shift."

Inside, at the foot of the column, with the lamps burning and the pendulums swinging, she lowered herself back into her chair with a long sigh and put her hand on the stone.

"Forty-seven," she said. "We wrote them all down. Every name. We'll read them out at the Tanks, when it's time. All forty-seven." She looked at him. "And then we'll read out the other number."

"What other number?"

"Thirty-one thousand, three hundred and sixty," said Perpetua Tally. "The ones who didn't go. The ones who heard you say *not before*. The ones who went home and made lamps." Her black eyes were steady on his. "You didn't do the forty-seven, Relief. A hundred and sixty-eight years did the forty-seven. You did the other number."

He couldn't answer. She didn't seem to expect him to.

"Now," she said. "You'll be wanting your suit."

* * *

They got him into it in the round room at the bottom of the tower. It took four of them. The liner, which hung off him now; he'd lost weight in the Quiet, and before. The clam of the suit closing — thunk, thunk, thunk — and the exo waking, and taking his weight, and for the first time in eleven rounds, for the first time in Holdfast, his body didn't hurt. The keel came into his spine. Teo's keel, clean and high and true. It had dropped. He could hear it had dropped, since the Shear — a semitone, perhaps; less. It was still singing.

And there was a light on in the corner of his visor. Amber. A small steady glow.

MESSAGE — BUFFERED — READY.

"It groaned," said Pim. "In your room. For ages. In the middle of the round when they took you. The Wardens were scared of it. They said it was the Drop talking. Nobody would go in." He looked anxious. "Then it went *ding*."

Cas looked at the light for a moment. Then he played it.

"Hale." Teo's voice, in his ear, very close. He closed his eyes. "Got your second one. The rats and the wedding and the — and her. Seren. Thank you for telling me her name." A pause. "And the last bit. You're going to tell them. All of it. On the — what did you call it — Kindling Round." Another pause. He could hear her breathing. "Christ, Hale. Good. Good. I'm proud of you. You won't want to hear that, so I'm saying it into a machine that's going to take a day to get to you, and you can't stop me."

He heard her shift in the chair.

"Right. Outside news, since you'll want it. The Collar's been on the hardline. Gaunt. I told him you were reseating Six. I've never lied to the Calm in my life before today and I'll tell you what, it's easy. It's the easiest thing I've ever done. That frightens me more than the Lattice does." A breath. "Averna's browning out. They're saying on the line that the fusion plants at Kovač are down to half, and the Canopy's flickering, and they've started rolling blackouts on the upper tiers. There's no announcement. Just the lights going off one tier at a time. People are very calm, Gaunt says. He sounds like he's going to cry.

"And I checked on your girl."

He opened his eyes.

"Civil Defence has the relocation lists. I've still got a login, from in here, on the hardline — don't ask. She's in the school on Tier Thirty with her mother, like you said. She's fine. There's a note on the list next to her name from the welfare officer. It says *asking everyone about stars*." A pause, and something that might have been a laugh. "So she's fine.

"I'm fine. The lee's fine. It moved a bit, about twenty minutes ago — the edge came across the floor, maybe a hand's width. I moved the chair. It's fine. I'm bored out of my skull. I've read the fire regulations twice." She was quiet for a moment. "Go and do it, Cas. Whatever it is. And then bring them out. All of them. Bring them out and I'll be here."

*Ding*.

He stood in the suit in the middle of the Tollers' round room, with the lamps burning and the pendulums swinging and Perpetua watching him from her chair, and he didn't say anything at all.

It was the first time she'd used his name.

* * *

Perpetua said goodbye to him first. She did it the way she did everything: quickly, and without dressing it.

"Go on, then," she said. "Off you go. Up the cursed spindle with my granddaughter. Close the Lamp." She sniffed. "Try not to fall off."

"I'll try."

"I'm keeping the Log." She put her knotted hand on the steel box on the workbench beside her. "Somebody should be here, with it, when you come back with the end of it. Somebody should write the last page." She looked at him with her bright black eyes. "You'll tell me how it ends. Won't you."

"If I can."

"Hmph." She took her hand off the box. "That'll do."

Then she looked at Ione.

Ione was standing by the door with her rope over her shoulder and her wrist in its splint and the key at her throat. She'd been waiting. She didn't come forward. And Perpetua didn't say anything to her. She only looked. It went on and on — the old woman in the chair and the young one by the door, the lamps between them, the pendulums swinging, the Bell coming up through the column and fading — and Cas understood that he was watching eleven years go by. Eleven years of a girl who came down from the top of the world with grey in her hair and a key in her hand, and a grandmother who'd lost her daughter to the Drop and then watched the granddaughter go up after something nobody else believed was there. Eleven years of that girl being called mad in the lanes. Eleven years of her sitting on this step in the dusk, eating greenbread.

"Go on," said Perpetua Tally, very softly. "Go and finish it, Ione."

Ione nodded once. She turned and went out.

* * *

Spindle Four stood at the far edge of the city, in the Drop District, alone.

The riggers had let them through. They were on every corner, in every lane of the District — the riggers' tower had become a fortress, lamps on every platform, ropes across every approach — and they stood aside for the cart and watched it go by in silence. Some of them touched the tarpaulin as it passed. One of them, a woman with a scarred face, said, "Haul, Ione," under her breath, and Ione said, "Haul, Bett."

The spindle's foot was a ruin. The lift terminal at its base had collapsed in the Founders' time, when the lift fell — the whole cage and a kilometre of rack-rail coming down together, Pim said, with a sound they'd heard in the Commons — and nobody had ever cleared it. The wreckage lay in a great twisted heap of rusted girders and snapped rail and fallen keelstone, overgrown with pale vine. And behind it, rising out of it, the spindle went up.

He'd seen it every day from the loft. He'd seen all of them every day. He'd never stood at the foot of one before.

It was a hundred metres across at its base. It went straight up out of the wreckage into the light, a vast black lattice of struts and braces and cross-members, open, skeletal, the violet glare of the Lamp coming through it in a million bright diamonds, and it went up. And up. And up. It didn't narrow, at first; then, very gradually, it did. It went up until the struts blurred into a texture and the texture into a line and the line into nothing, into the glare, into the fine bright falling tendrils of the Lace, four kilometres over his head.

Osk and Pim and the riggers got him out of the cart. He stood in the suit at the foot of the wreck, looking up. Ione stood beside him, with her head tipped back.

"Well," said Pim, in a very small voice. "It's big."

Ione took her rope off her shoulder and began to uncoil it, favouring the splinted wrist, and started tying it into the harness they'd spliced for the suit, her fingers moving fast.

"Don't look up," she said, without looking at him. "It only gets taller."
