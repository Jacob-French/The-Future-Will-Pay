---
title: The Witness
---

> I'll hold your rope. You hold mine.
> — Holdfast riggers' wedding vow

"It's me," said Ione.

She said it quite quietly, standing by the hollow in the dust, with her hands at her sides. As if she were telling him the time.

"No," said Cas.

"Listen to me." She didn't raise her voice. "I've thought about this. I've had eleven years to think about it, and I've had since the Rim, and I've had the — the hours. On the gantry. Waiting for her. Listen."

"Ione—"

"I've already lived my forever." She said it evenly. "Three years in that gallery. Three years of one night. I know what it is. I know what it's like to be in one moment and not get out. I'm not frightened of it. I'm the only person in the world who isn't. And you told me — you said it yourself, just now — it's once. For whoever's in it, it's only once." She looked at him. "I can do once."

"So can I."

"You've got a daughter." Her voice caught on it, and went on. "You've got a little girl called Nell who collects stars, and a woman in a chair at the bottom of the Anvil who's waiting to buy you a drink, and a whole city of — six hundred million people, you said, and a sky, and rain, and a sea. You've got an *ordinary* waiting for you. You haven't lived it yet. You told me in the hut you've had fifteen years of the same day. You haven't lived your ordinary." She shook her head. "I've got Gran. And Gran's eighty-three, and she's dying, and she's never once said so. That's all I've got. That's all I've had, since I came down. I've been a ghost in Holdfast for eleven years. I sat on the Tollers' step and watched people step round me. I've already lived my forever, Cas. You haven't lived your ordinary. It's me."

He looked at her for a long time.

"I saw it come down," he said.

She went still.

"On the Rim. In the Lace. The suit." He looked down at himself — at the grey plates, the gauntlets. "My suit. With your harness on it. Coming down Four. Alone. I saw it, and I thought: *that's me*. That's me coming back without her. I thought I was going to go home and you were going to die up here." He heard his voice shake and didn't stop it. "And I decided — right there, on the Rim, with you standing on my boots — that I wasn't going to let it. I thought, *whatever it asks for, I'll be the one who stays*. I thought if I stayed, the vision would be wrong. It'd be you coming down. Not me. I'd have *beaten* it. For once in my life I'd have looked at a projection and made it false."

Ione was staring at him.

"I saw it too," she said.

"I know. You told me. At seventeen—"

"No. I mean—" She stopped. Her face had gone very strange. "I've been waiting eleven years at the top of the wells for a man in a suit to come up. And I always knew — I've always known, since I was seventeen — that he comes back down. Alone. I saw it. So I knew. From the first minute. From when I pulled you out." She put her hand to her mouth. "I've known the whole time that you go home and I don't. And on the Rim, when we saw it again, I thought — I decided — I'd make it wrong. I'd stay. So you'd go home to your girl. So it'd be — *not* you, coming down alone, leaving me. So it'd be you coming down alone because I *chose*—"

She stopped.

They looked at each other across the hollow in the floor.

"We both—" he said.

"We both saw you." She was breathing very fast. "We both saw you come down, and we both decided to — we both—"

She stopped again.

And then something happened to her face. He watched it happen. He watched it the way he'd watched Ben's hand appear on the glass: all at once, whole, from nowhere. Her eyes went wide. Her hand came away from her mouth. She looked at him — at the suit — at the grey plates and the housing and the harness — and she took a step back from the hollow, and another, and her face crumpled.

"Cas," she said. Her voice cracked right down the middle. "Cas, it was the suit. It was never — it was the *suit*—"

He heard it. He heard it twice — once now, in the Crown, from her mouth, and once on the Rim, in the Lace, from nowhere, an hour ago, a lifetime ago, *it was the suit*, in exactly that voice, exactly that crack in it — and the two of them rang together in his head like two strokes of the same bell, and he understood.

They'd never seen *him*. Either of them. Not at seventeen, not on the Rim. They'd seen a suit. Grey plates and a humming keel and a harness spliced in rigger's strapping, climbing down Spindle Four alone. Whoever was in it. They'd both looked at the suit and seen him inside it, because it was his, because who else would be in it — and it had never been a vision of who lived. It had only ever been a vision of who wore the suit.

He looked down at himself.

Then he looked at her. Short, broad, strong. Her rope on her shoulder. Her splinted wrist. The white streak.

"It's you," he said. "Ione. It's you coming down. It was always you."

"*No*."

"It was you in it. On the Rim, raising its hand. At seventeen. It was always—"

"No." She'd backed up against a console. Dust rose round her in a slow cloud. "No. No. Then it's not — then it isn't a *choice*. Then it was always going to be — I don't get to — you don't get to—" She was shaking her head. "Then it's just the key again. It's just the arrows. It's just something that's true that nobody chose, and we're only—"

"It's already true," said Cas. "And I'm still choosing it."

She stopped.

* * *

He didn't know he was going to say the next thing until he said it.

"Ruth saw me."

Ione stared at him.

"In the Log. Year Thirty-One. I read it in the Tower, the first night. I didn't understand it. I didn't — I read it twice and turned the page." He could see it: the pencil, the short entry set apart with a line above and below. "She saw a man at the Keystone. At 02:09. After the key fell. Standing where she was kneeling. Dressed close, in grey, like a diver." He looked down at his gauntlets. At the suit. Under it, he knew exactly what he was wearing: the grey liner. The one-piece. The thing that made all three of them look like depressed seals. "His hand on a wheel that wasn't there. She said he looked at her like he knew her. She said he was very calm." He looked up. "She said there was a cord knotted round his wrist."

Ione didn't move.

"She's been looking at me," he said. "In the gallery. Every cycle. For three years, you watched her look up at the Keystone, at nothing, like someone was there. You stood in the place. You said it wasn't you." He swallowed. "It was me. She was looking at me. At — this. Now. Here."

Ione's hand had gone to her pocket.

He watched it. She didn't seem to know she was doing it. Her good hand went into the pocket of her tunic and closed round something there, and came out, and opened, and lay in her palm. A length of thin cord. The cord from the wedding. The one she'd tied round his wrist on the rooftop in the Drop District with the drums going and the tower swaying, and taken off again with one tug. *Riggers' hitch. Holds under load. Won't slip. That's the only one you'll need.*

She looked at it in her hand.

"It's not on your wrist," she said. Very quietly. "It's not there yet."

"No."

"She saw it there. So it's there. So someone ties it." She looked up at him. "So *I* tie it. That's the only way it gets there. I have to do it. I have to choose to do it. Like the arrows. Like the key." Her voice broke again. "You're asking me to *tie it on*."

"I'm not asking you anything," said Cas. "It's yours. It's your rope."

She closed her hand round it.

* * *

"Why?" she said.

It was a long time later. Or not long. They were sitting on the floor of the Crown, in the dust, by the hollow, side by side, their backs against the dead console. The keel hummed. The ghost of Ruth knelt by the Keystone, looking up. The thread of the Lace lay across the room, moving slowly. Outside the great window, the Lamp burned.

"Tell me why," said Ione. "Not the vision. Not the Log. Not what's true. *Why*. You came in here to die. You told me. In the hut. You came in because it was tidy. Because you thought you owed it." Her voice was quite steady now, very low. "If that's why — if you're doing this because you think you still owe it — then I'll fight you. I'll put you on the floor and take that suit off you myself and you can go home and I'll stay, and it doesn't matter what's true. I will. I'll make it false or I'll die trying. I'm not letting you do it for that."

He thought about it. He thought about it the way she thought about questions — turning it over, looking at it from underneath — because she'd earned that, and because he needed to know.

He thought about the window in the Collar. *At last. Almost a relief.* He thought about the Quiet, the dark, the small cold place with the simple shape: *everything I do adds to it*. He thought about the Rim, and the ledger, and the zero at the bottom of the page.

He examined himself for it. He patted his pockets.

It wasn't there.

"I'm not paying anything," he said.

She turned her head.

"I came in to pay," he said. "You're right. I came in here to pay it off. All at once. With me. And you told me — in the hut — that you can't. That it doesn't get smaller. That it just grows." He looked at the hollow. The brass wheel. "You were right. I'm not paying anything. There's nothing to pay. There never was. You showed me the bottom of the page and it was zero, and you said zero's balanced. It's balanced. I don't owe anything. I'm square."

"Then *why*—"

"Because I want to."

It was the truest thing he'd ever said. He heard it come out of him and knew it the way he'd known the fractal number on the telemetry screen. *I want to.*

"I want this," he said. "Not the dying. It's not dying. You told me. It's once." He looked at her. "Ione. For fifteen years I lived one day. The same day. The day with the pen. I stood in the gallery and watched myself sign it, over and over, thinking *don't*, the way you watched Ruth. And I never once — I never had a moment I'd want. Not one. Not one moment in fifteen years that I'd have wanted to keep. And now I've got one." He could hardly get it out. "I've got one. I want to be in it. I want to be in the moment where I know you're safe. Where I know it worked. Where Nell's going to see the stars and Teo's going to get out of her chair and thirty-one thousand people are going to walk down a ladder. Where you're going down Spindle Four in my suit, alive." He shook his head. "That's not paying. That's the opposite of paying. It's — it's the only thing I've ever wanted that I've been sure of."

She was crying without any sound.

"I want the moment," said Casimir Hale. "I'm choosing it. Not because it's true. Because I want it. It's just — it's true as well."

* * *

They chose the instant together.

He showed her the dial. The ring round the rim of the hollow, engraved with Hollowell's small precise figures. The interval. He worked it out with her, aloud, the way he'd worked out the curve in the Tollers' tower, the way she'd worked out the Lees: how long for her to get back down the stair, and along the Rim through the Lace, and through the Carousel's seam — Ben's seam, still there, she said; she'd seen it on the way back from the gantry, still shimmering — and across the gantry, and down the spindle, and down through the Breach. How long before she was past the Breach, well below the Rim, in the Lees, out of the Fold. How long it would be before she was safe.

"Three hours," said Ione. "If I'm quick. If the weather's kind."

"Three and a half," he said. "In case it isn't."

He set the ring. It turned under his fingers with a smooth, heavy click at each mark, like the dial of a safe.

"From the Crown," he said, "I'll be able to see Four. Down there. Through the window. If I look." He pointed through the great glass, down and to the left, past the glare of the Lamp, to where the black lattice of Spindle Four rose out of the violet gulf wrapped in its cobwebs of light. "I'll have to watch the needle. But I'll be able to see. And the keel's got its lights. I'll see you going down."

"You'll see me," said Ione.

"All the way."

"And at the end—"

"At the end, you'll be past the Breach. And I'll look. And I'll see your light." He was quiet. "That's the instant. That's where I'll be."

She was silent for a long time.

Then she said: "I'll learn one for you."

"What?"

"A star. When I get out. When I see them." She looked at the window, at the Lamp, at the only star she'd ever had. "You said you never looked. You never had a favourite. You said Nell would have known in a second." She wiped her face with the back of her good hand. "I'll find one for you. I'll ask Nell. I'll go out under the — the sky, the real one, and I'll pick one, and it'll be yours. And I'll look at it."

"Every night?"

"They come every night?" she said. "You said they come every night."

"Every night."

"Then every night," said Ione.

* * *

They didn't talk about it after that. They talked about other things.

He told her about rain again, because she asked. She asked what it tasted of. He said nothing, mostly, and sometimes dust, and in the Stacks, sometimes, of the metal of the gutters. She asked if you could drink it. He said yes. She said she'd drink it. She asked about the sea — whether it was loud — and he said yes, very, and that it never stopped, and she said that she'd lived with the Bell all her life so she'd be used to that. She asked what they ate, Outside. He told her. She was scandalised that nobody ate rat. He said she could start a fashion. She said she'd open a stall. *Holdfast's Other Citizens, Roasted.* He laughed. It came out of him in the dust of the Crown, a real laugh, loud, and she looked at him with that expression he'd seen on the spindle — as if he'd surprised her — and then she laughed too, the rough short stiff laugh, and couldn't stop, and put her forehead down on his shoulder.

And then, at some point, neither of them was laughing. And she lifted her head. And he took off the helmet — he'd had it on since the Rim — and put it down in the dust. And she put her good hand on the side of his face, and looked at him, the way she'd looked at him at the top of the well, patiently, attentively, as if he were a view she'd seen many times and still liked.

"I'm glad it was you," she said. "That came up the well."

"I'm glad it was you that pulled me out."

She kissed him. It was very gentle. It went on for a while.

* * *

Getting her into the suit was the hardest thing he had ever done.

Not because of what it meant. Because of the straps. The suit was built for him, and she was a head shorter and twice as broad through the shoulders, and it opened down the back like a clam and she stepped into it and it closed round her — thunk, thunk, thunk — and it didn't fit anywhere. The exo woke and took her weight and didn't know what to do with her, and she stood in it swaying like a child in her father's coat, her face tiny in the big curve of the visor, and said, *well, this is ridiculous*.

So he fitted it to her. The way Ben had fitted it to him in the hangar, a hundred years ago. He knelt in the dust in the grey liner and went round the suit, strap by strap, buckle by buckle, taking in the harness at the shoulders and letting it out at the hips, shortening the legs of the exo by two notches, cinching the webbing she'd spliced for him so that it would hold her instead. His hands were shaking. He made them stop, and they shook anyway, and he went on.

"Ione," he said, at the back of the suit, feeling along the strapping under the keel housing, "I can't see the—"

"—buckle," she said. "It's lower. Left. There."

His hands stopped.

He knelt there in the dust with his hands on the strap, and the words he'd said went through him, and the words he'd heard on the Rim went through him, and they rang together like the bell again — *Ione, I can't see the —* in his own voice, out of the air, out of nowhere, in the Lace — and he found that he was laughing. Kneeling behind her in the dust, laughing, with his forehead against the keel housing.

"What?" she said. "What is it?"

"Nothing." He found the buckle. It was lower. Left. There. He did it up. "Nothing. It's just — that one was only a buckle."

She didn't understand. Then she did. He heard her breath go in, and out, and then she was laughing too, in the suit, the sound thin and strange through the helmet speaker, and it went on until it wasn't laughing any more, and they both stopped, and he finished the straps.

When it was done she took three steps across the Crown in it, and turned, and took three back. She was steady. The exo had learned her. The keel hummed on her back. Her face in the visor was very small and very pale and very calm.

"It's heavy," she said.

"You'll get used to it."

"I've got about three and a half hours."

"You'll be fine."

"I know." She looked at him through the visor. At him standing in the dust in the grey liner — the close grey one-piece, slippery, cool, like a diver's. Like something worn under armour. She looked at it for a long time.

Then she opened the suit's chest compartment and took out the cord.

* * *

She tied it the way she'd tied it on the rooftop. Quickly. Over, under, round, through. Her gauntlet had to come off for it — she worked the clasp with her splinted hand and pulled it off and let it drop in the dust — and with her bare fingers she tied the riggers' hitch round his left wrist, above the cuff of the liner, flat and neat and interlocking, like two hands clasped.

Her fingers stopped when it was done. They stayed on the knot.

"I'll hold your rope," said Ione Tally. Her voice was quite steady. "You hold mine."

He knew the rest. He'd heard it on a rooftop with the drums going and the whole tower stamping. He said it with her.

"And if the one of us falls," they said, "the other one hauls."

Nobody shouted *haul*. There was nobody to shout it. There was only the Crown, and the dust, and the ghost of Ruth Tally on her knees by the Keystone, looking up at the place where he was standing.

Ione looked at the knot on his wrist. Then she looked at the ghost.

"She's looking at you," she said. "Look. She's looking right at you."

He looked. The faint white shape in the dust, the woman in the lab coat with her hair pinned up and the mole under her ear, kneeling by the open housing, her face turned up toward the place beside it. Toward him.

"A hundred and seventy-five thousand times," said Ione, "I watched her look at you. I stood where you're standing. Hundreds of times. To see if it was me she saw." Her voice broke, finally, completely. "It was you. It was always you. She was looking at you the whole time. She was looking at *you*."

* * *

They armed it together.

He knelt by the hollow in the grey liner with the knot on his wrist. She knelt beside him in the suit. The red lever stood between them with its black-and-yellow collar, exactly like the one by the lift that Ruth had pulled. He put his hand on it. She put her bare hand over his.

"So this is the start," she said.

"This is the start."

They pulled it.

It moved easily. It moved like a thing that had been waiting to be moved. It came down through its collar and seated with a sound — not a click; a *toll*, deep, a single note going down through the keelstone into Spindle One and on down, and on, into the Vault, into the Anvil, into everything — and he felt it go through him, the way he'd felt the hammer go through Tier Nine. The Lattice letting go. The last hold released.

Outside the great window, the Lamp changed.

It didn't grow. It didn't dim. But its light — its hard white-violet — went, very faintly, warmer. A thread of gold in it. And round it, the Lace stirred. All of it, at once, the whole vast filigree across the sky, rippling, the way a curtain ripples when a door opens somewhere in the house. The thread lying across the Crown moved, and lifted, and drifted a little toward the window, as if something out there were drawing a breath.

On the gauge, the needle trembled. And began to move.

He put his hand on the brass wheel.

It was warm. It turned under his fingers smooth and heavy and fine, a little click at each tooth, and the needle, which had begun to drift off the line toward the band of colour on its left, stopped, and hung, and came back. He held it there. He felt it wander, under his hand, in the wheel — a tug this way, a tug that way, like a fish on a line — and he held it on the line.

"Go," he said. He didn't look at her. He couldn't take his eyes off the needle. "Ione. Go."

She didn't move for a moment.

Then she leaned in — the suit's helmet close to his bare face — and pressed her visor against his temple. Hard. Just for a second. He felt the cold of the glass. He heard her breathe.

"Haul," she said.

Then she stood up, and turned, and he heard the suit's boots go across the Crown — heavy, steady, booming on the keelstone floor, the keel's hum going with them — and down the hatch. And the hum faded. And was gone.

He held the needle on the line.

* * *

She went down the stair onto the Rim, and the Lace was waiting for her.

It was moving. It had been moving slowly all the time they'd been in it; now it moved like weed in a flood. The threads swayed and swung and reached, rippling with that long slow breath that had come into them when the lever went down. The light had a thread of gold in it. The air was full of a sound she had never heard in the Lace before — a high, faint, singing, like a finger round the rim of a glass.

And there were no arrows.

She stood at the foot of the stair and looked at the steel plate of the Rim running back through the forest of light toward the gallery. Bare. Black. No chalk. No white rigger's arrows, three quick strokes, pointing the way between the threads.

Of course there weren't. She hadn't drawn them yet.

She had chalk. She always had chalk. She opened the pouch on the suit's belt — he'd put it there, when he fitted the suit, without saying anything; she'd seen him do it — and took out a stick, and knelt on the steel at the foot of the stair, and drew an arrow. Three strokes. Pointing ahead, and a little left, between two great hanging roots of light.

It looked exactly like the first one they'd seen.

She stood up. She walked to where it pointed. She drew another. She knew where it went. She knew exactly where it went, because she'd followed it, an hour ago, standing on his boots with his arms round her. She'd followed it because it was there. It was there because she was drawing it. It had never been anyone's. It had never come from anywhere. It was just true.

She drew them all the way. Every one. Kneeling in the suit on the flickering steel of the Rim — it flickered; she didn't look at it — with the threads swaying round her and the singing in the air and the keel humming round her in its metre of calm. She did not look into the threads. She did not look at the Lamp. She looked at the chalk in her bare fingers, and the steel, and the arrows.

In the middle of the Rim, where the threads were thickest, she heard her own voice.

"*Right. Right, and then straight.*"

It came out of the air beside her. Her voice, from an hour ago. From before. She stopped with the chalk in her hand, kneeling, and she knew exactly where it was coming from. She knew that somewhere very close to her, in the same place and not in the same place, a man in a keel suit was standing with a woman on his boots, and the woman was looking for the next arrow, and the man had just heard her voice come out of nowhere and gone cold.

She looked at the place where the next arrow had to go. Right. Right, and then straight.

"Right," said Ione, to the air, to the man, to the girl on his boots. Her voice was shaking. "Right, and then straight. It's all right. You'll find them. They're all here. I've drawn them for you."

She drew it.

* * *

She went through the Carousel without stopping.

She came in by the Rim door, into the corridor, and the ghost-light came on round her — *Is it going to be too bright?* — and she walked through it. She didn't look at the windows. She didn't look at the scratched fives on the walls, or the pallet of rags, or the pile of smooth stones. She walked straight along the curve to the place in the middle of the corridor where Ben's seam was, the hand's-width shimmer between the two branches, and she stopped in front of it.

And then she did stop. Just for a moment. She turned round.

The ghost-room was round her. Ruth was at the board, with her back to her, her hair pinned up. It was the second minute. *The protocol requires the interlocks off.* Crane was talking. Ruth's thumb had gone to her mouth.

Ione stood in the corridor in the suit and looked at her.

"I know who you're looking at," she said. "Later. At the Keystone. I know who it is now." She swallowed. "He's all right. He's — he's not frightened. You were right. He's very calm."

Ruth bit the side of her thumb, and didn't hear her, the way she'd never heard her.

"Goodbye, Ruth," said Ione Tally.

She turned to face the outer wall — the gantry side, the way home — and stepped through the seam.

* * *

The gantry swayed under her. The keel hummed. The Lace, above and behind her, was singing louder now, and the light was more gold than violet, and the gulf beneath the gantry was full of a strange shifting glow, as if the whole Vault were lit from a new angle. She ran. She couldn't run in the suit; she walked as fast as it would let her, three hundred metres of rusted steel lattice, past the loose bolt — she stepped on the plate beside it, and it rattled, and she nearly stopped, and didn't — to the door in the lattice of Spindle Four.

Down. The stairs. The ladders. The hut in the last Lee, its iron door standing open, the planks inside where they'd lain under his coat and hers. She didn't stop.

The Breach.

It was worse than it had been. She'd known it would be. The payment was gathering at the core; the Lamp was pushing and swinging and pulling all at once; the lamp-weather in the Breach was a gale. She went down the single spine of keelstone with its ladder rungs, and down swung round her, and swung, and swung, and the keel's bubble held. It held. She could feel it straining. She could feel it averaging the whole screaming world out over its metre of calm, and she understood now what he had felt on the way up with her wrapped round him, and she went down, rung after rung, not looking out, not looking down, the rungs coming up past her visor, black iron, pitted.

At the bottom of the Breach the lattice closed round her again. The great struts. A Lee. The weather dropped away.

She stopped on the walkway, and put her bare hand on a strut, and stood there, breathing.

She was past it. She was out of the Fold. She'd done it.

She didn't know what time it was. The suit's clocks had died on the Rim; she'd watched the numbers fall apart. She didn't know how long she had, or whether she'd made it, or whether up there in the Crown the needle was still on the line and the wheel was still turning under his hand.

She turned round. She tipped her head back. She looked up.

The spindle rose above her, black lattice, narrowing, into the glare. The Breach. The hut. The gallery, a ring round the neighbouring pillar, its windows full of moving light. The gantry, a thread across the gulf. And above it all, the Rim, a dark circle round the Lamp, hung with the Lace. The Lace was singing. The Lamp was gold.

And on the Rim, in the Lace — small, very far, at the edge of a clearing in the forest of light — there were two figures.

She could see them. She could see them quite clearly. A grey figure in a keel suit, standing by the Rim's inner rail. And another, smaller, standing in front of it, pressed back against it, the grey arms round her. Not moving. Two people, holding each other up, at the top of the world.

Looking down at her.

She stood on the walkway in the Lee with her bare hand on the strut and looked up at them for a long time. At herself, standing on his boots. At him, holding her. At the two of them, on the Rim, an hour ago, a lifetime ago, looking down at a small grey figure in a keel suit on Spindle Four, and thinking *it's me. That's me, coming back without her.*

She raised her hand.

She held it there. The palm out. She didn't know what else to do. She held it up, toward them, toward him, toward the girl on his boots who didn't know yet, who would find out in the Crown with her voice cracking down the middle — *it was the suit* — and she held it there, and held it, until her arm shook.

Then she lowered it.

She turned, and went on down.

She'd gone perhaps ten steps down the next flight of stairs when the light changed.

It didn't fade. It didn't flicker. There was no sound. The Lamp — which had been gold, and singing, and very bright — was simply, between one step and the next, not there. And the Lace was not there. And the light was not there. And there was nothing above her and nothing round her and nothing below her but the dark.

Ione Tally stood on the stair in the black, in his suit, with the keel humming round her in its little metre of calm, and the only light in the whole world was the soft band of it round the housing on her back, glowing faintly on the rungs.

Above her, very high up, in the dark, something that had been about to happen was never going to happen.

And somewhere in it, once, a man was looking down at her light.
