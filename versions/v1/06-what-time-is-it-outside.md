---
title: What Time Is It Outside
part: Part Two · Holdfast
---

> Remain at your station. Await relief. You will not be forgotten.
> — The Placard, Section 9 (the Watch's first scripture)

They got him over the lip of the well on his belly, like a seal hauled onto a rock, and then he was lying on his back on something hard and hot, and the light was so bright that his visor went black to protect him and he was blind.

He lay there. He had no choice about it. The weight was on him like a second body lying on top of his own — heavier, much heavier, than it had been in the conduit — and every breath was a push against it. The exo was whining. His arms were two dead things. The keel sang in his spine, high and clean, and under it, coming up through whatever he was lying on, was the long slow swell of the hammer.

Someone was touching his suit.

Small, strong, quick hands. They went round the neck-ring of his helmet, pressing, feeling. They found the seals at his shoulders, and the joints at his elbows, and the coupling of the keel. They were checking him. They were checking him the way he'd have checked a suit himself: methodically, in order, the places where air could get out.

"Don't take the helmet off yet," said the voice. The same voice from the well. "Breathe slow. The air's thick here. You'll want to open it slowly when you open it. Let your ears get used to it."

His visor was clearing. It was adjusting to the glare, dimming it by stages, and a shape came out of the white. A face. A woman's face, bent over him, very close.

"Can you hear me?"

"Yes," he said. His voice came out of the suit's speaker cracked and strange.

"Good." She sat back on her heels. "What time is it outside?"

For a moment he didn't understand the question. It was so simple. It was the question you'd ask a stranger at a bus stop. He looked at the display at the bottom of his visor, where the two clocks were — OUTSIDE, fed by the last good signal from the pucks; LOCAL, his own — and read it off without thinking.

"Thirty-three hours," he said. "And a bit."

She closed her eyes.

She was quite still for perhaps five seconds. He could see her lips moving very slightly, the way his did when he was doing sums. Then she opened her eyes again, and there was something in them he couldn't read — not surprise, not fear. Something more like the look of a person who has been carrying a heavy thing for a very long time and has just been told how much further they have to carry it.

"Then it's sooner than they think," she said.

"Is he the Relief?"

That was another voice, higher, breathless. A boy. Cas turned his head inside the helmet and saw him — a thin, pale, very dirty boy of about eleven, crouched on the other side of him with his hands on Cas's arm and his eyes as big as coins. He had a rat on his shoulder. It was a fat, sleek, brown-and-white rat with a red cord tied round its neck like a collar, and it was washing its face.

"Is he? Ione. Is he? Is he the—"

"He's a man in a suit," said the woman. "Get his legs."

* * *

They couldn't lift him. Nobody could have lifted him. But there was a cart — a flat wooden thing on four small iron wheels, with a long handle, the kind of cart you'd use to haul rope — and between them, with a lot of heaving and a lot of the boy swearing in an accent so thick Cas caught one word in four, they rolled him onto it. He lay on it on his back. The weight pressed him down into the planks.

Then the woman stood up, and took the cart's handle, and turned it so that his head was at the high end, and for the first time he could see.

He was lying at the top of a well, on a kind of stone lip, a round platform of black keelstone with a waist-high wall around it made of scrap: sheet metal, bits of pipe, a stripped-out door, all bolted and wired and welded together. Beside him, in the middle of the platform, was the mouth of the conduit he'd come up — a black round hole six metres across with the top of the ladder hooked over its edge. Around the hole, someone had painted on the black stone, in white, in big careful letters that went all the way round like writing on a coin: WATCH · REMAIN · AWAIT · WATCH · REMAIN · AWAIT.

And beyond the wall was Holdfast.

He did not know it was called that yet. He didn't know anything. He lay on the rope-cart with the weight on his chest and the sweat running into his eyes inside the helmet and he looked, and for a long time — he never knew how long — he forgot to breathe.

It was a city.

It went away from him across a plain of black stone as flat as a table, and it was built of everything. Of scaffolding: great towers of scaffolding pipe, bolted and lashed and welded, ten, twenty storeys high, with floors laid across them made of steel plate and planking and doors. Of containers: the big steel cargo boxes the Draw used to move parts, stacked in terraces, painted every colour, with windows cut in their sides and shutters hung over the windows. Of keelstone: slabs of it, black and shining, off-cuts and rejects from the Lattice's manufacture, laid as walls and floors and roofs, so that parts of the city looked as if they'd been carved from a single piece of night. It was stacked and crammed and piled together, lane on lane, tower on tower, bridges and walkways and ladders running between everything, lines of washing strung across every gap. There were gardens. Green things grew everywhere, in tanks and pots and troughs and on the roofs, climbing up the scaffolding in thick ropes of vine. There was smoke — thin grey threads of it rising straight up from a hundred chimneys. There was noise: the murmur he'd heard coming down the shaft, and it was voices after all. Thousands of voices. A city's worth of people talking, calling, laughing, hammering, a baby crying somewhere, somebody singing.

And above it, rising out of it, the spindles.

There were eight. He'd seen them in the old footage, in the mural in the crisis room, in schematics: the great maintenance pylons that ran from the floor of the Vault to the Rim four kilometres up. He had never understood, from a picture, what four kilometres meant. They went up out of the city like trees out of undergrowth — black latticework towers, each one a hundred metres across at the base, the city piled up around their feet like drifts of leaves — and they went up, and up, and up, narrowing, until they were lost in the glare.

And the glare—

He looked up.

The Lesion hung at the top of the sky.

It was a point. A single point of white-violet light, too hard and too bright to look at, like looking at the sun through a pinhole. The eight spindles rose toward it on every side and ended short of it in a thin dark ring, far up, that must be the Rim. Beyond the Rim the Vault curved up and away on all sides — black, a vast black dome, a sky made of the inside of a stone — and it should have been dark, and it wasn't. Because spreading out from the point of light, across the whole top of the sky, was the Lace.

He had seen its threads in the Works. A hair of light crossing a storeroom. A root system spreading down a wall. This was the thing they had grown from. It spread out from the Lesion in every direction at once, an unending, branching, glowing filigree, finer than frost, larger than weather, filling the upper sky from horizon to horizon like the veins in a leaf held up to the sun. It moved. Very slowly, almost too slowly to see, it was moving — branching, reaching, growing — and in places its longest threads came down out of the sky in fine bright streamers toward the tops of the spindles, like the tendrils of some enormous luminous jellyfish trailing in the current. It was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen. It was the most frightening. He couldn't decide which; his mind kept sliding between the two and couldn't find anywhere to stand.

The light it gave was violet and white and very even, like the light of an overcast noon, and there were no shadows anywhere, and it was warm. It was as warm as a summer afternoon in the southern tiers. Sweat was running down his back inside the liner.

The hammer came.

It came up through the stone under him, through the cart, through the planks, into his spine. But it was not the hammer. Not the blow, once a second, that he had listened to in his flat and in the Collar and in the Works for thirty hours. It was a swell. It began so gently he hardly noticed it — a faint rising pressure in the floor, like the first lift of a wave under a boat — and it rose, and rose, and went on rising, and at its height the whole city seemed to hum with it, every loose shutter faintly rattling, every scaffold pole faintly singing, the water in a trough nearby shivering into rings; and then, just as slowly, it fell away. It lasted — he counted without meaning to — about a minute and a half.

He knew what it was. He had known it the instant it started. It was one strike of Ruth Tally's hammer — one second of the outside world — arriving in here stretched so thin that it took ninety seconds to pass.

He looked at his visor. LOCAL RATIO: 703.

"There," said the boy, softly, beside him. He had his hand on the wall of the platform, flat, feeling it. "That's the Bell. Did you feel it? That's the Bell."

"I felt it."

"There's never been this many," the boy said. "Not ever. Gran Perpetua says—"

"Pim," said the woman.

The boy shut his mouth.

* * *

She was standing by the cart's handle with one hand on it, looking down at him, and now that his visor had cleared and his eyes had adjusted, he could see her properly.

She was short. Everyone here would be short — he'd see that soon enough — but she was short even so, and broad and dense through the shoulders and hips in a way that made him think of the body in the conduit, of the thick heavy bones. She wore the same kind of clothes: a patched tunic belted at the waist, a rigger's harness over it, loops of rope on one shoulder, heavy trousers with the knees reinforced in keelstone-grey cloth. Her forearms were bare and brown and roped with muscle, and they were scarred — pale lines across them, old burns, the marks of a lifetime of hauling rope. Her hands were the hands that had caught him.

Her hair was dark and cut short, roughly, as if with a knife. On the left side, above the temple, there was a streak of white in it. Not grey. White. A single clean stripe of it running back from her forehead, as though someone had drawn a line there in chalk.

And her face—

He could never, afterwards, describe her face well. He tried. He'd try for a long time. It was an ordinary face, broad-boned, weathered by the heat, and young, he thought — thirty, perhaps? — though the white streak made it hard to tell. It was not her face exactly. It was the way she looked at things. She looked at him on the cart the way you'd look at a view you had seen many times and still liked: patiently, attentively, without hurry, as though there were nothing he could possibly do or say that she hadn't already seen, and she'd be interested to see it again. Her eyes were grey-green and very steady. They didn't hurry. He had never seen eyes that hurried less.

Around her neck, on a cord, hung something black.

It was a key. A flat, dark, heavy-looking thing, the length of a finger, with a blunt cross-shaped bit. It lay against her tunic just below her collarbone. It didn't shine. It seemed, in the shadowless light, to drink the light in. He looked at it and something moved at the very back of his mind — some shape he half recognised, some picture from a manual — and then his tired brain let it go.

"What's your name?" he said.

She considered the question. "Do you need it?"

"I'd like it."

"Ione," said the boy, before she could answer. "She's Ione. Ione Tally. I'm Pim. This is Steward Harrow." He pointed at the rat. "He's named after a Steward. They all are. Mine are."

"Pim."

"Well, he asked."

The woman — Ione — looked at the boy. Then back at Cas. Something almost like a smile moved at the corner of her mouth, and was gone.

"You'll be all right on the cart," she said. "The weight will be bad for a few rounds. Don't try to stand. Drink when they give you water. It's clean." She looked away, across the city, at something he couldn't see. Her head was up. She was listening.

He heard it too, then. A whistle. High and shrill, somewhere off among the scaffold towers. Another answered it. Then a third, closer.

"Wardens," said Pim.

"Yes." Ione let go of the cart handle. "They watch the wells. Someone will have seen us from the towers." She looked down at Cas again, and there was something quick and serious in her face now. "Listen to me. There isn't time for most of it, so listen. They're going to call you the Relief. All of them. They'll kneel down. They'll cry. They'll take you to the First Steward and he'll call you the Relief too. Don't let them tell you what that means."

"What does it mean?"

"It means whatever they need it to mean." The whistles were closer. "When they're done with you — and they won't be done with you for a while — find the Tollers. Ask anyone for Toller's Tower. Ask for Perpetua."

"Why?"

She looked at him with those unhurried eyes. And he had the strangest feeling — a feeling he'd come to know well, over the weeks that followed, and never get used to — that she already knew exactly what he was going to say next, and was simply waiting, politely, for him to say it.

"Because," she said, "you came up out of the floor in a suit I've been waiting eleven years to see. And because you're going to want to know how long you've got."

Then she turned, and put her hand on the boy's shoulder, and said something to him too fast and too thick for Cas to follow. Pim nodded hard. And Ione Tally stepped to the wall of the platform, and put one hand on the top of it, and vaulted over it — lightly, easily, in that crushing weight, as though it were nothing — and dropped out of sight.

"She's Unwatched," Pim told him, in a whisper, as if it were an explanation. "She can't be here when they come. She'd get the Quiet." He patted Cas's gauntlet with a grimy hand. "Don't worry. I'll stay. I'm not anything. Nobody Quiets me. Steward Harrow, get *down* from there."

* * *

The Wardens came up the ramp to the well-platform at a run — six of them, then ten — and stopped dead.

They were big, for Holdfast. That was the first thing he learned about Wardens, and the thing he'd learn about all of them: they were the big ones, the men and women who'd grown a head taller than everyone else and twice as wide, and they wore it like a uniform. They had actual uniforms too: long coats of heavy grey cloth with a yellow lamp stitched on the breast, and on their heads — he stared — on their heads, hard hats. Old Works hard hats, cracked and mended and painted grey and polished to a shine, with the yellow lamp painted on the front. They carried staves: long poles of scaffold pipe with the ends wrapped in cord.

They stopped at the top of the ramp in a ragged line, breathing hard, and looked at the man in the grey suit lying on the rope-cart beside the Drop well, and nobody said anything at all.

Then one of them — the biggest, a slab of a man with a slow, square, heavy face and a grey beard cut close — took off his hard hat. He held it against his chest. And very slowly, as though his knees didn't quite want to, he knelt down on the black stone.

One by one, the others did the same.

"Relief," said the big man. His voice was deep and unsteady. "Lamp keep us. It's the Relief."

"It's the Relief," said the others, raggedly. One of them, a young woman at the end of the line, had begun to cry.

"I *said*," said Pim, to no one.

* * *

They carried him through the city.

Not on the rope-cart — the big Warden, whose name was Osk, sent for something better, and it came: a litter, a real one, a sort of stretcher-chair made of scaffold pipe and canvas, with poles for eight bearers. They lifted him onto it, suit and all, grunting, and it took all eight of them, big as they were, and they set off with him through the streets of Holdfast at a slow, heavy, lurching walk.

He saw it from there, the whole way, lying on his back with the Lace above him and the city going past on either side. And the city saw him.

Word had got there ahead of them. He never knew how; Pim, probably, who had vanished down the ramp the moment the litter came and was nowhere to be seen. But by the time the Wardens had carried him fifty metres from the well, there were people in the lanes, and by the time they'd gone a hundred there were crowds. They came out of doorways and down ladders and up from under walkways. They leaned out of container windows and hung off scaffold towers and stood on roofs. They were short — all of them, every one, men and women both, broad and stocky and heavy-boned — and they were pale, a strange sallow pallor under the violet light, and their clothes were the same patchwork of reused fabric as the dead body in the conduit, a thousand different mendings, every colour. They were silent at first. They stood and stared at the litter going by. And then someone — an old woman in a doorway — put her hand to her mouth and said, "*Relief*," in a cracked voice, and it went through the crowd like wind through grass.

*Relief. Relief. It's the Relief.*

They began to kneel. Not all of them. But many. They knelt in the lanes as he went by, and put their hands on their chests, or touched their foreheads to the ground, or held up children to look at him. They wept. Some of them sang: a thin, wavering, old-fashioned melody that he felt he almost knew. Some of them reached out, as the litter passed, just to touch the side of it, or the edge of his suit. The Wardens let them.

He had never been so frightened in his life. Not in the Shear. Not on the gallery floor.

He tried to look at things. It was the only defence he had. He looked at the lanes: narrow, twisting, paved in black keelstone polished smooth by a century of feet. He looked at the signs. There were signs everywhere, and he knew them — he had walked past them all his working life. STAIRWELL C. MUSTER POINT B. NO NAKED FLAMES. KEEP THIS DOOR CLOSED. They were station signs, the old enamelled plates from the Vault's walls and walkways, and they had been taken down and put up again as street names: a lane of containers with a sign bolted over its entrance that read CORRIDOR 9 — AUTHORISED PERSONNEL ONLY, and a crossroads marked with a big yellow EMERGENCY ASSEMBLY AREA plate that had been lovingly repainted so many times the letters stood up from it in ridges. He looked at the shrines: little niches in walls, with a lamp in them — an actual lamp, a clay dish of oil with a wick — and a picture behind it, painted, of a point of light with rays. He looked at a rat pen: a big wire cage built into the angle of two walls, full of fat, sleek, contented rats, dozens of them, with a sign over it that said, in careful hand-painting, HOLDFAST'S OTHER CITIZENS — PLEASE DO NOT FEED.

He looked at the mural.

It was painted across the whole side of a scaffold tower, fifteen storeys high, and the litter went right past its foot so that he saw it from below, rising over him. It showed a man. A tall man in a dark jacket, painted enormous, standing on a high place with his arm raised toward a white star in a violet sky. The man's face was noble and grieving, with silver hair. And in his raised hand he was holding up a key. A small black key, the length of a finger, on a broken chain. Beneath the man, in a crowd, painted small, were people in white coats and orange overalls, looking up at him and weeping. Across the bottom of the mural, in letters a metre high, it said:

:: THE GIVING
:: FOUNDER CRANE GAVE THE KEY TO THE LAMP
:: THAT IT MIGHT NEVER BE PUT OUT

Cas stared at it until the litter carried him past.

The key was black. It was the length of a finger. It had a blunt, cross-shaped bit.

* * *

The Steward's House stood at the foot of Spindle One.

He knew it was Spindle One because it was the biggest; because the others were further off and it was right there, filling half the sky, a black lattice tower a hundred and fifty metres across rising straight up from the heart of the city into the light; because at its foot, where in the old schematics there had been a lift terminal — the great steel shed where the Crown's lift came down — there was now a building. It was the only building he'd seen in Holdfast that looked as if it had been built all at once, on purpose. It was made of keelstone slabs laid with great care, black and shining, with a wide stair up to a doorway and tall narrow windows with coloured glass in them — bottle glass, he'd find out later, and lamp lenses, and the glass from old instrument dials, melted and set in lead. Over the door, carved into the stone, was the lamp with its rays.

They carried him up the stair and through the door into a long, high, cool hall, shadowed, lit by oil lamps, and set the litter down. The Wardens stepped back. The door closed behind them, and the noise of the crowd outside, which had been rising all the way here like a tide, dropped to a murmur.

A man was waiting for him.

He was old. Sixty, perhaps, or more — it was hard to tell with Holdfast faces. He was taller than most of them, lean where the others were broad, and he wore a long coat of plain dark cloth, very well mended, and no hard hat. His hair was silver, and there was a lot of it, swept back from a high forehead. He had a long, grave, intelligent face. It was, Cas realised with a small cold shock, a face he had seen before. He'd seen it a quarter of an hour ago, fifteen storeys high, holding up a key.

The old man came and stood beside the litter. He looked down at Cas without speaking. At the suit, the keel, the visor. At the Ministry crest, stencilled on the shoulder plate, which Cas had never noticed until he saw the man's eyes go to it and stop there and go very still.

Then the man smiled. It was a tired, careful, courteous smile, and there was something underneath it that Cas couldn't read at all.

"Welcome to Holdfast," said First Steward Halloran Crane. "Relief. You're a hundred and sixty-eight years late."
