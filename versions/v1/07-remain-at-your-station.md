---
title: Remain at Your Station
---

> Who keeps the Watch? — We keep the Watch. — Until? — Until Relief. — And when Relief comes? — Then we are relieved.
> — The Common Office, as said in the Commons every seventh round

They got him out of the suit in a small stone room at the back of the Steward's House, and it nearly killed him.

In the suit, with the exo taking most of it, the weight had been a crushing thing but a bearable one. Out of it — in the grey liner, standing on his own legs on the black stone floor — it was something else. It was like being filled with sand. His arms hung off his shoulders like buckets. His knees shook. His heart, which had been going hard since the ladder, went harder, labouring to push blood that weighed half as much again as it should up into a head that felt as though it were being slowly pressed into his neck. He took two steps and his vision went grey at the edges and the big Warden, Osk, caught him under the arms and lowered him into a chair like a man putting down a sack of cement.

"Slow," said Osk. "Slow. Everyone's slow the first rounds. Even babies."

"Babies?"

"From the Crèche." Osk had a voice like gravel being poured. "Born heavy, we are, but you still have to learn to stand in it. Takes a year." He considered Cas. "Take you longer, maybe."

They brought him water in a tin cup. It was warm and tasted of iron and something green, and it was the best thing he had ever drunk. They brought him food: a flat, dense, dark-green cake, like a biscuit or a very heavy bread, with a smell of pond and salt. He looked at it.

"Greenbread," said Osk. "Algae. Cricket flour. Salt. Eat it slow."

He ate it slow. It tasted of the sea, if the sea had been baked. He ate all of it.

The suit stood in the corner, empty and hulking, the keel humming quietly to itself on its back. Osk kept looking at it. Everyone who came into the room — and a lot of people came into the room, Wardens and Stewards in grey and black coats, on one pretext or another — looked at it, and then at him, and then looked away quickly, as though they had seen something they weren't meant to.

Then Halloran Crane came in, and everyone else went out, except Osk, who stood by the door with his hard hat under his arm and his eyes on the floor.

* * *

Crane sat down opposite him. He had brought a lamp — a real lamp, a clay dish of oil with a floating wick — and he set it on the table between them, though the room was lit perfectly well by the violet glow through the window. He folded his hands. They were long, fine, old hands, with an ink stain on the middle finger of the right.

"You'll forgive me," he said, "if I'm not quite sure how to begin. There are protocols for this. There is, in fact, an entire liturgy. I've read it many times. I've never expected to use it." He smiled slightly. "My grandfather used to say that it had been written by people who never really believed it would be needed, for people who never really believed it would come. I find myself unable to recall a single word of it."

Cas said nothing. He didn't know what to say.

"So instead," said Crane, "I'll ask you something simple. What is the Outside?"

"The Outside?"

"Where you came from. Below the Drop. Beyond it." Crane's voice was quite steady. "Our histories say that when the Lamp was kindled, the world beyond the Watch was burned. That the Founders were the last of it. That we keep the Watch for a world that is gone, until Relief comes out of the dark. I've always found that a very beautiful idea. I've never been quite sure I believed it." He looked at the suit in the corner, at the stencilled crest on its shoulder. "So. What is it?"

"It's a city," said Cas. "It's called Averna. It's big. Six hundred million people. It's where the Draw — where this place — sends its power. It's—" He stopped. He thought of the Tier Nine arterial at dawn, the bunting, the anthem. "It's fine. It's still there. Nothing's burned."

"And how long," said Crane, "since the Kindling? Outside?"

"Thirty-three hours."

Crane didn't move.

He was so still that for a moment Cas wondered if he'd heard. He sat with his long hands folded on the table and his eyes on the little flame of the lamp, and nothing in his face changed, nothing at all, and the stillness went on and on until it became a thing in the room, a thing you could feel pressing on you like the weight.

"Thirty-three hours," Crane said at last.

"And a bit."

"A day. A day and a half." Crane closed his eyes. "My grandfather's grandfather's grandfather was born in this city. And lived here, and died here, and was given to the Tanks. So was his son. So was his. And outside—" He opened his eyes. "Outside, it's still the same night."

"Nearly."

"Nearly." He breathed out. It was almost a laugh. "The Tollers will be very pleased with themselves. They've always said so. In a way that one could, if one was careful, pretend not to hear." He looked at Cas. "Is there anyone else? Coming after you?"

"No."

"Is there relief?"

Cas thought of Rask. The crisis room. Three suits. Ben in the hall of algae. Teo in a chair.

"There's me," he said.

Crane nodded slowly, as if that were the answer he'd expected. Then he stood up.

"Come," he said. "I'd like to show you something. Osk, help the Relief. Slowly."

* * *

The chapel was at the heart of the Steward's House, a small square room of black keelstone with no windows, lit by a single lamp that burned in a niche at the far end. There was nothing else in it. No benches, no altar, no pictures. Only the lamp, and behind the lamp, set into the wall in a frame of polished keelstone as though it were the most precious object in the world, a sheet of yellowed laminate.

Cas knew it before he could read it. He'd walked past it at Gate Nine a day and a half ago. He'd walked past it every working day for twelve years.

:: SECTION 9 — IN THE EVENT OF CONTAINMENT LOSS:
:: REMAIN AT YOUR STATION AND AWAIT RELIEF.
:: DO NOT ATTEMPT TO TRAVERSE THE LATTICE.
:: RELIEF CREWS WILL BE DISPATCHED.
:: YOU WILL NOT BE FORGOTTEN.

The stick figure beside its stick console. The Ministry typeface from the Year Twenties. Someone had once drawn a moustache on the stick figure at Gate Nine; nobody had ever drawn anything on this one. It had been cleaned — lovingly, with soft cloths, for generations — until the laminate had gone as clear and pale as horn.

"The Placard," said Crane quietly. "Our first scripture. It was on the wall of the assembly bay the night of the Kindling. The Founders gathered under it. Nineteen hundred of them. The Lamp had just been lit — the sky had just filled with light — the Drop had just taken the first of them who tried to go down. And Founder Crane stood under this and read it aloud to them. *Remain at your station and await relief.* And they did." He looked at it. "They've been doing it ever since."

Cas couldn't speak.

"You're thinking it's an evacuation notice," said Crane.

"It *is* an evacuation notice."

"Yes." Crane didn't seem offended. If anything, he seemed faintly amused, in a tired way. "My ancestor was a practical man. He needed something to hold nineteen hundred terrified people together in the dark, by the second week at the latest. This was on the wall. It had the great advantage of being true." He turned his head. "Look at the floor."

Cas looked. Running along the base of the chapel wall and out through the door, painted onto the black stone in gold leaf, worn almost away by feet in places and repainted over the wear so many times that it stood up from the floor like a ridge of metal, was a line of arrows. He knew them too. They were the arrows from the Vault's walkways, the little painted chevrons that showed you the way to your muster point in an emergency.

"They lead to Muster Point B," said Crane. "In the Commons. It's the holiest place in Holdfast. Pilgrims come to it on their knees on the seventh round. It is the place, you see, where they were told to wait." He paused. "My ancestor had a sense of humour. I think it was the only thing that kept him alive."

Cas made a sound. He didn't know whether it was a laugh or something else. Crane looked at him with that tired, courteous half-smile.

"Yes," said Crane. "I've often felt that way myself."

* * *

Back in the small stone room, with the lamp on the table and a fresh cup of iron-tasting water, Crane told him the history.

He told it well. He'd told it, Cas guessed, many times, to many children, in the Commons on high days. The Kindling: the night the Lamp was lit, the Founders at their posts, the great light filling the sky. Founder Crane in the Crown, who saw that the Lamp must never go out, and gave the key into it so that it never could. The Descent: nineteen hundred souls coming down the spindles into the Vault as the world beyond burned. The Drop, which took all those who tried to go down after it. The Sealing — the dark days when they understood they could not leave. The Watch: the Founders' vow to keep their station. The Crèche, the first children born under the Lamp. The Tanks, where the dead went to feed the living. The Tallies, the Bell, the rounds and the shifts. A hundred and sixty-eight years. Seven generations. Thirty-one thousand souls.

"Thirty-one thousand," said Cas.

"Thirty-one thousand, four hundred and six, at the last count. Four hundred and seven, now." Crane inclined his head at him. "The Founders were fruitful. They had to be. There was so little else to do." He folded his hands. "And now I'm going to ask you something, and I'd be grateful if you'd answer it as honestly as you answered the last."

"All right."

"The Bells are coming faster," said Crane.

He said it softly. By the door, Osk shifted his feet.

"In the Founders' time," said Crane, "the Bell came once every three rounds. Once every nine shifts. You could go days without feeling it. In my grandmother's time, once a round. In my own childhood, twice. When I became Steward, three. Ten, eight years ago. Now there are more than a hundred. It comes while you're eating. It comes while you're sleeping. The Stewardship teaches that this is the Quickening — that the Lamp's heart beats faster as Relief draws near." He looked up at Cas. "And now Relief is here. So. The Quickening. What is it?"

Cas looked at him for a long moment.

"The Bell's not the Lamp's heart," he said. "It's an alarm. A hammer, down in the station, outside the — below the Drop. It strikes once a second. Outside seconds. My seconds. It's been going since the Kindling. You're hearing each stroke stretched out, because time in here runs faster than out there. That's why a day and a half outside is a hundred and sixty-eight years in here."

"And it's quickening because—"

"Because time in here is slowing down. Coming back toward ours. The thing that makes time run fast in here is the Lamp. The Lamp is—" He looked for words. There were no Holdfast words for it. He used his own. "The Lamp is a debt. Everything the station ever gave the city outside, it borrowed. From the — from the ground of things. The emptiness. And the emptiness always takes it back. Plus interest. For ninety years the station kept putting off paying it. The night of the Kindling, it stopped being able to. And now the debt's being called in. Slowly at first. Then faster. The Bells are the sound of it coming due."

"And when it comes due?"

"All at once," said Cas. "At the Lamp. It'll be — everything. This city, and the station, and mine, and the world."

"When?"

"The Bells will tell you better than I can. My guess—" He made himself say it. "Weeks. Your weeks. Maybe less. And the Lace—" he nodded at the window, where the violet glow came in "—the Lace is part of it. When it reaches the Rim, it's over."

Crane was quiet for a while.

"Thank you," he said at last. "I thought it was something like that. I have thought it for some years."

"You knew?"

"I have the Tollers' numbers. The true ones. They come to me every seventh round, as they've come to every First Steward since the Founders." He moved his hand slightly on the table. "I can read a curve, Relief. I'm not a physicist. But I can read a curve."

"And you haven't told them."

"No." Crane looked at him steadily. "I haven't told them."

* * *

"I want to ask you for something," said Crane.

The lamp flickered between them. Outside the window, the long swell of the Bell began, rising up through the floor.

"In an hour," said Crane, "I'm going to have to take you to the Commons. There is no way to avoid it. Half the city saw you carried here. The other half has heard. They'll come whether I call them or not, and it will be better if I call them. There are twenty thousand people who will stand in the Commons tonight and look at you." He waited while the Bell reached its height and the shutters on the window hummed faintly and fell still. "They've been waiting for you for a hundred and sixty-eight years. Every one of them was told as a child that one day you'd come. And now you have, and in a few weeks, by your own account, they're all going to die."

"Maybe."

"Maybe." Crane accepted the correction gravely. "Maybe. So I'd like to ask you — when you stand up in front of them tonight — to tell them it will be all right."

Cas said nothing.

"I'm not asking you to lie," said Crane. "I'm asking you to be kind. They're going to look at you and they're going to want to know one thing, and it isn't the physics. It's whether they're alone. Tell them they're not. Tell them the Outside remembers them. Tell them you've come to help. Tell them to keep the Watch." His voice was very gentle. "Let them be calm. For the time they have left. Let them spend it with their children instead of screaming in the lanes. Let them die, if they're going to die, with the Lamp over them and the Relief among them and the Watch kept, instead of — the other way. I've read the old books. I know what the other way looks like." He paused. "It's the only mercy I have to give them. I'm asking you to help me give it."

Cas looked at the lamp. At the little flame, steady in its dish of oil.

"Can it be stopped?" said Crane.

"Maybe."

The old man's eyes moved very slightly.

"There's a mechanism," said Cas. "In the Crown. At the top of Spindle One. The woman who built this place designed it for exactly this. It won't save the station, but it might — it might close the Lamp. Seal it. Before the debt comes due." He hesitated. "It's locked. It needs a key."

Crane had gone quite white.

"A key," he said.

"A black key. About so long." Cas held up a finger. "Keelstone. With a—"

"Our Founder gave the key to the Lamp."

"He dropped it."

The words were out before he could stop them. They sat on the table between them next to the lamp. Crane looked at them.

"In Holdfast," he said finally, very softly, "we say *gave*."

* * *

The Commons had been Assembly Bay One. The sign was still over the great doors — ASSEMBLY BAY 1 — CAPACITY 2,500 — and someone had painted over the capacity with a lamp. It was a vast hangar of a place, the biggest single space in the Vault's floor, where in the station's working days the Lattice crews had mustered by the hundred at the start of every shift to be briefed and sent up the spindles. Its roof had been partly stripped for materials generations ago, so that it was open to the sky in long strips between the girders, and through the gaps the violet light came down, and the Lace.

There were twenty thousand people in it. There were more outside, packed into the lanes around it, up on the roofs, hanging off the scaffolds. He could hear them before he saw them — a sound like the sea, like surf on a beach — and when the Wardens carried him in through a side door onto a raised platform at the front of the hall, the sound stopped, all at once, as though a door had shut on it.

He was standing. He had insisted. Osk was at one elbow and another Warden at the other, and he was in the grey liner with a Holdfast coat over it that someone had found for him, and he was standing, and it was taking everything he had.

The faces went back and back. A sea of them. Pale, broad, heavy-boned faces under the violet light, upturned, silent, every one of them looking at him. Men and women and children. Old people held up by younger ones. Babies. He saw a woman in the front row with a baby asleep against her shoulder and a little girl of five or six holding her hand, standing on tiptoe, straining to see. He saw an old man weeping without any sound. He saw Wardens along the walls with their staves and their hard hats. He saw, on the wall behind him, a big board — a sort of scoreboard, black, with white numerals painted on hanging plates — that said BELL 119,488 and under it INTERVAL: 14 and a word he couldn't read.

Muster Point B was a yellow-painted square on the floor at the foot of the platform. It was surrounded by a low rail of polished brass. Inside it, on the black stone, the paint was worn down to bare stone in the shape of knees.

Crane stood beside him. He raised his hands. And the whole hall — twenty thousand voices — began to sing.

Cas knew the tune.

It took him a few bars. It was slow, much slower than he knew it, and the words were different — worn smooth, the way words get worn when they're passed through a hundred mouths, then a thousand, then a hundred thousand; the rhymes had slipped and the lines had changed and whole verses had grown out of nothing. But under it, unmistakable, was the Tending. The anthem. The children's choir version he'd heard on the Tier Nine lampposts at dawn yesterday, thirty-three hours ago, a hundred and sixty-eight years ago. *Tend the flame, tend the flame, for the ones who come after.* Here, it had become: *Tend the Lamp, tend the Lamp, till the Relief comes after.* Twenty thousand people, singing it very slowly under the Lace, in a hall where the paint on the floor had been worn through by knees.

He had to hold on to Osk's arm.

When they'd finished, Crane spoke. It was a call-and-response, and the whole hall knew it.

"Who keeps the Watch?"

"*We keep the Watch.*"

"Until?"

"*Until Relief.*"

"And when Relief comes?"

"*Then we are relieved.*"

"Relief," said Halloran Crane, into the silence, "has come."

The sound the hall made was not a cheer. It was something older than cheering. It went up into the girders and the gaps in the roof and out into the lanes, where the people outside took it up, until the whole city seemed to be making it. Somewhere at the back someone was screaming. People were kneeling, falling to their knees in waves, clinging to each other. The woman in the front row with the baby had her face in her free hand. The little girl was jumping up and down.

Crane turned to him. He said nothing. He just looked at him, courteously, and stepped back.

Cas faced the hall.

He had meant to be vague. He'd lain on the bed in the small stone room while they waited, and planned it: a few careful sentences that wouldn't be lies and wouldn't be the truth. *I've come from the Outside. The Outside is still there. I've come to try to help.* All true. All nothing. He'd been good at sentences like that for fifteen years. He had a whole professional life of them.

He opened his mouth. And he saw the little girl in the front row. She'd stopped jumping. She was standing very still, holding her mother's hand, looking up at him with an expression of such total, perfect, open trust that it went into him like a blade.

"I've come from Outside," he said.

His voice was thin. It didn't carry. But the hall was so silent that it did.

"The Outside's still there. It hasn't forgotten you." He swallowed. "I was sent to help you. And I — we're working on it. There are people working on it. Keep the Watch. Keep it a little longer. It's going to be—"

He stopped.

The little girl was looking at him.

"It's going to be all right," said Casimir Hale.

* * *

He'd been loved before. He supposed he had. Sabine had loved him, once, for a while, before the Review and the Standby and the silence. His mother had loved him. Nell loved him, in the fierce unforgiving way of ten-year-olds.

He had never been loved like this.

It came up at him out of that hall like heat out of a furnace. Twenty thousand people, weeping, singing, reaching toward him, calling out to him. A man at the front threw something up onto the platform — a little carved lamp on a cord, yellow plastic, like the one on the body in the conduit — and then someone else threw another, and another, until the platform was scattered with them. Mothers held up their babies. An old woman at the brass rail of Muster Point B was kissing the floor. Every face that he looked at looked back at him with a joy that was so complete, so helpless, that it didn't seem possible it could fit inside a person.

It felt wonderful.

That was the thing he'd remember afterwards, lying awake. Not the guilt — that came later, and it came hard. But in the moment, standing there with Osk holding his arm and the whole of Holdfast pouring its love up at him like light: it felt *wonderful*. It felt like the warmest, easiest, most natural thing in the world. He had told them a lie and they loved him for it, and every nerve in his body wanted to stay there, in that, forever, and tell them another.

That was the horror of it. That was the whole machine. He'd thought he understood the Commonweal. He'd been inside it all his life. He had never once, until that moment, understood why it worked.

Then he saw her.

She was at the very back of the hall, by the great doors, standing in a gap in the crowd with her back against the wall and her arms folded. Everyone around her was singing. She wasn't. Everyone around her was weeping or kneeling or reaching up toward the platform. She was standing perfectly still, looking at him over twenty thousand heads, with the white streak in her hair and the black key at her throat.

There was nothing in her face. No anger. No contempt. No disappointment, even — he looked for it, and it wasn't there. There was only patience. That same unhurried patience he'd seen at the top of the well, the look of someone watching a scene they'd seen before, many times, and would wait through to the end.

It was worse than anything else could have been.

She met his eyes. And across the whole length of the Commons, over the singing, very clearly, she moved her lips. One word.

*Tollers.*

Then the crowd shifted, and she was gone.

* * *

They gave him a room in the Steward's House. It was small and square, with a bed that was a wooden frame strung with rope and a mattress stuffed with something that crackled, and a window with a shutter, and a basin, and the suit standing in the corner humming to itself. Osk sat outside the door on a stool. Cas heard him settle there, and sigh, and heard the stool creak under his weight.

He lay on the bed. The weight lay on him. He could feel his heart labouring. He could feel every one of his ribs.

After a while he got up, which took a long time, and went to the suit, and took the little recorder out of its chest compartment. It was a slim grey Ministry thing, standard issue, with a crystal store that would outlast the suit. It had a channel that fed the suit's radio, through the orange puck at the top of the well, down through the Shear, to another puck, and another, and a chair in Lattice Station 14-C.

He sat on the floor with his back against the bed, and pressed the button.

"Teo," he said.

And then he couldn't think what came after. So he just talked.

"I'm through. I'm through, I'm alive, the keel held. Your keel. It's — there's a city in here, Teo. Up the conduit, on the Anvil. A city. Thirty-one thousand people. It's a hundred and sixty-eight years old. They've got — they've got rats, they keep them, they name them after their officials. They've got a hymn that's the Tending. They've got the Section 9 placard in a chapel with a candle in front of it. They thought I was a — they called me the Relief. They carried me through the streets. They—" He stopped. "They asked me to tell them it would be all right. So I did. Within an hour. I stood up in front of twenty thousand people and I lied to them and it was the best I've felt in fifteen years." He put his head back against the bed frame. "I don't know what I'm doing, Teo. I thought I would. I don't. I'll send more. Are you all right? You won't hear this for — I don't know. Don't answer. Yes, answer. I'll wait."

He pressed the button again. He sat there.

Then, after a while, he changed the channel. There was a second channel on the recorder, one that didn't go anywhere. It just stored.

"Nell," he said.

He sat there on the floor of a stone room in a city that shouldn't exist, under a sky full of light.

"You were right," he said. "There's one. There's a star in here. It's — it's not a very nice one. It's too bright to look at. They call it the Lamp. There's a sort of lace round it, all across the sky, like frost, and it moves. Everything here's lit by it. There's no night. They close their shutters when they want to sleep." He paused. "You'd have liked it. I think. You'd have been frightened of it, and then you'd have wanted to know what it was made of. That's what I did."

He sat there a while longer, holding the recorder.

"I'm still scared," he told her. "You said that's allowed."

He pressed the button, and put the recorder down on the floor beside him, and closed his eyes.

He was nearly asleep when something small and hard hit the shutter.

He opened his eyes. It came again: *tock*. A pebble. And a third time. He got up — slowly, slowly — and crossed the room, and eased the shutter open a crack.

Below the window, in the narrow lane that ran along the back of the Steward's House, there was a small, thin, very dirty boy with a rat on his shoulder, grinning up at him.

"She says," whispered Pim, "the Tollers want you. She says come now, while the Wardens are drunk. She says bring your notebook."
