title: The Standby
part: Part One · The Draw
---

> The Hollowell Draw is experiencing a minor margin irregularity. There is no danger to the public. Citizens are reminded that calm is care.
> — Office of Public Calm, bulletin 1, 04:30, Continuance Day

By twenty past six Casimir Hale had counted fourteen thousand, seven hundred and sixty strokes of the hammer, and the phone had still not rung.

He was sitting on the edge of his bed in the dark with his boots on. He had put the boots on at about a quarter past two, when the first stroke came up through the building like a truck driving over the roof of the world, and he had put on his trousers and his good shirt and his coat, and then he had sat down to wait, and he had been sitting ever since. At some point he had begun counting. He wasn't sure why. It was the kind of thing a man did when he had spent fifteen years being paid to wait for a phone call and the call had finally come and it had not come by phone.

The phone lay on the bedside table next to a glass of water he hadn't drunk. It was a heavy black Ministry handset with no screen and a single button, and it had been issued to him on his first day as Standby with a small ceremony in an office on Tier Two, and in fifteen years it had never once made a sound. He dusted it on Sundays. He had once, drunk, tried to call his own number on it and found that it did not make outgoing calls. It was not a phone, really; it was a promise, or a threat, or a leash, and it had sat there by his bed for fifteen years like a small black animal that might one day wake up.

Fourteen thousand, seven hundred and sixty-one.

The hammer did not sound like a sound. It sounded the way it would feel to be very small and live inside a drum. Every second, precisely, the whole building shuddered — the window glass hummed, the water in the untouched glass flinched into a ring, the cutlery in the kitchen drawer gave a small metallic cough — and then there was a second of silence, and then it came again. The flat was on Tier Nine, a hundred metres above the ground and four kilometres from the rim of the Pit, and at this distance, through all that concrete and air and sleeping city, it should not have been possible to feel anything that happened inside the Draw at all.

Cas knew what it was. He had known at 02:14, sitting up in bed with his heart going. He'd have known it in his sleep. There was a picture of that hammer in the second chapter of every textbook on vacuum engineering ever printed, and a caption underneath in Iris Hollowell's own words: *When all else has failed, the station will say so.*

Fourteen thousand, seven hundred and sixty-two.

At twenty-five past six there was a knock at the door.

* * *

There were two of them on the landing, a man and a woman in the charcoal overcoats of the Office of Public Calm, and behind them, a little apart, a third man who was older and softer and wore no overcoat at all, only a brown cardigan buttoned wrong.

"Dr Hale," said the soft man. "I'm so sorry to wake you."

"You didn't."

"No." The man's eyes went over him, the boots, the coat, the good shirt. Something that might have been approval, or pity, moved in his face. "No, I don't suppose we did. Ansel Gaunt. We've never met, but I've read a great deal about you. Shall we?"

The car was long and black and warm and smelled of ginger biscuits. There was a paper bag of them on the seat between them and Gaunt offered it to Cas and, when Cas shook his head, took one himself and ate it carefully over a handkerchief spread on his knee. The two in overcoats sat up front. The car pulled out into the lane and the hammer came up through its wheels and its seats and Gaunt's biscuit, and Gaunt closed his eyes briefly each time it did, the way a man might wince at a toothache he had decided not to mention.

"We didn't phone," said Gaunt, "because phones are recorded."

"By you."

"By us, yes. Which is precisely the problem. Things that are recorded have a way of becoming history. Things that aren't recorded have a way of becoming—" he searched for the word, dabbing a crumb from his lip "—negotiable. The Minister would like this morning to remain negotiable for as long as possible."

"How long have you known?"

"About the irregularity?"

"About the Draw."

Gaunt looked at him mildly. "Since two-fourteen, Dr Hale. Like everyone else in the Pit district who was awake, or who was woken. It's been rather hard to miss." A pause. "If you mean when did we know that it was *serious* — I'd say we're still finding that out. Which is why you're in my car."

They came out of the lane onto the Tier Nine arterial and the city opened up around them.

Averna at dawn on Continuance Day. Cas had lived in it all his life and it still, sometimes, stopped his breath. Ahead and above, the Stacks rose in their terraced ranks, tier on tier on tier, a hundred and forty levels of housing and gardens and plazas climbing into the grey, lit from within like vast lanterns, and above the highest of them the faint milky curve of the Canopy, the weather shield that had held back sixty years of storms. Down at street level the Continuance Day bunting was already up — blue and gold, THE FUTURE WILL PAY on every banner — and the public speakers on the lampposts were playing the Tending anthem on a soft loop, the children's choir version, very sweet. There were people out, a lot of people for this hour. They were walking in the ordinary way, carrying the ordinary bags, and they were not talking, and every one of them, Cas saw, flinched very slightly once a second.

"There's been no announcement?"

"There's been a bulletin. Minor irregularity. No danger to the public. Calm is care." Gaunt took another biscuit. "People are being very good. People are always very good at first."

The car turned south, toward the Pit. They passed a plaza with a Quota clock on a pillar in the middle — a big public display, the kind every district had, showing the Draw's output in real time in tall gold numerals. Cas had grown up with those clocks. As a boy he'd stood in front of the one on Tier Four and watched the numbers climb on Deepening Day and cheered with everyone else. It said 00.00 TW. As they passed, a man in a Ministry jumpsuit on a ladder beside it reached up and switched it off, and it went dark, and a small crowd that had gathered to look at it stood looking at the dark instead.

"The Minister felt," said Gaunt, "that the number was unhelpful."

"Zero?"

"The number before zero. For about forty minutes after two-fourteen the clocks showed an output of a hundred and nine terawatts. Then they started showing numbers that aren't numbers. Then zero." He folded his handkerchief. "I'm not a physicist, Dr Hale. But I can read a room, and I read a hundred and nine terawatts, and I read the faces of the engineers in the crisis room who read it, and I thought: I'll go and fetch the man who wrote the memo."

Cas said nothing.

"The Margin Review," said Gaunt gently. "Year Seventy-Six. Section four, the projections. You were the junior author. I've read the original. Not the version that was published. The original, the one with your name on it and Dr Adeyemi's. It had some very striking phrases." He looked out of the window. "'A structure of obligation that cannot, even in principle, be discharged.' I remember thinking that was a remarkable sentence to find in a technical report. It sounded like scripture."

"Adeyemi wrote that."

"Did he? He's dead now, of course. Natural causes." Gaunt said it without emphasis, the way you'd say a man was tall. "And you signed the revised version. The one that said the Margin was stable."

"Yes."

"Nobody made you."

"No."

"No." Gaunt seemed to think about that for a while. "That's the thing I've never understood. Most of the people I talk to in my job, I can tell you exactly what made them. A child. A debt. A photograph. But you, Dr Hale — nobody lifted a finger. We simply asked, and you signed, and we gave you a flat and a phone and you sat by it for fifteen years like a good dog." He turned from the window and met Cas's eyes, and his own were very tired and very kind. "I'm not saying it to hurt you. I'm saying it because I want you to understand that I know exactly who you are. It will save us both time."

The hammer came up through the car.

"How long," said Cas, "until we get there?"

* * *

You saw the Pit before you saw the Draw. You always did — it was fourteen kilometres across and three deep, a crater dug out of the old industrial belt in the Year Minus Six, and the Draw sat in the bottom of it like a vast black egg in a bowl. From the rim, on a normal morning, you saw the Collar first: a ring of converter halls and admin blocks and trunk-line heads, lit up and smoking, with the great superconducting lines marching away from it over the city on pylons like the legs of enormous insects. Behind the Collar, the dark bulk of the Works. Behind the Works, the dome.

This morning, from the rim, you could hardly see it at all.

The air over the Pit was moving. That was the only way to put it. It shimmered, the way air shimmers over a road on a hot day — except that it rose in a column, a clear, trembling pillar at least ten kilometres wide, going straight up into the grey dawn until it was lost against the Canopy. Through it the Draw looked wavering and uncertain, as if seen through water. The edges of things kept coming apart and knitting back.

And there were birds. Hundreds of them — gulls, starlings, the fat grey Averna pigeons that lived on the Stacks — circling above the rim in a great slow wheel. None of them flew over the Pit. They came to its edge and turned away, came and turned away, around and around, like water circling a drain it would not go down.

Their car stopped at a cordon of Civil Defence vehicles and Calm Office cars on the rim road. The hammer was much louder here: a vast, dull, full-body blow, once a second, that made the gravel on the road jump. A Civil Defence sergeant waved them through, and as they passed her Cas saw her glance at her wristwatch and then at a clock on the dashboard of her truck and frown.

"The clocks near the rim run fast," said Gaunt, who had seen him see it. "Twelve minutes fast, at the cordon, since two. They were eight minutes fast at five. It's creeping out. Nobody seems able to tell me whether that's important."

"It's important."

"I thought it might be." Gaunt took off his cardigan, folded it carefully, and laid it on the seat. Underneath he was wearing a very good grey shirt. "The Minister's waiting."

* * *

The crisis room was in the Collar's admin block, on the fourth floor, in a long room that had been built as a conference suite for foreign delegations. It still had the gold carpet and the portraits: Iris Hollowell, severe and hollow-cheeked, with a cigarette she'd been painted without; the first Minister of Continuance, cutting a ribbon; a sweeping mural of the Vault seen from the Crown, the eight spindles rising to the Lesion, the Lesion a gold star with painted rays. Someone had pushed the delegate tables together and covered them with screens and paper and cold coffee, and about twenty people were standing around them in the stunned, overlit way of people who had been awake all night doing something that wasn't working.

Cas knew about half of them. Engineers, mostly — Collar shift leads, a couple of the Works directors, a woman from the Lattice office he'd been at university with who looked at him across the room, went white, and looked away. People who had been told at some point, by someone, that the Standby was not somebody you talked to. He'd got used to that look. He'd seen it on the faces of old friends in the street.

At the head of the table stood Ondine Rask.

The Minister of Continuance was a tall, spare woman in her sixties with silver hair cut close to her skull and a dark suit without a crease in it. She had been Minister for nineteen years. She had presided over the First Deepening, and the export treaties, and the Canopy's extension over the southern coast, and Cas had seen her face on every public screen in the city since he was a young man. It was said she slept four hours a night. It looked, this morning, as though she had slept none, and it looked as though she had decided that this was nobody's business.

"Dr Hale," she said. "Thank you for coming."

As though he'd had a choice. He nodded.

"You'll forgive me if I don't waste your time. We have had a Continuance Event." She said the phrase with no irony at all. "At approximately 02:05 this morning, during a scheduled test, Tensioner Six in the Lattice lost its seat. The interlocks failed to compensate. Since that time we have been unable to re-establish control of the Lesion, the Lattice, or the Rollover. We have no communication with the Crown or with any part of the station inside the Works. We evacuated the Collar at 03:10. At around the same time we lost the output. The Commonweal grid is running on reserve and on the old fusion plants at Tessarine and Kovač, which were not built to carry it, and which will begin to fail within eighteen hours. Those are the facts." She put both hands flat on the table. "I have been assured by every engineer in this room that the situation can be recovered, and I have been assured by every engineer in this room that they do not know how. Colonel Gaunt tells me you might."

"Why?"

She blinked.

"Why does Colonel Gaunt tell you that," said Cas. "I haven't been inside the Draw in fifteen years. I haven't been allowed to read a Lattice report since Year Seventy-Six. Everyone in this room knows the station better than I do now."

"Because," said Gaunt, from the doorway, where he had settled himself on a gold chair with his hands folded, "everyone in this room has spent the night telling the Minister what they think she would like to hear, and I have some small professional experience in noticing when people are doing that. Whereas you, Dr Hale, have spent the last fifteen years with nothing whatsoever to lose." He smiled at Rask. "It's a rare quality, Minister. I'd use it while it lasts."

Rask looked at Gaunt for a long moment, then back at Cas.

"What do you need?" she said.

"Everything you've got. Starting with the last thing the Crown sent."

* * *

They gave him a chair and a screen and the Lattice woman — her name was Petra Imre, she'd sat behind him in second-year field theory and once lent him a pencil — brought him the telemetry without looking at him, and he went through it, and the room went on around him in a hush.

It didn't take long. That was the worst of it. Afterwards he'd think that if it had taken longer, if he'd had to hunt, he might have had time to get used to it on the way.

The drone logs first. At 04:40 they had sent a survey drone into the Works through Gate Nine. It had flown for forty minutes by the Collar's clock. When it came back, its own internal clock said eleven hours and six minutes had passed. Its battery was dead. Its camera had recorded, for most of those eleven hours, a corridor it had flown down and back up again, over and over, as the gravity inside shifted and threw it off course, and at the end, just before the battery died, a thin line of white-violet light crossing a hall far off, like a hair on a lens.

Then the Crown. The last telemetry burst before the link failed, 02:05:31. Most of it was screaming numbers — output, tensioner loads, core potential off every scale anyone had drawn. And one small, dry line at the bottom that the instruments computed automatically from the shape of the Lesion's boundary, a figure that had read *2.000* — a perfect sphere, a clean, smooth edge — every second of every day for ninety-one years.

It read *2.73*.

He looked at it for a while. He was aware of the hammer. He was aware of his own hands on the table, which were very still.

"What is it?" said Rask.

"The boundary dimension." He heard his voice from a small distance. "It's a measure of the — it's how rough the edge of the Lesion is. How much surface it has. A smooth sphere is two. A cloud is about two and a half. It's gone to two point seven."

"And that means?"

"It means the edge isn't an edge any more. It's fractal. It's broken into—" He moved his hand. He thought of frost on a pane. "Into edges, on edges, on edges. Infinitely. A boundary with no smooth part anywhere on it."

"Can it be repaired?"

"It isn't *damaged*, Minister. It's—" He stopped. He looked round the table. All those faces. They were looking at him the way people look at a doctor who has just come in from reading the scan.

"I need to explain something," he said, "and I need you to let me finish, and I need you not to call it anything else while I'm doing it."

Rask's face didn't move. "Go on."

"The Draw doesn't make energy. Everyone in this room knows that. It borrows it. The Lattice holds a region of space below its ground state — below zero, below empty — and the difference is what we sell. The vacuum lends it to us, and the vacuum always gets it back. That's not a theory, that's not Hollowell, that's field theory, it's as old as the Long Heat. You can make a patch of negative energy. You can't keep it. It has to be paid back by a pulse of positive energy, and the payment is always *more* than the loan. The longer you hold it, the bigger the payment. They called it quantum interest, before any of us were born."

"We are all aware of the principle of the Rollover, Dr Hale."

"Then you're aware that the Rollover isn't a solution. It's a refinancing." He heard his voice sharpen and didn't stop it. "Hollowell's trick was to keep borrowing to pay the interest on what we'd already borrowed. Push the payment forward. Push it forward again. Ninety-one years. Every year a little more of the output goes back into the Lattice just to keep the loan from coming due, and every year the loan is bigger, and every year we deepened the draw to make up the output, which made the loan bigger. We were feeding it seventy percent. The Review projected it would pass a hundred percent inside twenty years — the Draw would be eating more than it made, just to stand still. We'd have had to feed it from the grid. For ever. That was always the shape of it. That was the *best case*."

"That report," said Rask, very quietly, "was revised."

"Yes. By me." He held her eyes. "It didn't change the physics."

Nobody in the room was moving.

"The Rollover's gone," he said. "Six unseated, the Lattice lost the geometry, and the boundary broke. There's no mechanism holding the payment back any more. The debt's coming due. Ninety-one years of it, with interest, and the interest compounds. It will be paid in one pulse, at the core, all at once." He looked down at the screen, at the numbers, and did the arithmetic in his head because he had done it a hundred times, fifteen years ago, at night, on the backs of envelopes. "Order of ten to the twenty-six joules. The impact that killed the dinosaurs was about ten to the twenty-three and a half. So call it two or three hundred of those. At once. Here."

It was Petra Imre who spoke first. "When?"

"The boundary's still evolving. The clocks at the cordon are running faster; that tells me the core potential's still off the scale and still shifting. When it starts to fall, the debt's being called in. When it falls far enough—" He made himself say it plainly. "Two days. Three at the outside. Maybe less."

Someone at the far end of the table sat down on the floor.

"Evacuation," said a Works director, a heavy man called Brask whom Cas vaguely remembered as a rugby player. "We evacuate the city—"

"To where? Six hundred million people, in two days. And it wouldn't matter. It's not a bomb. It's the planet. The first second takes Averna. The shock and the heat do the rest. You could be on the far side of the world and it would find you in the first hour."

"Bury it," said somebody else. "Collapse the Pit. Pour—"

"You can't bury a debt."

"Then what," said Rask, "do you propose?"

And there it was. He'd known it was coming since the car. Maybe since 02:14. Maybe since Year Seventy-Six. He found that he was very calm.

"There's a mechanism in the Crown," he said. "Hollowell designed it. It's in the original schematics, and it's in her last paper — the one that was withdrawn — and it's called the Clause. It isn't a repair. It doesn't save the Draw. It ends it. The theory is that if the boundary ever failed badly enough — and it has — the same distortions that are going to kill us could be turned around and used to fold the core shut. Seal the whole thing. Debt and payment together. In a—" he hesitated over her phrase "—in a moment that doesn't have a future. I don't fully understand it. I don't think anyone alive does. There's a line on the Keystone plate that nobody's ever explained. *The sealing requires a witness.*"

"Hollowell's final paper," said Rask, "was withdrawn because it was unsound."

"It was withdrawn because it described how to turn the Draw off."

The silence went on for several strokes of the hammer.

"The Clause is locked," Cas said. "It takes the Minister's key."

For the first time, something flickered in Ondine Rask's face.

"The key," she said, "was in the custody of Deputy Minister Crane. Who is on site."

"On site."

"Deputy Minister Crane was attending the test. As the Ministry's representative." She said it perfectly evenly, and every engineer in the room looked at the table. "He is currently unaccounted for, along with the Crown crew and approximately nineteen hundred members of the night shift who were in the Lattice and the Vault at the time of the event."

Nineteen hundred.

Cas had been trying not to do the next piece of arithmetic. He did it anyway. He found that he was doing it out loud, softly, the way he used to talk himself through proofs in the library at night.

"The clocks at the cordon are twelve minutes fast after four hours," he said. "The drone lost eleven hours in forty minutes, in the outer Works. If the core potential is as high as the Crown's numbers say — and it'll have been highest at first, right after the rupture, before the debt started to be called — then in the Vault itself, near the core, it isn't hours. It's — the ratio could be tens of thousands. Hundreds of thousands." He looked up. "Minister, if anyone survived in there — if anyone was sheltered, if they had air and water — then in the four hours since two o'clock, they've lived—"

"That will do, Dr Hale."

"—years. Decades. By the time we get anyone in there, they could have been alive in there longer than the Commonweal has existed—"

"*That will not be repeated outside this room.*"

It was the first time she'd raised her voice. It rang off the gold walls. In the doorway, Gaunt had closed his eyes.

Rask straightened. She took her hands off the table.

"We have," she said, "three suits."

* * *

They were called keel suits. He had heard of them, the way you hear of the cathedrals in cities you'll never visit: a Lattice maintenance programme from the Year Seventies, cancelled in the Eighties, three prototypes built in the Tallow Works labs and never used. Suits designed to let a human being work inside the Lattice's gradients, where the pull of the tensioners and the push of the Lesion would crush a person like a paper cup. They were said to be almost unimaginably expensive. Each one had a sliver of keelstone at its heart, spun up like a gyroscope — keelstone, the hyperdense stuff the Lattice itself was made from, which cost more per gram than anything else on Earth because only the Draw could make it.

"They'll let a team get through the Works," Rask said. "Possibly into the Lattice. Possibly further. Our engineers believe that from the Crown, with the proper sequence, Tensioner Six could be reseated manually. If Six is reseated, the Lattice can re-establish the geometry. If the geometry is re-established, the Rollover can resume. The Draw continues." She said the last two words with a peculiar emphasis, as though laying them down on the table between them like a card. "Can Six be reseated from the Crown, Dr Hale?"

He thought of the number at the bottom of the telemetry. 2.73. He thought of a tensioner rotor the size of a building sitting off its bearing in a Lattice that no longer had a shape to hold it. He thought of the Keystone.

"Yes," he said.

It was astonishingly easy. It always had been. That was the thing about lies in the Commonweal: there was never any resistance. You opened your mouth and they slid out like a coin into a slot.

"Good." Rask nodded, once. "Then you'll lead the technical side. Civil Defence will lead the team. You'll leave as soon as the suits are ready." She turned her head. "Major Lisk."

He hadn't noticed the woman by the window. She'd been standing in the corner behind the delegate screens with her arms folded and her back to the mural, watching, and she came forward now with the slightly rolling gait of someone who had spent a long time on boats or in boots. She was in her fifties, broad and weathered, with cropped iron-grey hair and a face like a well-used tool. The Civil Defence badge on her shoulder had been sewn back on by hand at least once. The ribbon bar under it went down two rows.

"Teodora Lisk," she said, and held out her hand.

He shook it. It was a hard, dry hand with a missing fingertip.

She didn't let go at once. She held on a moment and looked at him — up, down, back up — with an expression he couldn't read. It was the look, he thought afterwards, that a person gives a ladder before climbing it: not unfriendly; simply trying to work out how much weight it would take.

"You're the one who wrote the thing," she said.

"I'm the one who wrote the thing."

"Hm." She let go. "Well. Congratulations on being right."

* * *

Later — an hour later, maybe two, after the plans and the arguments and a call to the Tallow Works that went on for forty minutes and ended with somebody in the lab crying — he found himself alone at the end of the corridor outside the crisis room, standing at a window.

The window looked south over the Pit. From up here he could see the Draw properly: the Collar, the dark jumble of the Works, the dome rising out of the middle like the back of some enormous animal asleep in the crater. The shimmer rose over it, vast and clear and trembling. The birds wheeled at its edge. The hammer came up through the floor and through the glass under his fingers, once a second, once a second, and he found he had started counting again without meaning to. Twenty-one thousand and something.

He thought about nineteen hundred people.

He thought about a pen. He could remember it exactly: it had been a Ministry pen, blue, with the Continuance flame on the clip. He'd noticed at the time that it was a good pen, that it wrote smoothly. That was the thing he remembered most about the most important moment of his life — that he had noticed the pen was nice.

He had never told anyone that. There'd never been anyone to tell.

Down in the Pit something shifted in the shimmer, a great slow ripple running upward like a wave through a curtain. The birds broke their circle and scattered and came back.

Two or three days. A pulse at the core. And three suits, and a lock that nobody had the key to, and a line on a brass plate that nobody understood.

He should be afraid. He knew that. He examined himself for fear, the way you'd pat your pockets for keys, and found — not nothing. Something. But what he mostly found, when he looked, was a kind of settling. A quietness. The way a room goes quiet when the last guest leaves.

*At last*, some part of him said. He didn't like the part of him that said it. He didn't argue with it either.

It was almost a relief.
