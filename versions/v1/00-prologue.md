title: Nine Minutes
label: Prologue

> The vacuum will lend to anyone. It is the most patient creditor in the universe. It has never once asked to be repaid on time.
> — Iris Hollowell, lecture notes, Year 3 of Continuance

At eight minutes to two in the morning on the eve of Continuance Day, Ruth Tally stood in the Crown of the Hollowell Draw and watched a man from the Ministry comb his hair in the reflection of the thing that powered the world.

The Crown was the highest room in the station, a lens-shaped gallery of black keelstone perched at the top of Spindle One, four kilometres above the floor of the Vault. Its window was a single curved sheet of armoured glass forty metres long, and through the window, above and a little ahead, hung the Lesion. It did not look like much. People who came up for the first time always said that. They expected fire, or a whirlpool, or some dreadful eye. What they got was a point of white-violet light so small and hard it seemed less like an object than a flaw in the glass — a nail driven through the night. You could not look at it for long. You could not quite look away from it either. The engineers had a saying about that, which was that the Lesion looked back, and they said it as a joke, mostly.

Deputy Minister Aldous Crane leaned close to his own reflection, which was superimposed on the Lesion's, and pressed a strand of silver hair into place with two fingers.

"Is it going to be too bright?" he asked the camera operator. "I don't want to squint. The Minister squinted in the Year Eighty footage and she's never forgiven the crew."

"We'll stop down," said the operator, a young woman with a shaved head and a Ministry lanyard. "You'll look like a painting, sir."

"A painting." Crane seemed to consider this and find it acceptable. On a chain around his neck, lying against his dark jacket like a pendant, was a key: a flat, heavy, blackish thing the length of a finger, cut from keelstone and stamped along its shaft. Ruth had seen it once before, in a photograph in the training manual. She had not expected to see it in her own control room, being worn as jewellery.

"Tally," said Crane, without turning round. "How long until we're ready?"

Ruth looked at the board. Her board: fourteen years she'd been in the Crown, six as shift engineer, and she could have read it with her eyes shut by the sound of the relays. The Lattice was at nominal. The Rollover was feeding at seventy-one percent, which was a number that would once have caused a scandal and now caused a memo. Tensioner Six was running a bearing temperature eleven degrees above the line, as it had been for three quarters, because its maintenance slot had been deferred three quarters running to keep output up for the export contracts.

"We're not," she said.

Now he turned.

He had a good face for the Commonweal: open, confident, a little lined in the right places, the kind of face that could say *there is no cause for concern* and make you feel ashamed for having been concerned. He smiled at her with it.

"Ruth. It is Ruth? Ruth, I understand. Believe me. I've read the protocol."

"Then you've read that the Deepening Test requires the Lattice interlocks to be taken offline for the duration."

"For eleven minutes."

"For eleven minutes during which, if anything happens to a tensioner, the Lattice can't compensate on its own. It's a test we run in the spring, with the full day crew, the Chief Engineer, and two spare tensioner rotors spun and waiting. Not at two in the morning with a night shift, the Chief on leave, and Six running hot."

"Six has been running hot since the summer," said Crane. "I've seen the reports too. And Six is still here."

Behind her, at the tensioner desk, Fen Marsh made a small noise that could have been a cough. Ruth didn't look round. Fen had two children and a mortgage on a Tier Twelve flat and had once, at a shift party, drunkenly explained to Ruth that he believed in the Continuance the way he believed in gravity: not with any enthusiasm, but because it was stupid not to.

"Deputy Minister," Ruth said, "respectfully. It's not my call. It's the Chief Engineer's call, and he's not here, so it goes to the Director of Operations, who is asleep in Tessarine and whom I tried to wake an hour ago."

"Director Obi has been informed," said Crane's aide, a narrow young man with a tablet, "and has deferred to the Ministry."

Ruth looked at him. He looked at the tablet.

"At dawn," Crane said, in the patient voice of a man explaining something to a child whom he likes, "the Minister of Continuance is going to stand in front of the Quota clock in Hollowell Square with a hundred thousand people and eleven heads of state watching and announce the Second Deepening. Thirty percent more output. Thirty percent, Ruth. Do you know what that means?"

"It means thirty percent more debt."

A pause, very slight.

"It means," said Crane, "that the Aurelian Compact signs the trunk-line extension, which means we power forty million homes that are currently burning coal in the dark. It means the Canopy stays up through the storm season. It means your daughter — Maddy, isn't it? — grows up in the safest, brightest city that has ever existed. And the Minister would like to show footage of the test, conducted tonight, live, successfully, from this room, by you, because a test is a promise and a promise is only worth something if you keep it." He smiled again. "She asked for you, you know. By name. She said, 'Tally's the best we have.' I'd hate to have to tell her she was wrong."

It was very gently done. That was the thing about the Commonweal: nobody ever raised their voice. Nobody ever said *or else*. They said your daughter's name, and they said it kindly, and they mentioned that Maddy's placement at the Upper Tier academy was, like all placements, subject to annual review by the Ministry, and they did not mention it at all, and you heard it anyway.

Ruth thought about Maddy, asleep in her mother's flat on Tier Nine with her feet out of the covers and the glow-in-the-dark fish her father had stuck to the ceiling before he left. She thought about the eleven degrees on Six. She thought: *it will probably be fine*. That was the sentence. That was the whole machine. Ninety-one years of it had run on that sentence. It will probably be fine.

"Eleven minutes," she said.

"Eleven minutes," said Crane, and patted her shoulder, and went to stand where the light was best.

* * *

They ran it by the book because there was nothing else to run it by.

"Crown to Lattice, stand by for Deepening protocol, test designation Continuance Ninety-One."

"Lattice standing by."

"Rollover to manual."

"Rollover to manual, confirm." Fen's voice. "Feeding at seventy-one, holding."

"Interlocks." Ruth's thumb hovered over the key switch. There was a moment when she could have not done it. She would remember that moment for the rest of her life, which would turn out to be a strange and very long life, in a place she could not at that moment have imagined; she would remember it the way you remember the last step before a stair you didn't know was missing. "Interlocks off."

"Interlocks off, confirm."

The Crown did not change. The window did not change. Down on the board, a row of green lights went amber, which meant only that the Lattice was now trusting its tensioners to hold themselves, the way a trapeze artist trusts her hands after the net's been taken away.

"Camera's rolling," said the operator softly.

"Commence Deepening. Draw to one-oh-five."

"One-oh-five."

It came up through the floor first. It always did. There was a note the Lesion made when it was being worked — not a sound exactly, more a pressure in the long bones — and every engineer in the Draw knew it and none of them had a good word for it. Ruth's grandmother, who had been Lattice crew in the first generation, used to call it *singing*. The Lesion was singing now, a long low swell that made the water in Dieter Hask's cup shiver into rings.

"One-ten."

"Output at sixty-eight terawatts," Dieter said from the output desk. "Sixty-nine."

"One-fifteen."

"Six is at plus fourteen," said Fen. "Plus fifteen."

"Noted."

"One-twenty."

"Seventy-two terawatts." Dieter's voice had gone very flat. "That's a record."

"Of course it is," murmured Crane, for the camera.

Out in the window, the Lesion had not grown, but it had sharpened; it had the look of something pulled taut. Ruth had seen it like this twice before, in spring tests, surrounded by the day crew with their coffee and their jokes and the Chief Engineer stalking up and down behind them humming. She had never seen it at night, with the Vault below them dark but for the work-lights on the spindles, eight black pillars rising out of the floor into the glare like the columns of a drowned temple.

"One-twenty-five."

"Six is plus nineteen."

"One-thirty."

"Plus twenty-two. Ruth."

"I see it."

"It's over the line."

"I see it, Fen. Holding at one-thirty. Deputy Minister, we're at the protocol maximum, we have your number, I'm bringing it down."

"Five more," said Crane.

She turned. He was not looking at her; he was looking at the camera, and his face was lit white-violet on one side, and he said it again, pleasantly, to the lens: "Five more, Ruth. Let's give them a round number. One-thirty-five."

"The protocol maximum is one-thirty."

"And the Minister has authorised one-thirty-five." His aide held up the tablet as if it were a holy relic. "It's in the order. Five more, and then you can bring it down, and we'll all go home and watch ourselves on the news."

Afterwards — and there would be a great deal of afterwards — Ruth would try to decide what she had been thinking when she said *one-thirty-five*. She would write it down in a logbook, in pencil, in a hand that got smaller as the years went on and the paper ran out, and she would never get it right. The truest version she ever wrote was this: *I thought that I was tired. I thought that I had been told. I thought it would probably be fine.*

"One-thirty-five," she said.

The clock on the wall of the Crown began to run fast.

* * *

It was an ordinary clock, an analogue one with a sweep hand, put up in the Crown by some engineer decades ago as a joke about the station's timekeeping. Ruth saw the sweep hand go round once in what felt like half a second. Then again. Then it was a blur.

"Six," said Fen. "Six is—"

The floor dropped. Not the floor: her weight. Everyone in the Crown staggered at once as though the room had fallen a metre and been caught. The camera operator went down on one knee. Dieter's cup went off the desk and did not fall. Ruth saw it. It left the edge of the desk and hung, tilting, a little brown tongue of coffee reaching out of it in a slow curl, and it went down through the air as if through honey, and it took so long to fall that she had time to look away from it and look back.

"Six has unseated," Fen said, and his voice came to her strangely, the words arriving a hair before she saw his mouth make them. "Six is off its bearing. Six is off. Lattice is—"

"Rollover to full," Ruth said. "Interlocks on. Interlocks *on*."

"Interlocks on." The amber row went green, and went amber again, and went red.

"Lattice not accepting."

"Draw to zero. Kill the Draw. *Kill it.*"

"Draw at zero." Dieter was hitting the panel with the flat of his hand. "Draw at zero, it's — output's still climbing, it's not us, it's not us drawing, it's coming out on its own—"

And out in the window, the Lesion broke.

She would try to describe it later, too, and fail. The nearest she came was this: it was like watching frost form on a pane of glass, if the frost were made of light, and if the pane were the world. The hard white point did not explode. It did not grow. Its *edge* — the edge that had always been a simple, perfect boundary — came apart into edges, and those edges into more edges, branching and branching into a filigree so fine and so endless that the eye slid off it and came back and slid off again. Lace. It spread outward in every direction at once, delicate, luminous, and it made no sound at all.

Everyone in the Crown had stopped moving, except that they had not stopped; they were moving at different speeds. She saw Crane's aide lift his tablet to his face in a single slow, graceful arc that took, perhaps, ten seconds. She saw the camera operator, on her knee by the window, flicker, and stand, and be on her knee again. She heard someone scream and then saw them open their mouth to do it.

*Time dilation*, said the calm part of Ruth's mind, which was the part that had passed the exams. *Local gradients across the room. The boundary's gone fractal. The core potential is spiking. We are in a region of—*

"Keystone," she said out loud, and ran.

* * *

The Keystone was set into the floor at the far end of the Crown, under a hinged plate of brushed steel that nobody ever opened. The plate had words on it. Every engineer who had ever worked the Crown knew the words by heart, the way you know the words on a gravestone you walk past every day, without ever really reading them.

:: HOLLOWELL CLAUSE
:: FOR USE IN CATASTROPHIC BOUNDARY FAILURE ONLY
:: THE SEALING REQUIRES A WITNESS

Nobody knew what the last line meant. The consensus in the canteen was that Iris Hollowell had been a genius and a mystic and had lived on coffee and cigarettes and that geniuses were allowed to be cryptic on brass plates. The Clause itself was classified above Ruth's level. What she knew was this: under the plate was a lock, and behind the lock was the only mechanism in the Draw that did not feed the Lesion but ended it, and the lock took one key, and the key was the Minister's, and tonight it was hanging around the neck of a man in a good jacket who had wanted a round number.

She dropped to her knees and wrenched the plate up. Beneath it, black keelstone, a smooth recessed housing, and in the middle of it a keyhole the shape of a narrow cross.

"Crane."

He was standing where he had been standing, in the best light. The light was different now. It came in through the window in a shifting white-violet surf, and it lit him from every side, and his face in it looked very young and very frightened.

"Give me the key."

"What will it do?"

"It will close the Lesion."

"Close it." He said it as if it were a word in a foreign language. "Close it how? For how long? We can't — Ruth, the Minister—"

"There won't be a Minister. There won't be a Draw. Look out of the window, Aldous. There won't be a *city*."

Something happened to his face then, and she thought later — much later, when she'd had more time than anyone should have to think about one face — that it was the moment he understood, and that what he understood was not that the world might end but that if he gave her the key, the thing he had been sent here to film would be the last day of the Hollowell Draw, and that it would be his name on it. She thought he was not a monster. She thought he was simply a man who had lived his whole life inside the Continuance and could not, in that second, imagine the outside of it. It is hard to give away the thing that holds up the sky.

"There must be another way," he said.

"*Give me the key.*"

He put his hand on it. He did. She saw his fingers close around the chain. She saw him start to lift it over his head.

The gantry beneath the window buckled.

A single thread of the Lace — as fine as a hair, as bright as a filament in a bulb — had reached the outer frame of the Crown. Where it touched, the keelstone did not break, because nothing broke keelstone. It *aged*. A strip of the frame as wide as her hand went grey, then white, then sagged like wax, and the grated walkway along the window, which was bolted to that frame, tore free along a third of its length and dropped half a metre with a shriek of metal.

Crane was standing on the walkway.

He fell against the rail. The chain around his neck caught on something — a bolt head, a twist of torn grating — and snapped. Ruth watched the key go. She watched it with the same terrible slowness as the coffee cup, and knew as she watched that it was not slow for the key; it was only slow for her. It turned over once in the white-violet light. It slid between the bars of the grating. And it fell — not down, toward the Vault floor, where things ought to fall — but *up and out*, toward the window, as if the Lesion's light were the true ground and it was dropping home. The glass did not stop it. She never understood that part. It was on one side of the window, and then it was beyond it, a small black speck tumbling into the Lace, and then it was not anywhere.

Crane made a sound she would hear for years.

She was still on her knees by the Keystone. She looked down, stupidly, at the empty keyhole. Then she looked up.

There was a man at the Keystone.

He was kneeling where she was kneeling. That was the only way she could put it afterwards. He was kneeling *where she was*, in the same place, and she could see him perfectly, and he was not touching her. He was dressed in close grey, like a diver, like something worn under armour, and he had one hand on a small wheel she had never seen — a wheel that was not there, that the housing did not have — and around his wrist was a cord tied in a knot. His face was turned toward the window. It was an ordinary face, tired and lined and unshaven, and it was completely calm. It was the calmest face she had ever seen. He looked like a man who had been there for a very long time and could stay there for longer.

Then he turned his head and looked at her.

It lasted — she never knew. Less than a second. He looked at her as if he knew her. As if he were sorry for her, and glad of her, both at once.

Then there was no one there.

* * *

"Tally! Tally, the lift!"

Fen had her by the arm. He had her by the arm, and then he had her by the arm again, a moment later, as if he'd had to do it twice. Everyone was moving now, in stutters, in slurs. The camera operator was dragging Crane back from the edge of the torn walkway; his face was grey and wet. Dieter had his board open and was yanking fuses out by hand as if that would do anything, as if anything would do anything.

"We have to go down," Fen said. "We have to go down *now*, the Lattice is — the whole — Ruth, if it touches the spindle—"

"Yes," she said. "Yes. Evacuate the Crown."

They went. Eleven people and a camera. The lift was at the end of the gallery, a steel cage on a rack-and-pinion rail that ran four kilometres down the inside of Spindle One, and it held twelve, and they got in, and Ruth did not. She stood at the gate.

"Ruth—"

"One second."

On the wall by the lift, behind a small glass panel, was a red lever with a black-and-yellow collar. Every room in the Draw that had controls also had one. The engineers called it the Dead Man's Hammer. Pulling it did only one thing: it released a flywheel in the station's deep structure that drove an iron hammer against a keelstone anvil, once per second, for as long as the flywheel turned — and the flywheel had been built to turn for days. It was the oldest alarm in the Draw, older than the electronics, older than the Ministry, a thing Iris Hollowell had insisted on over every objection. It meant one thing and one thing only, and every soul in the station would understand it, even if every screen and every speaker in the place was dead.

It meant: *nobody is at the controls*.

Ruth broke the glass with her elbow and pulled it.

For a moment nothing happened. Then, deep below them, somewhere down in the black body of the station, something enormous let go.

She stepped into the lift. Fen slammed the gate. The cage dropped.

Through the lift's mesh she saw the Crown go up and away from her — the torn walkway, the empty Keystone with its plate thrown back, the window full of spreading light — and then the black inner wall of the spindle, rushing. In her hand she was holding, she discovered, the station logbook, which she had no memory of picking up. She held it against her chest. Beside her, Aldous Crane was weeping silently with his eyes open. The camera operator had her arms around him. The young man with the tablet was staring at the tablet, which showed a clock, which was counting upwards faster than any clock should.

*Maddy*, thought Ruth. *Maddy, Maddy, Maddy.* She had the absurd conviction that if she said the name enough times, fast enough, she could make it reach her.

Then, from far below, up through the rails and the cage and the soles of her boots and the bones of her legs, came the first stroke of the hammer.

It was not a sound. It was too deep, and too long, and too slow, for a sound. It came up through the spindle like a tide coming in under a door. It lasted, and lasted, and lasted.

Afterwards — for the rest of her life, and for the lives of her children, and their children, and theirs — they would call it the Bell.

She looked at her watch. It said 02:14. She would find out, eventually, what it meant that she was the last person to see it say so. It had taken nine minutes.
