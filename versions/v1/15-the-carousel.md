title: The Carousel
---

> Crown to Lattice, stand by for Deepening protocol, test designation Continuance Ninety-One.
> — Ruth Tally, 01:58, the night of the Kindling (heard 175,000 times)

The spindles leaned together as they rose.

He hadn't seen it from the city — from the city they had looked like eight straight black pillars going up into the glare — but from the hut under the gallery he could see it plainly. They leaned in toward each other the way the poles of a tent lean in toward the ridge, so that at the Anvil they stood kilometres apart and up here, just below the Rim, they were close enough to be joined. And they were joined. Gantries ran between them: long, narrow, open walkways of steel lattice slung from spindle to spindle across the violet gulf, some of them intact, some of them sagging, some of them hanging in broken halves from one end like dead vines.

The gantry from Four to One was intact. Ione had checked it on her scouting trip. Three hundred metres of steel walkway, a metre wide, with a handrail on each side, sagging slightly in the middle, running from a door in the lattice just above the hut straight across the gulf to Spindle One.

And at its far end, wrapped round the outside of Spindle One like a ring on a finger, was the gallery.

He could see it from the gantry's start. A long, low, curving structure with a roof and windows, circling the spindle, its windows facing inward — toward the Lamp, toward the core. The windows were dark. And above it, not far above, the spindle ended in a shape he knew from the mural in the crisis room, from the old footage, from the textbooks: a lens of black keelstone perched on the tip of the pillar like a seed on a stalk. The Crown. And above the Crown, the Rim — the vast dark ring that ran round the Lamp, joining the tops of all eight spindles — and above the Rim, filling the whole sky, very close, so close he could see the finest of its threads moving, the Lace.

He was in the suit again. The keel hummed. It had dropped again. He'd tried not to listen to how much.

Ione stood beside him at the start of the gantry with her rope on her shoulder and her pack on her back, looking across at the gallery. She was very calm. She had been very calm all morning. She had eaten, and drunk, and re-bound her wrist, and checked his harness twice, and said almost nothing, and been very calm.

Her hands were shaking.

He saw it when she lifted one to point. A fine, fast tremor, like a leaf in a draught. She saw him see it, and looked at it herself, and put the hand down by her side, and closed it.

"It's the only way," she said. "The Lace has closed the other gantries. The gallery's the only way to the Rim, and the Rim's the only way to the Crown."

"I know."

"I'm just telling you."

"I know," he said. "Ione. I know."

She nodded. She didn't look at him. She stepped out onto the gantry.

* * *

It swayed. Every step, it swayed — not much, a slow gentle swing under their feet like a rope bridge — and the handrails creaked, and the violet gulf opened beneath them on both sides, four kilometres of it, down to the Anvil and the tiny still city at its heart. He didn't look down. He looked at Ione's back. She walked quickly, lightly, one hand on the rail, not looking down either.

Halfway across, she stopped.

"This is where I found it," she said.

He looked down at the walkway. Steel lattice, a metre wide, pitted with rust. There was nothing there. It was just a place.

"Right here." She touched the key at her throat. "I'd walked across here four thousand times. I knew every bolt." She pointed at one — a rusted bolt-head on the edge of the walkway, a little bigger than the others. "That one's loose. It rattles if you step on the plate next to it." She stepped on the plate next to it. It rattled. "Four thousand times. And one time I came across and there was a key lying here. Right here. Next to the loose bolt."

He looked at the place. It was just a place. A square of rusted steel lattice in the middle of a walkway in the middle of the air.

*Where it had never been, and then it was.*

"Come on," said Ione. And went on.

* * *

The gallery's door was open. It had always been open, she said. It had been open eleven years ago. It had been open for a hundred and sixty-eight years. It was a plain steel door in the gallery's outer wall with a faded sign above it — CROWN SUPPORT RING — AIR PLANT — AUTHORISED ONLY — and beyond it, darkness.

Ione stopped on the threshold.

She stood there for a long time. He didn't touch her. He stood behind her on the gantry and waited, and the gantry swayed, and the Lace hung overhead, and she stood in the doorway of the place where she had spent three years of her life, with her hands closed by her sides.

Then she said, low, "It'll come on when we're in. Don't be frightened of it. It can't see you."

And went in.

He followed.

It was a corridor. Long and curving, curving away to the left and right, following the round of the spindle; he could see about thirty metres each way before it bent out of sight. The outer wall, behind him, was plain steel. The inner wall — the one facing the spindle, facing the core — was windows. A long unbroken band of thick glass panes, floor to ceiling, one after another round the whole curve. And along the foot of the windows ran a trough. A long steel tank, knee high, full of water, full of something green and thick and softly glowing. The air-garden. It smelled of the sea, of the algae halls in the Works, of greenbread. The water in it was still. The green in it was alive.

The windows were dark. And then they weren't.

It came on the way a screen comes on. There was nothing, and then — all at once, all round the curve, in every pane — there was light. White-violet. Soft. Shifting. And in it, standing in the corridor, as solid as he was, as close as Ione—

People.

A room. He was standing in a room. He could see the walls of the corridor through it, the steel, the trough, the windows — but over them, *in* them, lit from inside like figures in a stained-glass window, there was a room. A long lens-shaped room with black walls and a great curved window, and consoles, and chairs, and people at the consoles. A young man with a mug. A woman on a stool by a camera with a shaved head and a lanyard. A thin young man holding a tablet. A heavy man at a board with his sleeves rolled up. Through the great window of the room, hanging in the dark, a single point of white-violet light, hard and perfect as a nail.

And in the middle of the room, combing his hair in the reflection of the window, a tall man with silver hair and a good dark jacket, with a key on a chain round his neck.

"Is it going to be too bright?" said Aldous Crane. "I don't want to squint."

The voice came from everywhere. From the windows, from the walls, from the air. It was thin and slightly hollow, like a voice in a recording, and perfectly clear.

Cas stood in the corridor of the gallery with his hand on the wall and couldn't move.

"We'll stop down," said the woman with the camera. "You'll look like a painting, sir."

And beside him, very softly, not looking at him, looking at the room — Ione said the next line. Her lips moved a half-second before the ghost's did.

"*Tally. How long until we're ready?*"

"Tally," said Aldous Crane, without turning round. "How long until we're ready?"

And at the board — right there, three metres from Cas, standing with her hands on the edge of the console and her back to him — a woman in a white coat, thirty-eight years old, with dark hair pinned up and a mole on her jaw below her left ear, said:

"We're not."

* * *

He watched it all.

He hadn't meant to. He'd meant to keep moving — to follow Ione, who'd begun, slowly, to walk along the curve of the corridor, through the ghost-room, her hand trailing on the steel of the outer wall. But he couldn't. He stood in the corridor with the light of the Crown moving over his visor and he watched it happen.

Crane, with his good face, being patient. Ruth, at the board, being tired. *The protocol requires the interlocks off.* The aide with his tablet. *Director Obi has deferred to the Ministry.* He watched Crane say Maddy's name. He watched Ruth's face when he said it. He watched her put her thumb to her mouth and bite the side of it, without knowing she was doing it, while Crane talked about the Aurelian Compact and forty million homes.

He watched her say *eleven minutes*.

He watched them run the test. The call and response. *Rollover to manual. Interlocks off.* The green lights going amber on the board — he could read the board, he could read every figure on it, clear as print, lit up in ghost-light in the corridor wall. *One-oh-five. One-ten.* The coffee in Dieter's mug shivering into rings. *Six is at plus nineteen.* The Lesion in the window, sharpening. Crane saying *five more* to the camera.

Beside him, Ione said, very softly, "Now."

And Ruth closed her eyes. For about half a second.

"One-thirty-five," she said.

The clock on the wall began to run fast.

He'd read it in pencil in a Log in the Tollers' tower. He'd read it in the telemetry on a screen in a room with gold carpet. It wasn't like reading it. The floor of the ghost-room dropped and everyone in it staggered, and the coffee mug left the desk and hung, and fell, slowly, slowly, its brown tongue of coffee curling out; and Fen at the tensioner board said *Six has unseated* a hair before his mouth moved; and in the great window of the ghost-room the Lesion *broke*.

He watched it happen. The frost. The lace. The single hard point of light coming apart into edges on edges on edges, spreading outward, silently, in every direction. He'd seen the Lace for weeks now, from the city, from the spindle, filling the sky. He'd never seen it be born. It was the most terrible thing he had ever seen, and it was beautiful, and it made no sound.

"Keystone," said Ruth Tally, and ran.

She ran right past him. Through him — he felt nothing; the light went over his visor and she was on the other side of him — and down the length of the ghost-room to its far end, where the corridor curved, and dropped to her knees and wrenched up a plate in the floor. He saw the plate. He saw the words on it, lit in ghost-light on the steel floor of the gallery, clear as a sign.

:: HOLLOWELL CLAUSE
:: FOR USE IN CATASTROPHIC BOUNDARY FAILURE ONLY
:: THE SEALING REQUIRES A WITNESS

He saw the keyhole under it: a narrow cross.

And he saw — with a lurch that went through his whole body, that made him put out his hand to the wall — the mechanism under the plate. The housing. The black keelstone, the recessed ring round the keyhole. The fittings. He'd seen them before. He'd seen them in a schematic, in the back of a withdrawn paper, in a library at night twenty years ago. He knew what they were for. He knew what they would do, when the lock turned. He understood — standing in the gallery with the ghost-light running over him — exactly how the Clause had been built, and why, and what it would take to run it.

Almost exactly.

*The sealing requires a witness.* He still didn't know what that meant. But he knew, now, where the witness would have to stand.

"Give me the key," said Ruth.

He watched Crane not give it to her. He watched Crane's face do the thing it did — he'd read about it in her Log, *the moment he understood*, and now he saw it, the fear coming into that good face, the young frightened man coming out from behind the Deputy Minister. He watched Crane's fingers close on the chain. Watched him begin, slowly, to lift it.

He watched the gantry go.

A single thread of the Lace — a hair of light — reached the frame of the ghost-Crown's great window, and where it touched, the black keelstone went grey, and white, and sagged like wax. The walkway tore. Crane fell against the rail. The chain snapped.

And the key fell.

Not down. He'd wondered about that since the Log. He'd wondered about it since the mural. It left Crane's chain and turned over once in the white-violet light and slid between the bars of the grating and fell *toward the window*. Toward the Lamp. Toward the light. Not down, the way things fell. Not up, the way the Lamp pushed. It fell *along* something — along a slope that wasn't in space at all. And he understood, watching it, the way he'd understood the mechanism: near the Tear, the gradient of time was so steep that it was steeper than the gradient of anything else. Things near it didn't fall down, or up. They fell *forward*. Into the future. Along the only slope that mattered. The key fell forward out of the Crown, through the glass as if the glass were a moment that had already passed, and into the Lace, and was gone.

He knew where it had gone. He didn't know where it had come out. He looked at the key round Ione's neck.

And at the far end of the ghost-room, on her knees by the Keystone, Ruth Tally looked up — at nothing. At an empty space in front of her, beside the open housing. She went completely still. Her face changed. Something in it opened. She looked at the empty space for about a second, as though someone were standing there, as though someone were looking back at her.

"She always does that," said Ione quietly, beside him. "Right there. At the Keystone, after the key goes. She looks at nothing like someone's there." She paused. "There's never anyone there. I used to stand in the place she's looking at. Hundreds of times. To see if it was me she saw." She shook her head slightly. "It wasn't."

The ghost-Ruth came back to herself. *Tally! Tally, the lift!* The Crown crew running. Ruth at the gate of the lift. The glass panel. The red lever.

She pulled it.

And the gallery windows went dark.

For about three seconds, there was nothing. The corridor. The steel. The trough of green. The dark windows. Ione beside him, breathing, and the keel's hum in his spine.

Then, all round the curve, the light came on again.

"Is it going to be too bright?" said Aldous Crane. "I don't want to squint."

* * *

They walked.

The corridor curved round the spindle. The ghost-Crown went with them. It didn't matter where you stood — the room was always there, in the windows, in the walls, laid over the gallery like a picture on glass. Nine minutes. *We're not.* *Eleven minutes.* *One-thirty-five.* The key falling. Ruth looking at nothing. The lever. Dark. *Is it going to be too bright?*

Ione walked a little ahead of him, one hand trailing on the outer wall, and he saw that she was not looking at the ghosts. She was looking at the wall.

The wall was covered in marks.

He saw them after the second cycle: scratched into the steel, all along the outer wall of the corridor, at about the height of a woman's shoulder. Fives. Strokes in fives, the fifth across the four, in rows. Row after row. They went along the wall as far as he could see in both directions, and when he looked up, they went up — above head height, up to the ceiling, where someone must have stood on the edge of the algae trough to reach. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Scratched with a nail, or a knife, or a rope-hook, into the steel. And every so often, among them, other marks. A word. A drawing. A face — a woman's face, with her hair pinned up and a mole below her ear, drawn very carefully in scratched lines, over and over, twenty times, fifty.

He stopped in front of one. It was very good. It had been done slowly, by someone with all the time in the world.

"You counted," he said.

"I'm a Toller." Ione didn't stop walking. "I told you." A pause. "There's a place further round where I stopped. For the months on the floor. There's a gap. You'll see it."

He saw it. A stretch of wall about two metres long, where the fives simply stopped — and then, beyond it, started again, in a different hand. Stronger. Neater. As if someone had got up off the floor one day and decided to learn everybody's name.

There was a pallet of old rags on the floor under the window there, flattened, dusty. There was a tin cup by the algae trough. There was a length of rope, knotted into something complicated — a net, a sling — hanging from a pipe. There was a heap of little stones, polished smooth, that must have come up in someone's pocket from the Anvil a long time ago, and been turned over and over in someone's fingers until they shone.

Ione walked past all of it without looking.

The corridor curved. Ahead of them, round the bend, he could see a door — the gallery's inner door, Ione had said, the one that led out onto the Rim. It was standing open. Beyond it, violet air, and the great dark curve of the Rim.

They walked toward it. It came closer. They reached it. Ione stopped, just short of the threshold, and he stopped beside her.

"Watch," she said.

She stepped through.

And the door they had come in by — the outer door, the plain steel door with CROWN SUPPORT RING above it — was in front of them. They were standing just inside it, looking out at the gantry to Spindle Four. The gallery curved away on either side.

"Is it going to be too bright?" said Aldous Crane. "I don't want to squint."

* * *

It took him eleven cycles to believe it.

He tried everything she'd tried. He knew she'd tried it all; he tried it anyway, because he had to, because he was a man who had to see a thing for himself before he could know it. He went through the inner door, and came in through the outer. He went through the outer door, onto the gantry, and walked twenty metres along it toward Spindle Four, and turned round, and came back — and the door he came back through was the inner one, the Rim door, and he was walking into the gallery from the other side. He went through a window. The panes were thick but one of them, down near the trough, was cracked, and he put his gauntlet through the crack and pulled out a piece and climbed through — and fell forward onto the floor of the gallery corridor, facing back the way he'd come, with the ghost-Crown lighting up around him. He tried the roof. There was a hatch. It opened onto the roof of the gallery, a curving strip of steel roof round the spindle's side, and he climbed out onto it and walked along it and came down through the hatch he'd gone up.

Every way out was a way in.

"It's a ring," he said. He was standing in the middle of the corridor. His voice sounded strange. "It's — the whole gallery's been wrapped. Topologically. Every boundary's been glued to another boundary. Space goes out one side and comes in the other." He looked at the light in the windows. "There's a filament. Through the gallery. Somewhere. The Tear's run a thread through here and the space round it's been — folded round it. Like a sleeve round an arm."

"Yes," said Ione. She was sitting on the edge of the algae trough, watching him. "I know."

"There'll be a seam. There has to be. Where the edges meet. Where it's been joined."

"Yes. There was one. There was one where the key came from. Where my — where the woman was standing." She looked down at her hands. "It's not there now. I've been looking for it for eleven cycles. I've been looking for anything. Anything different." She was quiet. "There isn't anything different. There's never anything different. That's the thing about it. That's the whole thing."

The ghost-Crown moved round them. *Five more, Ruth.*

"The seam was only there," said Ione, "because something came through it. The key came through it. The woman came through it. Something from outside came in, and where it came in, you could see the join. Like a thread pulled through cloth." Her voice was very even. "Nothing's come in. So there's no join to see. There's just the ring. And the light. And the nine minutes."

She put her hands on the edge of the trough, on either side of her, and held on to it.

"I'm going to sit down now," she said. "For a little while. I'm sorry."

* * *

He'd been afraid of it since the gantry. Since the doorway. Since he'd seen her hands shake.

She sat down on the floor. Right there, by the trough, with her back against the green-lit steel and her knees up, in the place where the scratched fives stopped. She put her arms round her knees. She put her forehead down on her arms. And the ghost-Crown moved over her — Ruth running for the Keystone right through the place where she sat, the key falling through her, the light going over her like water over a stone — and she didn't move.

He knelt down in front of her. The suit made it hard. He did it anyway.

"Ione."

She didn't move.

"Ione. Look at me."

"I can hear you," she said into her arms. Her voice was quite calm. "I'm not gone. I'm just — sitting. I'm going to sit for a bit. You can look for the seam. I'll be here. I'll just be here."

*Is it going to be too bright?*

He sat down beside her. He got the suit's back against the trough and his shoulder against her shoulder, the way she'd sat beside him in the hut, and he sat there.

The nine minutes went round. *We're not.* He counted them. He couldn't help it. Twelve. Fifteen. Twenty. He sat beside her and counted the cycles, and the light went over them, and she didn't move except to breathe. Once, round about the twenty-fifth, he felt her shoulders start to shake, very slightly, against his arm, and he put his arm round her, the suit's heavy arm, and she leaned into it and went still again.

He talked to her.

He didn't know what else to do. He talked about anything. He told her about Nell's ceiling, the glow-in-the-dark stars, the constellations put up with a chart and a ruler. He told her their names. Betelgeuse, which was red. Rigel, which was blue. Sirius, the brightest, which the old sailors called the Dog Star. Deneb. Vega. Fomalhaut, which was lonely, which sat by itself in a part of the sky with nothing else bright near it. He told her about rain — what it was like, standing in it, on the Tier Nine arterial, the summer the Canopy failed over the northern tiers for a week, when he was a boy; how it came down so hard it bounced; how warm it was. He told her about the sea. About Teo in the Tessarine underpass with twenty-three people on a rope. About Ben, and MINISTER →, and I HAVE HAD 8 BIRTHDAYS. About Adeyemi putting his palm flat on the walls of the Works and listening.

*One-thirty-five.*

"Keep talking," she said, once, into her arms. That was all. So he kept talking.

And while he talked, he thought.

He couldn't help that either. He thought about seams. About a thread of the Tear running through this gallery, and the space folded round it, and the place where the fold must be glued. He thought about how you'd find a seam in a thing you couldn't see, if nothing came through it. He thought about frost on a window. About a hair of light crossing a storeroom and branching into itself, the same pattern at every scale. About a man in a hall of algae with a tape measure and eleven years, measuring a filament's branches, month after month, finding the rule.

He stopped talking.

"Ione," he said.

She didn't answer.

He took the sketchbook out of the suit. He had to open the neck-ring to get at it, and he did, and the warm algae-smelling air came in, and he pulled out the black cloth-bound book and opened it on his knee, in the ghost-light, and turned the pages. Thirty pages of Ben. Grease-pencil diagrams copied in his own pencil and Teo's. The branching. The throats. *D = 2.73.* *IF THERE'S A SHEAR IT'LL HAVE SEAMS. GO THROUGH THE THROATS.*

"The fold follows the thread," he said. He wasn't sure if he was talking to her or to himself. "The space is folded round the filament. So the seam's where the filament forks. It has to be. Ben showed it. Where it branches — the throat between the branches — that's where the geometry's weakest. That's where it had to be glued." He turned a page. "And the branching's self-similar. Same ratio at every scale. So if I know where one fork is, I know where the next one has to be—"

He looked up. Round the gallery. At the ghost-light. At the key, falling through it, toward the window, toward the Lamp. At the thread of the Lace in the ghost-window, the first one, the one that touched the Crown's frame.

"It's in the window," he said. "The filament. The one that made this. It's the first thread. The one that touched the Crown. It's still here. It's *in the light*." He was on his knees now, the sketchbook in one hand. "Ione. Ione, you've watched that thread a hundred and seventy-five thousand times. You know it better than anyone alive. Where does it fork? The first fork. After it touches the frame. Where does it *branch*?"

She lifted her head.

Her face was wet. Her eyes were red. She looked at him for a moment as if she didn't know who he was. Then she looked past him, at the ghost-window, where the Crown was just beginning its fourth minute. *Rollover to manual.*

"Wait," she said. "It's not — it doesn't come for five minutes."

They waited. He knelt in front of her with the sketchbook open. She sat with her back against the trough, watching the window.

*One-thirty-five.*

The Lesion broke. The Lace was born. And a single thread of it — fine as a hair, bright as a bulb — came out of the frost and across the ghost-window and touched the frame.

"There," said Ione. She was on her knees too now. She was pointing. "*There.* It touches the frame there. And it goes along it, that way, for — about an arm. And then it splits. Two. There. And the left one goes up and the right one goes—" she stopped "—the right one goes toward the Keystone. Toward where Ruth is. It branches again just before it gets to her. I've watched it. I've watched it a thousand times, I used to lie on the floor and watch it — it branches just before it gets to her and one branch goes up and one goes—"

"Where does the one that goes up *go*?"

She turned her head, following it. Up. Out of the ghost-window, across the ghost-ceiling, over their heads. Her eyes moved along something he couldn't see.

"There," she said. She was looking at a place on the outer wall of the gallery, above the scratched fives, near the ceiling, on the curve of the corridor six metres away. "It goes there. It goes into the wall there. Every time."

He looked at the sketchbook. At the branching ratio. At the throats. Ben's writing, in his own pencil. *THE SEAM IS ALWAYS BETWEEN TWO BRANCHES. NEVER ON ONE.*

"Then the seam's between them," he said. "Between the branch that goes to Ruth and the one that goes into the wall. In the throat." He did it on the page, fast, the ratio, the angles. "It'll be — there." He pointed. A place in the middle of the corridor, a little way round the curve, at about head height, in empty air. "Somewhere there. Ione. You know what a seam looks like. You've seen one. Look."

She got up. She walked toward the place he'd pointed at. She walked slowly, the way she'd walked on the gantry, one hand out. The ghost-Crown moved round her. Ruth ran past her for the Keystone.

She stopped. Her head tilted.

"Oh," she said.

He came up beside her. He looked.

It was almost nothing. A shimmer. The faintest ripple in the air, like the edge of heat over a road, like the edge of the lee in Teo's booth. A vertical line of wrongness hanging in the middle of the corridor, a hand's width wide, the height of a person, that you would never have seen if you hadn't known exactly where to look. Through it, the corridor on the far side jumped very slightly, as if seen through a flaw in glass.

"That's it," said Ione. Her voice was very small. "That's what it looked like. When the woman was standing there. That's exactly what it looked like."

"Ben," said Cas.

"What?"

"Ben found it." He heard his own voice shaking. "Eleven years in a room with a tape measure. He found it."

Ione looked at him. Then at the shimmer. Then she held out her good hand, without looking, and he took it in his gauntlet, and they stepped through together.

* * *

They were on the Rim.

It was a walkway. A vast one — ten metres wide, steel plate on keelstone, with a rail on the inner side — running round the top of the Vault in a great dark circle, joining the tops of all eight spindles, curving away on both sides until it met itself far across the sky. Behind them, the gallery; its inner door stood open, and through it he could see the corridor, and the windows, and the ghost-light moving in them. *We're not.* Ahead of them, over the rail, the drop into the Vault — four kilometres of violet air — and across it, the Lamp.

It was very close. He'd never been this close. It hung out there in the middle of the air, the single hard point, too bright to look at, and from it, in every direction, the Lace came out — the endless branching filigree, filling the whole sky above them — and its lowest threads hung down, just beyond the rail, fine and bright and moving slowly, close enough that he could see them branching into each other at scales so small his eyes couldn't follow.

Above and to the left, a little way along the Rim, Spindle One rose up past the rail and ended in the Crown.

Ione let go of his hand.

She walked to the rail. She put both her hands on it, the good one and the splinted one. She stood there looking out at the Lamp. And then her knees went, and she sat down on the steel plate of the Rim with her back against the rail and her hands over her face, and she cried.

Not the way she'd cried in the hut. Not quiet. She cried the way a child cries — great gasping, heaving, ugly sobs that shook her whole body, that went on and on, that bent her over her knees. He'd never heard a sound like it. He sat down beside her on the steel plate and didn't touch her, because he didn't know if she'd want him to, and then touched her anyway, his gauntlet on her back, and she didn't push it away.

"I thought—" she said. She couldn't get it out. "I thought I'd never — I thought I'd never have to — see her again—"

"I know."

"Every time. Every *time*. She closes her eyes. And I sit next to her. And I think *don't*. And she—"

"I know."

"She was my *friend*," said Ione Tally, into her hands. "She was my best friend and I had to watch her do it a hundred and seventy-five thousand times and she never once heard me say don't."

He put his arms round her. She let him. She turned into the suit and put her face against the chestplate and held on to the harness and cried, and he held her, on the Rim, at the top of the world, with the Lamp hanging in the air in front of them and the Lace moving slowly overhead and the ghost-light still going round and round in the gallery behind them. *Is it going to be too bright?*

It went on for a long time.

When it was over, she sat up. She wiped her face with her good hand. She looked at the gallery door, and the light moving in it. She looked at it for a while.

Then she turned her back on it.

"There," she said, and her voice was hoarse, and steady. "That's done."

She got up. She looked along the Rim toward Spindle One, where it rose past the rail and ended in the Crown. And beyond it — between them and the Crown, hanging down across the Rim in long bright curtains, filling the walkway from rail to wall, moving slowly — the lowest threads of the Lace.

It was like a forest. A forest of frost. Of light. Thread on thread on thread, branching and branching, hanging down from the sky to the steel of the Rim and spreading across it in fine luminous roots, a hundred metres of it, between them and the Crown.

"That's the Lace," said Ione. "That's the bit that kills you."

He looked at it. He listened to the keel humming in his spine. It had dropped again. He knew exactly how much.

"Hold on to me," he said. "Whatever you see."
