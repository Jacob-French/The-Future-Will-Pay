---
title: The Crown
---

> I am sorry. There was no other way to build it.
> — Inscription, inner plate of the Keystone, signed I.H.

The Crown was full of snow.

That was what he thought, coming up through the hatch into the grey light: that it had snowed. That in the hundred and sixty-eight years since Ruth Tally and eleven others had run for the lift, snow had come into the highest room in the Draw and lain there, deep and soft and undisturbed, over everything.

It was dust. It lay over the floor ankle-deep, and it lay over the consoles and the chairs and the desks in thick grey drifts, and it lay along the top of the great curved window in a soft grey ridge. When they walked through it, it rose round their boots in slow clouds and hung in the air and didn't settle. The air was very still. It smelled of nothing. Of stone and age.

He'd seen the Crown in the ghost-light. He'd seen it in the old footage, in the mural in the crisis room. Crisp. White coats. The needle swinging over. He'd seen it thirty minutes ago — a hundred and sixty-eight years ago — full of people, full of light.

He stood in it now and didn't recognise it.

The consoles had furred. Their screens were grey and blind under the dust, their switches rusted into place, the plastic of their casings gone brittle and cracked and curled. Dieter's mug was on the desk where he'd left it, and when Cas brushed the dust off it, it was not a mug. It was a shape of mug, in stone — the coffee in it gone to a brown crust, the ceramic crazed all over with fine cracks, like something dug out of a tomb. The chairs had collapsed on themselves. A clipboard hung on a hook by the door, and the paper on it had gone to a grey lace that fell apart when he breathed on it.

The clock on the wall had stopped. Its hands had fused to its face. They pointed at a time that meant nothing — twenty past something, or twenty to — and would never point at anything else.

"It's old," said Ione. She was standing in the dust by the hatch with her arms wrapped round herself, looking round. "It's so *old*."

"Time's been going faster up here. Much faster." He looked at the window. "And going round. This whole room's been in the Fold since the Kindling. It's had—" he didn't know "—a very long time."

The window was the worst of it. The great curved sheet of armoured glass, forty metres long. Along its lower edge the walkway still hung where it had torn from its frame, sagging, twisted, half-fallen. The frame above it, where the first thread of the Lace had touched — the black keelstone that nothing could break — had gone white. A strip of it as wide as a hand, all the way along one section of the frame, gone white and soft and slumped like the wax on a candle. And through the glass, very close — so close that the Crown seemed to hang in it like a lantern hung in a tree — the Lace. Its threads came down past the window in bright curtains. One of them came *through*. He saw it and stopped. A single thread, fine as a hair, came in through the glass near the torn walkway as if the glass weren't there, and crossed the room at waist height, and went down into the floor near the far end of the gallery — near the place where, in the ghost-light, Ruth had knelt.

It moved, very slowly, the way the threads always moved. It didn't flicker. It was the most real thing in the room.

"Don't touch that," he said.

"No," said Ione.

And in the far end of the Crown, down by the thread, there was a faint light.

He'd thought it was the Lace, at first. It wasn't. It was the ghost-light — the same white-violet glow he'd seen going round in the gallery windows, but much fainter here, a thin shimmer in the dust-thick air, like the memory of a picture. The room's own light, from that night, still just barely hanging in it. He could see, if he looked, the faint shapes of people. Moving. Running. Faded almost to nothing.

At the far end of the room, by the Keystone, a woman in a white coat was on her knees in the dust, looking up.

* * *

The plate was still open.

It lay back on its hinge where Ruth had thrown it, a hundred and sixty-eight years ago, a square of brushed steel furred with dust. Cas knelt and wiped the dust off it with his gauntlet. The letters came up under his hand, engraved, clear.

:: HOLLOWELL CLAUSE
:: FOR USE IN CATASTROPHIC BOUNDARY FAILURE ONLY
:: THE SEALING REQUIRES A WITNESS

Under it, set into the floor, the housing. Black keelstone, smooth and round, the size of a dinner plate, with a recessed ring around its edge and, in the middle, a keyhole. A narrow cross.

Ione knelt beside him. She knelt exactly where the ghost of Ruth was kneeling — he saw her do it, saw the faint white shape of the woman in the lab coat overlap with her, the two of them in the same place for a moment, one real and one light, both looking at the lock — and Ione didn't flinch. She'd done it a hundred thousand times. She took the key off its cord.

She held it for a moment.

"Do you want to—" she said.

"No," said Cas. "It's yours."

She looked at him. Then she put the key into the lock.

It went in. It went in the way a thing goes into the place it was made for: smoothly, completely, with a small soft sound like a breath let out. The cross-shaped bit sank into the cross-shaped hole and seated. She put her fingers on the bow.

She turned it.

It was stiff. It didn't move, and then it did — a quarter turn, heavy, resisting, the way a lock resists that hasn't been turned for a hundred and sixty-eight years or ever — and then there was a click. Deep. Not in the lock. In the floor. In the whole Crown. It went down through the keelstone into Spindle One like the first stroke of the hammer going down through Tier Nine, and he felt it in his knees.

The housing opened.

The recessed ring round its edge split into segments — six of them, black keelstone petals — and drew back, sliding under the floor, like the iris of an eye opening. And under it, where the housing had been, there was a hollow in the floor. And in the hollow, there was the machine.

It was very simple. That was the first thing he saw. After a lifetime of the Draw, of its vast humming inhuman machinery, the rectifier halls and the tensioners the size of buildings, it was almost shocking how simple it was. A wheel: a small brass wheel set upright, about the size of a hand, finely toothed round its rim like the wheel of an old instrument, with a knurled grip. Beside it, a gauge: a round dial under glass, with a needle, and a single line drawn across its face from edge to edge, and a narrow band of colour on either side of the line. Beside that, a lever — red, with a black-and-yellow collar, exactly like the Dead Man's Hammer by the lift. And round the whole of it, set into the rim of the hollow, a ring. A dial. Engraved with figures, very small, going round.

And on the inside of the housing's lid — on the back of one of the six petals, which had folded up and back and now stood upright at the edge of the hollow like a little headstone — there was a second plate.

He read it. He read it on his knees in the dust with the thread of the Lace moving slowly a metre away and the ghost of Ruth Tally kneeling beside him looking up at nothing, and Ione beside her, and the keel humming low in his spine.

:: HOLLOWELL CLAUSE — PROCEDURE
::
:: 1. SET THE INTERVAL.
:: 2. ARM. THE LATTICE WILL RELEASE ITS LAST HOLD. THE DEBT WILL BE CALLED.
:: IT WILL BE PAID AT THE END OF THE INTERVAL.
:: 3. HOLD. AS THE PAYMENT GATHERS, ITS PHASE WILL WANDER.
:: KEEP THE NEEDLE ON THE LINE BY HAND. DO NOT LET IT CROSS.
:: 4. AT THE END OF THE INTERVAL THE FOLD WILL CLOSE UPON THE PAYMENT
:: AND UPON ALL THAT IS WITHIN THE CROWN.
:: IT WILL CLOSE INTO A SINGLE INSTANT.
:: NOTHING WILL PASS OUT OF IT. NOTHING WILL PASS INTO IT. IT WILL NOT END.
::
:: THE SEALING REQUIRES A WITNESS.
:: THE WITNESS DOES NOT RETURN.
::
:: I AM SORRY. THERE WAS NO OTHER WAY TO BUILD IT. — I.H.

He read it twice.

Then he sat back on his heels in the dust and didn't move.

* * *

"Tell me," said Ione.

She'd read it too. She was kneeling beside the hollow, looking at the plate, and her face was very calm, the way it had been calm all morning on Spindle Four, and her hands were in her lap, and they were perfectly steady. He noticed that. They weren't shaking now.

"Tell me what it does," she said. "Plainly. I've watched it for three years and I never knew. Tell me."

So he told her.

He did it plainly. The way he'd told her about the Lees, about the Shear, about the Bells. He'd spent his whole life learning to say complicated things so that they sounded simple, and then fifteen years saying simple things so that they sounded complicated, and he found now that the first skill had come back to him, all at once, like a language you'd spoken as a child.

"The debt's coming due anyway," he said. "You know that. The Lamp's going to pay it — all of it, all at once — when the Lace reaches the Rim. Forty rounds. Less. Whenever. We don't know exactly when. Nobody does." He touched the ring round the hollow. The dial. "This lets you choose when. You set the time. You pull the lever — that's *arming*. The Lattice lets go of the last thing it's holding. The loan gets called. And at the end of the time you set, the payment comes. Right here. At the Lamp."

"And then everything ends."

"No. That's the — that's what she built. That's the Clause." He looked at the thread of the Lace crossing the room. "When the Lamp broke — the night of the Kindling — it didn't just break. It broke *time*, up here. It made the Fold. The Lace, the Crown, the Carousel — you've seen it. Things going round. Keys falling forward. Arrows nobody drew. The Fold's full of loops. And Hollowell knew it would be. She knew that if it ever broke badly enough to kill us, it would break badly enough to do *that*. And she built this to use it."

He took a breath.

"At the moment the payment comes," he said, "the Fold closes. Round the Lamp. Round the Crown. Round the payment. It closes up into a loop. A loop in time. A loop so short it's — nothing. An instant. One instant, whose future is itself. The payment starts — the very first instant of it, the very first edge of the light — and then the loop closes, and that instant is joined to itself, end to beginning, and it's sealed. It can't go on to the next instant. There isn't a next instant. The debt's paid, and the payment never arrives. It's always just about to. For ever." He heard his voice go strange. "She built a sarcophagus. Out of a single moment."

Ione didn't move.

"And the rest of it?" she said. "Holdfast? Outside?"

"Outside the seal, everything goes back. The debt's inside the instant, and the loan's inside the instant, and they balance, and they're sealed. Nothing's pushing any more. The Lamp's gone. Time down there comes back to — ordinary. The same as Outside. The Shear goes. The Drop's a ladder." He looked at her. "It works, Ione. I think it works. I think she was right."

"*The sealing requires a witness*," said Ione.

"Yes."

"Tell me that. Plainly."

He looked at the gauge. At the needle, resting now at one side of the dial, and the line across its face, and the narrow coloured bands on either side.

"When you arm it," he said, "the payment starts to gather. At the core. And as it gathers, it — wanders. Its phase. It won't come in straight. It'll come in the way the lamp-weather came in the Breach, in gusts, in swings. And the seal has to close on it exactly. Exactly in phase. Or the loop won't close clean, and the payment gets out." He touched the brass wheel. "So someone has to keep the needle on the line. By hand. Watching it. Turning the wheel, a little this way, a little that, as it wanders. All the way to the end. Right up to the instant."

"Why a person?" said Ione. "Why not — tie it? Lock it?"

"Because it wanders. It won't sit still. You'd need something that could see it and think." He shook his head. "And nothing that thinks can live up here except a person. Not a machine. You've seen what the suit's electronics are doing. Up here, a machine's clocks come apart in a second. It has to be eyes. And a hand. And a head that can guess which way it'll go next." He looked at the plate. "That's the witness. That's all she meant. Somebody to *watch*."

"And the witness is inside the Crown," said Ione. "When it closes."

"Yes."

"*All that is within the Crown.*"

"Yes."

"*Into a single instant.* *It will not end.*"

"Yes."

She looked at him.

"And they're in it," she said. "The witness. For ever. In one instant. Living it."

"No," said Cas.

She blinked.

"Not living it," he said. "Not the way you mean. Not over and over. You told me. On the spindle." He found his voice was quite steady. "If a loop's really closed, there's nobody inside to be tired of it. It's just once. It's once, and it's true, and it stays true. It's only for ever for whoever's outside." He looked at the brass wheel. "It's not a prison. It's not the gallery. Whoever's in it doesn't go round. They're just — there. In one instant. Once. Exactly as they are." He paused. "It's only for ever from out here."

She didn't answer. She was looking at him with an expression he'd never seen on her face before and couldn't read at all.

He looked back at the plate.

*Default*, he thought. The word you couldn't say in the Commonweal. The word Rask had never said, that the Ministry had never said in ninety-one years, that he'd never once said out loud in his life.

"Default," he said. Out loud, in the Crown.

It sounded like a very small word.

He found that he felt calm. Entirely calm. Calmer than he had felt at the window in the Collar, calmer than he'd felt in fifteen years. Calmer than he could remember feeling. He'd come in here for this. He hadn't known what it was, and it was here, under a plate in the floor, and it was simple, and it was something he could do.

* * *

"Take the key out," he said. "We don't need it any more. It's done its—"

She was already reaching for it. It was still in the lock, in the middle of the hollow, standing up among the wheel and the gauge and the lever. She took hold of its bow and drew it out.

The thread of the Lace moved.

It had been lying across the room at waist height, fine as a hair, from the window to the floor. It had been lying there perfectly still except for its slow weed-in-a-current drift, a metre from the Keystone. And as Ione drew the key up out of the lock, the thread drifted — a few centimetres; a hand's width — and one of its finest branches, a filament so fine it was almost only a glitter, swung across the hollow.

It touched the key.

It was nothing. A brush. Less than a second. There was a sound, very faint, very high, like a fingernail on glass. And Ione's hand jerked, and the key came out of the lock, and she was holding it up in front of her, in the dust-thick air, staring at it.

Along the shaft of the key, from the bow almost to the bit, there was a line.

A fine bright line, scored into the black keelstone. Keelstone, that nothing could scratch. It shone faintly in the grey light, white-violet at first, then fading, as they watched, to a thin pale grey. A scratch. Straight, and very fine, and a little crooked at the end, where it ran off the edge of the shaft near the bit.

Ione made a sound.

She was staring at it. She turned the key over in her fingers — slowly, very slowly — and ran the ball of her thumb along the line. From the bow to the bit. And back. The way you'd trace a scar.

"It's always had this," she said.

He didn't understand, for a moment.

"This," said Ione. Her voice was very thin. "This scratch. It's always had it. Since the gantry. Since I picked it up. I've — every night, Cas. Every night for eleven years. I lie in the hammock and I hold it and I run my thumb along the scratch. Here. It's how I go to sleep." Her thumb moved along it. "It goes crooked at the end. Just here. I know it. I know exactly where it goes crooked. I've always wondered what did it. What could scratch keelstone." She looked up at him. Her face was white. "*This* did it. Just now. It's always had it because it's just got it."

He looked at the key in her hand. At the thin grey line along its shaft.

*The jinn must return exactly as it left.* The old paper. His professor, joking. *You'd have no way to know where it came from. Because it didn't come from anywhere.*

"It has to go back," he said.

Ione didn't move.

"The key," he said. "It came from here. It came out of the Fold, onto the gantry, in the Carousel. For you to find. And now it's — it's done. It's opened the lock. It's got its scratch. It's exactly as it was when you picked it up." He made himself say it. "It has to go back. To the gantry. Eleven years ago. For you to find it."

"There's no one else," said Ione. Very quietly. "Is there. To take it."

"No."

"Because I found it. So I have to leave it. So I can find it." Her thumb was still on the scratch. "It never came from anywhere."

"No."

"It was never *made*. It never—" She stopped. She closed her hand round the key. She held it against her chest, against her breastbone, the way she'd worn it on its cord for eleven years. "Cas. How? How do I—"

And then she stopped, and her head came up, and she turned — slowly — and looked down the length of the Crown, away from the Keystone, to the far end of the room. To the place where the floor stepped down, where a short steel stair went down into a lower space under the main deck.

"The service deck," she said.

* * *

She'd seen it from below. That was what she told him, going down the stair. For three years, from the gantry — every time she crossed it, four thousand times — she'd seen the underside of the Crown above her, the black keelstone lens on the top of Spindle One, and in its underside, a row of small round windows. Portholes. The service deck, under the main floor, where the Crown's machinery had been. And in one of the portholes, sometimes — not every cycle, but sometimes — there'd been a light.

"Not the ghost-light," she said. "Not the Crown's light, going round. A different light. Steady. Grey. Like daylight through a door." She was going down the stair ahead of him, one hand on the rail, the key clenched in the other. "I used to wonder what it was. I used to think it was someone up there. Watching." She stopped at the bottom. "It was this."

The service deck was low and cramped and full of dead machinery — pumps, cable trunks, a bank of rusted cabinets — and dust, the same deep grey dust, and on the far side, in the curved outer wall, a row of small round portholes looking down.

And in the middle of the deck, near the portholes, the air was wrong.

He saw it at once. He'd have seen it anywhere now. A shimmer. A vertical line of wrongness hanging in the air a little above the floor, the height of a person, a hand's width wide, like the seam in the Carousel — but brighter. Much brighter. It wasn't a ripple. It was a *door*. A tall narrow slot of grey light standing in the middle of the dead deck, as if someone had cut the air with a knife and there was a grey afternoon on the other side of it. Round its edges, very faintly, the finest threads of the Lace came and went.

And through it — when he stepped round to look through it — he saw the gantry.

It was below. The slot of light looked *down*, as if it were a window in a floor; through it he could see the long steel walkway from Four to One, slung across the violet gulf, a little way below them. Pitted with rust. Sagging slightly in the middle. Exactly as it had been an hour ago, when they'd crossed it.

Not exactly. He looked, and looked again. The rust was a little less. The handrail, which had been bent in one place near the middle, was straight.

And at the near end of it — where it met the curved wall of the gallery, the ring round Spindle One — the gallery's windows were lit. The ghost-light, going round.

"That's then," said Ione. She was standing beside him, looking down through the slot. Her voice was very calm. "That's the gantry. Then. Eleven years ago. When I was in there." She looked at the gallery's lit windows. "I'm in there. Right now. Then."

Neither of them said anything for a while.

"I don't know how long it stays open," he said at last. "It might close. It might—"

"I know." She looked down at the key in her hand. Then at him. "I'll be quick."

"Ione—"

"I know how it goes," she said. "I've always known how it goes. I just never knew it was me." She almost smiled. "I'll put it on the gantry. By the loose bolt. And I'll stand at the end, by the wall, where she saw me. Where I saw me. And she'll come across, and find it, and look up. And I'll turn away." She put her hand flat on his chestplate. "And then I'll come back. That's all. That's how it goes."

"How do you know you come back?"

She looked at him with those grey-green eyes.

"Because she found a seam," said Ione, "where the woman had been standing. And went through it. And got out." She took her hand off his chest. "I'll leave it open for her. On my way back."

She stepped into the light.

* * *

He waited.

It was seconds. He counted them — of course he counted them — standing in the dust of the service deck in the grey light from the slot, with the dead machinery all round him and the keel humming low in his spine. Eleven seconds. Twelve. He watched the slot. He couldn't see her through it. He could see the gantry below, and the gulf, and the lit windows of the gallery, and no one.

Twenty. Thirty.

He thought about a girl of seventeen lying on the floor of the gallery below, letting the nine minutes go through her. He thought about her getting up, one cycle, and deciding to learn their names. He thought about her crossing the gantry four thousand times, knowing every bolt.

Forty-one. Forty-two.

She came out of the light.

She came through it the way you'd come through a doorway in a high wind — stumbling, catching herself, her good hand out. And she was different. He saw it at once. It had been forty-two seconds and it had not been forty-two seconds for her; he could see it in her face. It was grey. Her eyes were red. Her lips were cracked, as though she hadn't drunk for hours. There was rust on her knees, on her hands — the gantry's rust — and dust in her hair, and she was shaking. Not a tremor. All of her.

Her hands were empty.

"Ione—"

She walked straight past him. She walked to the wall of the service deck, under the portholes, and put her hand on it, and stood there with her back to him, her head down, her other hand pressed against her mouth.

"How long?" he said.

"I don't know." Her voice came out muffled. "Hours. Hours. I had to wait. I put it down — by the bolt — and I went to the end, by the wall, and I waited. And she didn't come. And she didn't come. And the light went round and round in the windows and I could hear it, *is it going to be too bright*, and she didn't — and I thought, what if she doesn't, what if I've got it wrong, what if it's the wrong cycle, what if—"

She stopped.

"And then she came," said Ione.

He didn't move.

"She came out of the gallery door. Onto the gantry. And she was—" Ione's shoulders moved. "She was so *thin*, Cas. She was so thin. I didn't — I don't remember being that thin. Her arms were like sticks. Her hair was filthy, she'd been cutting it with a — with a bit of metal, you could see, it was all hacked. And she had the grey. Just starting. Just here." She touched her own temple, the white streak, without turning round. "She was walking with her head down. Like she'd walked across there four thousand times. Because she had."

Her voice broke. She waited. She went on.

"And she saw it. She stopped. She just — stopped. And stood there. She stood there for such a long time, looking at it. And then she crouched down and picked it up. And she looked at it in her hand. And she ran her thumb along it." Ione's own thumb moved, against the wall, along nothing. "Along the scratch. The first time. And then she looked up."

"And she saw you."

"And she saw me." Ione turned round then. Her face was wet and grey and he had never seen anything so tired. "And she said *Mam?*"

He couldn't speak.

"She said *Mam*," said Ione Tally. "Like a question. Like a little girl. *Mam?* And I stood there at the end of the gantry, and I wanted—" Her face twisted. "I wanted to say, *no, love, it's not Mam, it's you, it's you, you get out, you get out of here and you go home and Gran's waiting and you'll be all right, you'll be all right, you'll be—*"

She put both hands over her face.

"But I remembered," she said, through them. "I remembered she didn't say anything. The woman. She didn't say a word. She just turned away. I've remembered it for eleven years. So I knew. I knew I didn't. And I stood there, and I tried to make my mouth — and I couldn't. I don't know if I couldn't or if I just — didn't. I don't know. I don't *know*. I stood there and I let her think I was Mam. I let her think Mam had come up to find her. I let her run along the gantry toward me shouting *Mam* and I turned my back on her and I walked away."

He crossed the deck. He put his arms round her. She let him. She put her face against the chestplate of the suit, and he held her, and she shook.

"I left the seam open," she said, into the suit, after a long time. "Where I'd been standing. I felt it close behind me when I came through, but not all at once. Slowly. Like a wound. I think she'll — I know she'll get through. I know she does. I'm here." She laughed, a terrible small laugh. "I'm *here*. So she does."

"She does," he said. "You did."

"I let her think I was her mother."

"I know."

"She's going to go down the spindle," said Ione, "and look in a water-trough and see she's twenty, and she's going to spend eleven years thinking her mother came up to the top of the world to save her and then turned away. She's going to wonder, every night, why she turned away." Her voice was very quiet. "And it was me. It was always me."

He held her.

Behind them, the slot of grey light hung in the dead air of the service deck. As they stood there, it narrowed. Slowly, like a door swinging shut. A hand's width. A finger's. A line. And then only a faint shimmer, and the threads of the Lace coming and going round the place where it had been, and then nothing.

* * *

They went back up the stair into the Crown.

The hollow in the floor stood open. The machine in it: the small brass wheel, the gauge with its line, the red lever, the ring of the dial. The petal of keelstone standing upright at its edge with the plate on it. Beside it, the faint ghost of Ruth Tally, on her knees in the dust, looking up at nothing. At the place beside the Keystone, where someone would stand to reach the wheel.

Ione went and stood by the hollow. She looked down at the plate. She read it again. He could see her lips move.

*The sealing requires a witness.*

*The witness does not return.*

Then she looked up. Not at him.

At the suit.

He was standing in it, in the middle of the Crown, in the dust, with the keel humming low in his spine. And she looked at it — at the grey plates, the gouge down the left side, the housing on his back with its band of lights and its white painted *1*, the rigger's harness she'd spent three nights splicing — the way you look at a thing you've seen before, a long time ago, from very far away.

He looked at it too. He looked down at his own gauntlets. At the chestplate. At himself.

Neither of them said anything at all.
