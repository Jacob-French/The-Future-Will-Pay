title: The Lees
part: Part Three · The Ascent

> Chalk falls true in a Lee. Chalk falls crooked out of one. When chalk falls up, pray.
> — Riggers' saying, Holdfast

For the first kilometre, it was only climbing.

That was how Ione put it, at the bottom, tying him in. *The first kilometre's only climbing. After that it's the Lees.* And he'd nodded as if he understood, the way he'd nodded at so much in Holdfast, and found out what it meant by doing it.

Spindle Four was hollow. From the city it looked like a solid black tower, but from inside it was a cage — a vast open lattice of keelstone struts, a hundred metres across at the base, rising around them in a great latticed tube, with the violet light of the Lamp pouring in through every gap in a million bright slanting bars. Up the inside of the lattice, clinging to it, ran the old maintenance ways: iron ladders and iron stairs and narrow steel walkways, bolted to the struts, zigzagging up and up the inside of the tube in flights and landings and switchbacks until they vanished into the glare. The lift's rack-rail had run up the centre once. Its stump still stood there, a twisted black mast rising out of the wreckage at the bottom and ending, ragged, a few hundred metres up, where it had snapped.

They climbed the stairs. Where there were no stairs, they climbed the ladders. Where the ladders had rusted through or fallen away — and there were many places where they had — Ione went up the bare lattice itself, finding holds in the struts with her hands and her boots, her splinted wrist held a little stiffly, the rope paying out behind her from the coil on her shoulder. When she reached the next walkway she'd tie off and call down, and he'd come up after her on the rope. The suit's exo did most of it. The keel hummed. The harness she'd spliced round his hardpoints held.

It was only climbing. It was the hardest thing he'd ever done.

Because he got heavier.

He felt it begin at about three hundred metres: not a sudden thing, not a gust or a shear like the Works, but a slow, patient, steady increase, as though someone were adding sand to his pockets a grain at a time. At five hundred, the exo's whine had changed pitch. At seven hundred, he noticed his breathing — not from effort, but from weight, the sheer work of lifting his own ribs. The suit's display, when he looked, said LOCAL G: 1.8. At the bottom of the spindle, in Holdfast, it had said 1.4.

"You'll weigh twice what you weigh in the city by the first kilometre," Ione said, when he told her. She was sitting on a walkway above him, eating greenbread, her legs dangling over the drop. "Three times by the second. By the Rim — I don't know. I wasn't counting."

"How do *you* do it?"

She looked at him with mild surprise. "I was born to it," she said. "We all were. Holdfast's heavy. I've been climbing spindles since I was nine." She considered. "And I'm small. The small ones do better. Less to lift."

She held her hand out flat, palm up, over the edge of the walkway. There was a little heap of white chalk in it. She blew on it, gently, and the chalk went off her palm in a puff, and drifted, and fell. He watched it. It fell straight down, in a thin white curtain, all the way into the bars of violet light below until he lost it.

"That's the last true chalk for a while," she said.

* * *

At a kilometre, the Lees began.

He understood what she'd meant, then, about the chalk. The Lamp's push, which had been growing steadily as they climbed, began to *vary*. You'd climb a flight of stairs, heavier and heavier, and come out onto a landing near one of the great keelstone struts — the huge black beams that braced the lattice, some of them five or ten metres thick — and suddenly the weight would ease. Not all of it. Some. As though a hand pressing on your shoulders had lifted a little. And then you'd step away from the strut and it would come back.

"That's the strut pulling," said Ione. "Against the Lamp. It's like the Anvil. It's keelstone, it pulls. Near it, the pull and the push fight. You weigh less." She blew chalk. It went off her palm and drifted — not down, but down and *sideways*, toward the strut, in a long slanting curve. "See? It's pulling the chalk, too. Crooked chalk. That's a Lee. A little one."

The Lees were how you climbed a spindle. He learned them over the next three rounds, or what would have been three rounds, down in the city. There were little ones by every strut, where you could stand and breathe. There were big ones — rare, precious — where two or three struts came together, where the pulls added up and cancelled the Lamp's push almost completely, where the chalk fell so crooked that it hung in the air, turning slowly, and you could lie down on the walkway and feel as light as you'd felt in the city. Those were where you rested. Those were where you slept. The riggers had built little caches in the big ones, generations ago: a platform of planks, a water-catcher of canvas, a box of stores bolted to the strut. Most of them had rotted away. Ione knew the ones that hadn't.

And the Lees moved. That was the thing that had sent her up alone, those three rounds before the Kindling festival, when he'd sat on the steps of the Steward's House blessing ropes and not hearing a word anyone said to him. The Lamp was getting weaker — the Lightening, the debt coming due — and as it weakened, the balance between its push and the struts' pull shifted. Lees she'd climbed through at seventeen had closed up. New ones had opened. Some had drifted a few metres up a strut. Some had split in two. She'd found them all, on that trip, by chalk and plumb bob and feel, and marked them. As they climbed he saw her marks: little white arrows scratched into the black keelstone with a nail, and numbers, and here and there a word. *GOOD. SMALL. BAD — GO RIGHT. SLEEP.*

"You did all this in three rounds?" he said.

"Four." She didn't look round. "And I was a lot faster without you."

He laughed. It came out of him before he could stop it, a real laugh, surprised, loud in the suit, and he heard it echo round the inside of the spindle, and she glanced down at him from the ladder above with an expression he couldn't read.

"What?" he said.

"Nothing." She went on climbing. "You don't do that much."

"Laugh?"

"Mm."

He thought about it. He thought about the canteen in the Collar and the tea that was mostly a structural material and Teo saying *Sounds like my marriage*. He thought about the gallery floor and MINISTER →.

"I'm getting better," he said.

* * *

On the second round of the climb — the second time they slept — he looked down.

They were at two kilometres. They'd stopped in a big Lee, one of the rigger caches, a platform of old planks lashed to the junction of three struts, with a canvas water-catcher and a steel box of rotted stores and a view straight down through the open lattice of the spindle to the city. He was lying on his back on the platform, in the suit, feeling almost light for the first time in a round, and he rolled over onto his front and looked down through the gap between the planks.

Holdfast was very small.

It lay at the bottom of the world, a tiny intricate patch of scaffold and container and keelstone spread across the black disc of the Anvil, the other seven spindles rising out of it like the pillars of a temple seen from its roof. The Commons was a speck. The Drop District was a smudge at the edge. Smoke went up from it in threads so fine he could hardly see them.

And as he watched, it began to go dark.

It started at the centre — at the Steward's House, at the foot of Spindle One — and went outward. A wave. A sort of dimming, a darkening, spreading out across the city from the middle to the edges. The shutters, he realised. The shutters coming down at the end of second shift. The night horn — he couldn't hear it; he was too high, too far — and ten thousand shutters closing.

But it was slow. He'd stood in the lanes and heard that wave go past, the clatter and bang of it; it took a minute, perhaps, to cross the whole city. From here it crept. It spread across Holdfast the way ink spreads into wet paper, lane by lane, so slowly that he could watch a single street go dark, house by house, and count his breaths between the houses. He lay there on the planks and watched it for what felt like a quarter of an hour before it reached the edges.

"What—"

"Watch the smoke," said Ione. She'd rolled over beside him on the planks and was looking down too, her chin on her folded arms.

He watched the smoke. The thin grey threads going up from the city's chimneys. They weren't drifting. They should have been — there was always some air moving, down there, the Lamp's heat stirring it — and they weren't. They stood up out of the chimneys like wire. Like grey hairs. Perfectly still.

"We're faster than them," said Ione. "Up here. The higher you go, the faster you live. Down there, that's a minute. Up here, it's—" she shrugged "—longer. And it gets longer. By the top of the Lees you'll look down and they'll be a picture. Nothing moving at all. We'll sleep a whole sleep and wake up and they'll still be in the same night." She blew a little puff of chalk out over the edge and watched it drift away sideways, turning. "We can get to the top and back, if we're quick, before they've finished one round."

"It's holding its breath," said Cas.

"Yes." She sounded pleased that he'd seen it. "That's what I thought. When I was seventeen. I lay here — right here, this cache — and looked down at it and thought, *the city's holding its breath*. And I thought: if I stayed up here, if I stayed long enough, high enough, the whole of Holdfast would stand still for me. Nobody would grow up. Nobody else would go down a well. I could keep them all exactly as they were, as long as I liked." She was quiet. "I didn't, though. I went on up."

He looked at her lying beside him, her chin on her arms, looking down at the still city. At the white streak in her hair, bright in the Lamp's light. At the stitches over her eye.

"Why?" he said. "At seventeen. Why did you go up?"

She didn't answer for a while. Down below, the city went dark.

"I'll tell you," she said. "Later. When we're higher. It's easier to tell higher up." She rolled onto her back and put her arm over her eyes. "Sleep. We've got a long way."

* * *

Teo's last message arrived on the third round.

The suit began to groan in the middle of a climb — a long, low, falling note from the helmet speaker, so deep it was almost below hearing, so slow it didn't seem to move at all — and he stopped on the ladder and hung there, listening, until Ione called down *what is it* and he said *Teo* and she came back down to him and they climbed on together to the next Lee and sat there, and waited.

It went on for a long time. Much longer than the others. They were higher now, living faster, and her minute had to stretch further to reach them. It groaned on through half a round, a vast slow wordless sound filling the lattice around them like the voice of the spindle itself, and he sat on the planks with his back against a strut and his knees up and listened to every moment of it, and Ione sat beside him and didn't say anything.

Then *ding*.

He switched it to the suit's outer speaker so that Ione could hear.

"Right," said Teo Lisk. "Last one. I know it's the last one. You said the electronics would go and I believe you, you're the sort of pessimist who's usually right." A breath. "Got your — got all of it. The Commons. The tank. Your old man with the lamp. The lamps in the lanes." Her voice caught and went on. "The forty-seven. I'm sorry, Cas. I'm so sorry. I know what you're doing with that, right now, wherever you are. I can hear you doing it from here. Stop it."

A pause.

"I've got Nell's file. Three weeks of you. I'm going to give it to her myself. By hand. I'll go to Tier Thirty and I'll find her in her gym with her lizard and I'll sit with her while she listens, and if she wants to cry I'll let her, and if she wants to hit me I'll let her do that too. You've got my word. And I'll tell Jonah about the dog. I'll get him the bloody dog myself.

"And you look after that rigger. The one with the white streak. You never said much about her, but you didn't have to, you said her name forty times in three weeks. It sounds like nobody's looked after her in a long while. Somebody should."

Beside him, Ione went very still.

"You owe me a drink," said Teo. "A large one. Don't forget. I've written my boys' names on the window, here, where I can see them from the chair. Mattias and Joss. Mattias is on shift at Kovač, holding up the grid. Of course he is. Joss is fine. I've written Ben's under. And the lady's — Seren's. And I've written yours. I hope you don't mind. It seemed right."

A long pause. He could hear her breathe.

"Guilt's a lousy reason, Cas. I said it at the booth and I'm saying it again because you're the kind of thick-headed bastard who needs to hear things twice. Don't you dare go up there and do something noble because you think you owe it. Do it because it's useful. Or because you want to. Or because *she* wants you to. Anything. Anything but that."

A breath.

"I'm fine. The lee's fine. I'll be here." A pause. "Bring them out, Cas."

*Ding*.

The suit hissed. The display at the bottom of his visor flickered, and showed a line of text — SIGNAL LOST — and flickered again, and showed nothing. The little icon for the radio went grey.

He sat on the planks with his back against the strut. Ione sat beside him. Neither of them spoke. Far down through the lattice, the city lay in its night, the smoke standing up from it like wire.

"She sounds," said Ione eventually, "like somebody I'd like."

"You would."

"*The rigger with the white streak.*" She said it slowly, as if trying the words for size. Then she looked at him sideways. "Forty times?"

He felt his face go hot inside the helmet.

"She was exaggerating," he said.

"Mm," said Ione, and looked away, down at the sleeping city. But he saw the corner of her mouth, and it had moved.

* * *

On the fourth round they felt the last Bell.

They were at nearly three kilometres. He weighed — the display said, before the display began to lie to him — two point nine times what he'd weighed on the Tier Nine arterial, a thousand years ago, a day and a half ago. The exo was screaming. They moved from Lee to Lee now in short, brutal dashes, never more than a few dozen metres between them, Ione going first and then hauling him, both of them resting in every crooked-chalk pocket they could find, sometimes for an hour, before the next. Below them the city had stopped. It lay in its shuttered dusk like a painting of itself, the same night it had been in when they'd looked down from two kilometres, the smoke from its chimneys standing up in fine grey threads that didn't move at all.

The Bell came up the spindle.

It came so slowly that at first he didn't know it for what it was. A pressure. A faint rising hum in the strut under his hand, very deep, like a tide coming in under a pier. It rose. And rose. It took so long to rise that he had time to notice it, and wonder, and understand, and turn to look at Ione. She had her palm flat against the strut, and her eyes closed.

It went on rising. Minutes. The whole spindle was humming with it, every strut, the walkways vibrating faintly under them, a vast slow note going through the lattice like a wind through a forest. It reached its height. It held there. It began, so slowly he couldn't be sure of it for a long time, to fall.

It lasted, he guessed, about a quarter of an hour.

When it had finally gone, Ione took her hand off the strut and opened her eyes.

"One hundred and twenty-two thousand, nine hundred and thirty-one," she said.

"You've been counting?"

"I'm a Toller." She said it as though it were obvious. "I've always counted. Even when they threw me out." She looked down at the pulsing city. "That's the last one we'll hear."

"The last—"

"Up here, they get too slow. The next one will take a round to rise and a round to fall. You won't be able to tell it from the spindle breathing." She looked at him. "That was the last Bell. For us. Unless we come down."

He looked at her.

*Unless we come down.*

Neither of them pursued it.

* * *

That night — that sleep; there were no nights, up here — they stopped in the best Lee on the spindle.

It was at the junction of four great struts, three kilometres up, where the lattice narrowed and the black beams came together from four directions in a vast knot of keelstone, and between them there was a pocket of calm so deep that Ione's chalk, when she blew it, didn't fall at all. It hung in front of her face in a small white cloud, turning, glittering in the violet light, like dust in a sunbeam. There was a cache: a platform of planks, a water-catcher with water in it, a box. And it was so light — so wonderfully, dizzyingly light — that when he stepped onto the platform he nearly fell, overbalancing, his body braced for a weight that wasn't there.

He took the helmet off. He hadn't taken it off for four rounds. The air was thin and warm and smelled of hot stone and chalk. He sat on the planks, in the suit, with the helmet in his lap, and put his head back against the strut, and breathed.

Ione sat opposite him with her back against another strut. She'd taken off her harness and her boots. She'd unwrapped the splint on her wrist and was flexing it, slowly, wincing. Above them, through the narrowing lattice, the Lace hung in the sky — very close now, much closer than from the city, its fine bright threads hanging down in long trailing streamers toward the spindle-tops, moving very slowly, like weed in a current. Below them the city lay frozen in the middle of its night.

"I was seventeen," said Ione.

He didn't move. He didn't want to startle it.

"My mother had gone down the Drop two years before. I'd held the ladder for her, when she painted the wall. They didn't give me to the Drop. They said I was a child." She flexed her wrist. "I think they thought I'd be quieter, without her. I was. For two years I was very quiet. I counted Bells. I worked the ropes. I didn't talk to anyone except Gran."

A pause.

"And then one round I went up. I didn't tell anyone. I didn't decide to, really. I just went. I told myself I was going to find the edge of the world. Where the Lamp stopped and something else started. I thought if I went high enough I'd see over it. Over the Rim. Into whatever was on the other side." She almost smiled. "I was seventeen. And I think—" She stopped. "I think I thought I might find her. Up there. I knew she'd gone *down*. I watched her go down. But I thought — if everything goes round, up there, the way people said — if the Lamp was a place where things went round—"

She didn't finish.

"I climbed this spindle," she said. "I didn't know the Lees then, not the high ones. I found them. I nearly died four times. I got to the top of the Lees — about half a kilometre above us — and there's a gallery there. Round the outside of Spindle One, near the top. A walkway, a ring, going right round the spindle, with a roof over it and windows. The old crews used to use it. It had an air-garden in it — a long tank of algae all the way round, to keep the air fresh for the Crown. And a gantry, across from Four to One. I went across the gantry. I went into the gallery." She looked at the chalk hanging in the air in front of her. "And the light came on."

"The light."

"The Crown's light. The night of the Kindling. It's — it's still there. In that gallery. You'll see. It goes round and round. The light from those nine minutes. It's trapped. It goes round the spindle in the gallery windows and never gets out. And when you're in it, you're—" she searched "—you're *in the Crown*. On the night. You can see it all. The people. The Crown crew, in their white coats, at their boards. The Lamp in the window, before it broke. Founder Crane with his key on its chain and his hair all combed. Ruth." She said the name gently. "Ruth at the board. I didn't know who any of them were. Not then. I thought they were ghosts. They were all lit up, sort of, from inside. You could see through them. You could walk through them."

"And then?"

"And then it all happens. Six goes. The Lamp breaks. The walkway goes. The key falls. Ruth pulls the hammer. They all run for the lift. Nine minutes." She paused. "And then it starts again."

The chalk turned slowly in the air between them.

"I tried to leave. Of course I did. I walked out of the gallery the way I'd come in. And I walked straight back into the gallery, at the other end, where I'd started. As if the gallery was a ring and I'd just gone round it. Every door. Every window. The gantry back to Four — I walked across it and I was coming back across it the other way, into the gallery. Everything led back in." She said it very plainly. "And the light started again. Ruth at the board. *Crown to Lattice, stand by for Deepening protocol.* Nine minutes. And again. And again."

"How long?"

"Three years."

He'd known. She'd told him, in the Tower, that first night. It was different, hearing her say it here, at the top of the world, with the chalk hanging in the air.

"Three years," he said.

"I counted." She almost smiled again. "Of course I counted. A hundred and seventy-five thousand, two hundred and — some. Nine minutes each. I lost count a few times. There were a few months when I didn't count anything." She looked at her wrist, flexing it. "There were a few months when I didn't do anything at all but lie on the floor of the gallery and let them happen through me. Ruth walked through me. Crane walked through me. The key fell through me. Again and again and again and I lay there and let it."

"How did you — food? Water? For three years?"

"The air-garden." She glanced at him. "Algae. And the gallery had a condensate trap for the Crown's water. I lived on algae and condensation for three years." A pause. "You'd have liked that. Your friend. The one in the hall."

"Ben."

"Ben," she agreed. "He'd have understood."

He couldn't answer.

* * *

"It wasn't a time loop," said Ione.

She said it later, after a long silence, when the chalk had drifted a little way off and hung near the strut.

"Not for me. I thought it was, for years. I thought I was going round and round in time. But I wasn't. I got older. My hair—" she touched the white streak "—that happened in there. My body went on. I remembered everything. Every cycle, I remembered every cycle before it. I was living forward. The whole time. Just — in a place that went round." She looked at him. "It was *them* that went round. The ghosts. The light. They were the loop. Not me."

"The light's on closed paths," said Cas slowly. "Round the spindle. Trapped. And the gallery—"

"The gallery joins up with itself. You walk out and you walk in." She nodded. "Yes. That's it. That's what I worked out, eventually. I was the only thing in there that was going *forward*. Everything else was going round."

"That's worse," he said.

"Yes." She didn't look away. "That's what I learned. That's the thing I learned in there, in three years, that nobody in Holdfast would ever believe." She held his eyes. "Ruth lived those nine minutes once. Just once. In the Crown, on the night of the Kindling. It was the worst nine minutes of her life and she lived them one time and then she went down the lift and had a whole life after, and Tomas and her children and eleven grandchildren, and died an old woman. The light in the gallery isn't *her*. It isn't reliving it. It isn't tired of it. It doesn't know. It's just the same nine minutes, being true, over and over." She looked at the chalk. "I'm the one who lived them a hundred and seventy-five thousand times. I'm the only one who ever did. The horror of it was never in the loop. It was in whoever was watching."

He sat without moving.

"If a loop's really closed," said Ione, "if nothing in it goes forward, if nothing in it can remember — then there's nobody inside it to be tired of it. It's just once. It's once, and it's true, and it stays true. It's only forever for whoever's outside, looking in." She shrugged a little, as if embarrassed by how much she'd said. "That's all. That's all I know. I worked it out on the floor of a gallery when I was nineteen. In there, nothing happened once. Out here, everything does."

He thought about fifteen years. He thought about a flat on Tier Nine and a phone that never rang and a man who got up every day and did the same nothing and went to bed. The same day, over and over, for fifteen years. And a pen, a nice blue Ministry pen, that he'd picked up once, one time, and signed with.

He had lived that one moment — the pen — five and a half thousand times. Every morning. He was the only one who had.

"How did you stay sane?" he said. "In there. Three years."

Ione thought about it seriously. She always thought about his questions seriously; he'd noticed that, in the loft, in the Tower. As if every question deserved to be turned over and looked at from underneath before you answered.

"I decided to love them," she said.

"The ghosts?"

"The people." She looked up at the Lace. "After the months on the floor. I got up one cycle and I thought: I can hate this, or I can learn it. And I started watching. Properly. Every one of them. I learned all their names — they say each other's names, you pick it up. Fen at the tensioner board. Dieter at output, who drinks his coffee too hot and burns his tongue every single time. Oksana with the camera, who's frightened of heights and never looks out of the window. The boy with the tablet, who's in love with Oksana." A small smile. "And Ruth. I learned Ruth best. I sat beside her at the board. For three years. I know her better than I know anyone living. She has a mole, here—" she touched her own jaw below the ear "—and she bites her thumb when Crane talks. Just the side of it. She doesn't know she does it. And when she says *one-thirty-five*, she closes her eyes first, for about half a second. She doesn't know she does that either."

She was quiet for a moment.

"She was my best friend," said Ione, "for three years. And she never heard a single word I said to her."

The chalk hung in the air. Far below, the city didn't move.

"And the key?" said Cas.

"One cycle," said Ione, "it was there. On the gantry. After a hundred and seventy-five thousand cycles where it wasn't. It was the only different thing that had ever happened." She touched it where it hung at her throat. "And I looked up from it. And far off — at the far end of the gantry, where it meets the wall of Spindle One, a long way off, further than it should have looked — there was someone standing. A woman. Not lit up. Not a ghost. Real. Standing where no ghost ever stood. With grey hair."

He didn't breathe.

"I thought it was my mother," said Ione simply. "I shouted. *Mam.* I ran along the gantry toward her. And she turned away. And where she'd been standing, when I got close, the air was — wrong. Shimmering. A seam. I went into it and I was out. On the Rim. Out of the gallery. Out of the loop." She let go of the key. "I never saw her again. I came down the spindle in two rounds. Gran met me at the bottom. She said I'd been gone for two days. I looked in a water-trough when I got home and I was twenty, and I had this." She touched the white streak.

"And the figure in the suit?" he said. "Coming down?"

"That was on the way down. Across the gulf, on another spindle. Very far. Just once." She looked at the suit he was sitting in, the humming keel. "I didn't know what it was, then. I only knew I'd never seen anything like it."

He looked at the suit too.

*There are only three suits.*

Neither of them said anything else.

* * *

She slept, in the end. She lay down on the planks with her head on her folded rope and her splinted wrist on her chest and was asleep in about a minute, the way riggers slept, the way soldiers slept, completely.

He didn't.

He sat with his back against the strut, in the suit, with the helmet in his lap, and watched her. He watched the white streak in her hair, and the black stitches over her eye, and the slow rise and fall of her breathing in the almost-weightless calm. He watched the chalk, which had drifted over her and hung above her face in a small white cloud, glittering, turning. He watched the city far below them, still as a photograph — thirty-one thousand people caught in the middle of a single night while he sat and watched a woman sleep for hours.

He thought about a hundred and seventy-five thousand cycles. About a girl of seventeen lying on the floor of a gallery for months, letting the worst night in the world happen through her. About that girl getting up, one cycle, and deciding to learn their names.

He thought about Teo's voice. *Do it because you want to. Or because she wants you to. Anything but that.*

He found — examining himself, the way he'd examined himself for fear at the window in the Collar, patting his pockets — he found something he hadn't expected. He didn't have a word for it at first. It was small. It was very quiet. It was sitting somewhere under his breastbone, where the sketchbook was, under the neck-ring of the suit, against his heart.

It was that he wanted to see what happened next.

Not what happened in the Crown. Not whether the lock would turn or the Clause would work or the plate meant what he was beginning, in the back of his mind, very quietly, to be afraid it meant. Not that. Something smaller. He wanted to see her wake up. He wanted to see what she'd say when she did. He wanted to see the chalk fall, when they left the Lee, and her blow it off her palm, and the look on her face when she read it. He wanted the next round. And the one after that.

He sat very still with it, the way you'd sit very still with a bird that had landed on your hand.

It had been a long time. He'd forgotten what it felt like. He'd forgotten that it was possible to want, carefully, just to go on.

Below them — so slowly that he had to watch for a long time to be sure of it — at the very centre of the city, at the foot of Spindle One, the first shutter of the morning began to rise.
