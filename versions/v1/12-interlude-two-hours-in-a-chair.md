---
title: Two Hours in a Chair
label: Interlude
---

> Nobody's keeping score. Nobody ever was.
> — Teo Lisk, to a man in a keel suit, Lattice Station 14-C

**Minute 0.**

She watched him go up the ladder, and then she watched him stop being a person.

It was the strangest thing she had ever seen, and she had seen a stack fire in Kovač turn a forty-storey housing tower into a candle. He climbed. Ordinary. Grey suit, humming keel, her keel, gauntlets on the rungs, one after the other, climbing on the back side of the ladder where the boy's map said to climb. Thirty metres. Thirty-five. And then he reached the part of the shaft where the air was wrong, the trembling curtain, and he went into it — and he *stuttered*. That was the only word. He was on one rung and then three rungs up and then two rungs up again and then five. Bits of him were ahead of other bits. For a second she saw his arm reaching up out of the top of the curtain while his boots were still under it and the middle of him was a smear, a wet grey streak on the air, like a thumbprint on a lens.

Then he was above it. And he was moving so fast that she couldn't follow him. A blur, going up the shaft like something fired. Then a dot. Then nothing.

"Talking to you," she said into her radio. "Still here. Keep going. Nearly there."

Her radio made a noise like a bird.

She sat back in the chair.

"Right," said Teo Lisk to the empty room. "Right, then."

* * *

**Minute 1.**

She took stock, because that was what you did.

Station air: good. Pressure: good. There was a panel by the door that told her so, and she believed it, because her ears weren't popping and she wasn't dizzy. Water: a cooler, two-thirds full, with a stack of paper cups. Food: the suit's ration paste, which was vile, and a first-aid cabinet, which on inspection contained bandages, burn gel, a foil blanket, a bottle of painkillers with most of the painkillers gone, and a single packet of digestive biscuits, one-third eaten, with a sell-by date nine months ago.

She ate one. It was soft. She ate another.

The suit without its keel was just a suit. Heavy. The exo worked. She could walk about. Every so often the room seemed to sway, very slightly, like the deck of a ship, and she'd put her hand out to the back of the chair and it would pass. The lee, the calm patch, the pocket of ordinary. It filled the booth. Just about. She'd dropped washers at the edges: by the door, by the far wall, in the corners. Straight down, every one. Good.

She looked out of the viewport at the ladder going up into the trembling air, and at the bottom of the ladder, the shape lying crumpled at its foot.

"Farr," she said. "You'd have had a theory about all this."

The room didn't answer. She hadn't really expected it to.

* * *

**Minute 2.**

She went down through the hatch with the foil blanket.

It was stupid. It served no purpose. She knew that. She knelt beside the body at the bottom of the conduit — the little broad body in its patchwork clothes, its legs white bone and its face half a face — and unfolded the blanket, and laid it over, from the head down, and tucked it round. The foil crackled. It shone in the pearly light. The little yellow lamp on its cord was still showing at the neck; she tucked that under too.

"There you go, love," she said. "Whoever you are."

She went back up. She shut the hatch.

* * *

**Minute 4.**

The radio chirped.

Not like before. Not the bird-squeaks she'd been getting since he went through, the little ragged scraps of him arriving squashed. This was a single long *tweeee*, rising, about the length of a breath, and then a click.

And the little amber light came on at the bottom of her visor. RECEIVING. BUFFERING. RETIMING.

She sat forward in the chair.

It took the suit a minute and a half to chew it out. She watched the light the whole time. Then it went *ding*, and there he was, Casimir Hale, his dry, tired, clever voice in her ear — and it was *long*. It went on and on. She sat in the chair and listened to him talk, and her mouth fell open, and after a while she realised it had been open for some time, and shut it.

*There's a city in here, Teo.*

Thirty-one thousand. A hundred and sixty-eight years. Rats with the names of officials. The Tending, sung wrong. The Section 9 placard in a church.

*They called me the Relief.*

She laughed out loud. She couldn't help it. She sat alone in a booth at the bottom of the world and laughed until she had to wipe her eyes with the back of her gauntlet, and her visor got in the way, so she took the helmet off and laughed some more.

And then he said the other thing. *I lied to them. Within an hour.* And he said it the way you'd tell a doctor where it hurt.

She stopped laughing.

It had been four minutes. Four minutes, on her suit clock, since he'd climbed up that ladder. He'd got to the top, and been pulled out by a stranger, and carried through a city, and made a saint, and stood up in front of twenty thousand people, and gone to bed, and sat on the floor and talked to her about it — and all of that was *a day*, for him. Most of a day. While she'd eaten two biscuits and covered a dead woman with a blanket.

"Bloody hell, Hale," she said.

Then she thought about it for a moment, and put the helmet back on, and pressed the button on her own recorder, and said: *Right. Your first chirp's come through.*

* * *

**Minute 11.**

The hardline rang.

She hadn't known it was there until it rang. It was a telephone — an old one, a handset on a hook on the wall by the dead monitors, grey plastic, a curly cord. It rang with a bell. An actual bell. She stared at it for three rings and then got up and picked it up.

"Lattice Station Fourteen-C."

"Major Lisk." Gaunt. Of course it was. That soft voice, as if he were ringing to ask whether she'd enjoyed a book he'd lent her. "What a relief. We weren't sure the line would still be live. We lost your pucks about twenty minutes ago; this is the old copper, it runs all the way round the Lattice. I've been ringing every station on it." A pause. "Is Dr Hale with you?"

She looked out of the viewport at the ladder.

"No," she said.

"No?"

"He's gone up. Through the Shear. With my keel. His was damaged." She kept her voice very flat. "Farr's dead."

A silence on the line.

"I'm very sorry," said Gaunt, and to her considerable surprise, it sounded as though he were. "He was — very young." Another pause. "And Dr Hale is — proceeding?"

"Yes."

"Proceeding to the Crown."

"Yes."

"To reseat Six."

The chip. Silver seal. *Any means.* Underlined. She'd dropped it in a coolant channel and watched it go.

"He's reseating Six," said Teo Lisk. "As we speak."

She'd never lied to the Calm in her life. Not once. Not in twenty-nine years. You didn't. It wasn't that anyone had ever told her not to; it was simply that you didn't, the way you didn't put your hand in a fire. And it came out of her as smooth and easy as water out of a tap, and Gaunt said, "Oh, that's good. That's very good. The Minister will be so relieved," and she stood there holding the grey plastic handset and felt it go all the way through her, cold, like swallowing ice.

*Easy*. It was the easiest thing she'd ever done.

"Major," said Gaunt, "I won't keep you. I only wanted you to know — the grid's failing. Kovač is at half. We've started the rolling cuts on the upper tiers. We're telling people it's maintenance." His voice changed, very slightly. "People are being very calm. I've never seen anything like it. They sit in the dark in their flats and wait for the lights to come back. Nobody's asking. Nobody's asking anything." He stopped. "I find I'd almost rather they did."

She let the silence sit.

"Well," said Gaunt. "Do give Dr Hale my regards, if you speak to him."

He hung up.

She put the handset back on its hook. She sat back down in the chair. After a while she noticed that her hands were shaking, and she put them flat on her knees and held them there until they stopped.

* * *

**Minutes 12 to 30.**

The chirps kept coming. Every few minutes. A long rising *tweeee*, a click, a minute and a half of amber light, a *ding*. And then Cas, talking.

She got used to the rhythm of it. She'd sit in the chair with her boots up on the dead monitor desk and wait for the next one, the way you wait for the next episode of something. Four minutes. Six. Three. She'd hear the chirp and her whole body would go still.

He told her about the Tollers. About a tower full of pendulums, and an old woman with eyes like a bird who'd made him soup, and a column of black stone with every Bell of a hundred and sixty-eight years chalked on it in fives. He told her about a Log. About letters to a little girl called Maddy who was seven. That one she had to stop and play again, and then she sat with it for a while, with the helmet off, looking at the ladder, before she could listen to the next.

He told her about the market. About rat. About a boy called Pim who named his rats after officials.

He told her about Ione.

He didn't say much, at first. *There's a woman.* She'd pulled him out of the well. She had a white streak in her hair. She was a rigger. She had a key. She'd been somewhere, up near the top, and come back different, and nobody believed her. That was all, the first time. But then the next chirp he mentioned her again. And the next. *Ione says. Ione showed me. I was up in Ione's loft.* She was in every one. By the fifth or sixth, Teo realised she could picture her: a short, broad, scarred woman with a rope on her shoulder and a way of looking at you that made you feel she'd read the end of the book.

And his voice was changing.

She noticed that too, around minute twenty. She played an early one again to be sure, and then a later one, one after the other. The early ones were him: dry, careful, flat, a man reading out a list. The later ones weren't. They were — she didn't have the word. They had *weather* in them. He laughed, sometimes. He stopped in the middle of sentences because he'd seen something out of a window and wanted to tell her what it was. He talked about a wedding on a rooftop and she could hear, under the words, that he had been happy at it. For an hour, on a rooftop, with a cup of something that took the skin off his throat, he'd been happy.

"Well," Teo said to nobody. "Look at you."

* * *

**Minute 21.**

The lee moved.

She was halfway through a message — the one about the wedding, the riggers tying their wrists together — when she felt the chair tip. Very slightly. A lean to the left, like a car taking a bend. She sat up. The room swayed. On the floor by the far wall, where she'd dropped a washer when she first came in, the washer slid.

It slid about a hand's width across the floor, toward the corner, and stopped.

She watched the corner. She watched it until her eyes ached. Nothing else happened. The room steadied.

She got up and dragged the chair a metre to the right, into the middle of the room, and sat back down in it, and pressed play again. When she recorded her next message, she mentioned it. *The edge came across the floor, maybe a hand's width. I moved the chair.* Casual. As if she were telling him the weather.

It was the weather. That was the thing. It was only the weather.

* * *

**Minute 34.**

She found the login by accident.

She'd been poking at the dead monitors out of boredom, and one of them wasn't dead — a little grey terminal under the desk, on the same old copper as the hardline, humming to itself. It had a Civil Defence emblem on its login screen. Some maintenance link. She typed in her own service number and password, not expecting anything, and it let her in.

She sat and looked at it for a moment. Then she pulled up the relocation lists.

It took her four minutes to find Nell Hale. Tier Thirty, Hollowmere Academy sports hall, with mother Sabine Hale-Okoro. Status: well. And a note in the welfare column in the slightly harried shorthand of a volunteer who'd been awake for thirty hours: *Child v. calm. Asking everyone re: stars (?). Has lizard (not hers).*

Teo laughed out loud in the empty room.

Then she typed in her own sons' names.

Joss first. University accommodation, relocated to a transit hall on Tier Forty. Status: well. No note.

Mattias. Kovač. Kovač wasn't on the relocation lists; Kovač had its own. She dug. It took her ten minutes. She found him in the end on a Kovač municipal list, a crew list, not a relocation list: *M. Lisk, electrician, Kovač Fusion Station B, emergency staff, on shift*. On shift. At the plant that was holding up the whole grid at half power.

Of course he was.

She sat looking at his name on the screen for a long time.

Then she took the marker out of the pocket of her liner — the black marker from the supervisor's desk in B-7, the one Hale had written backwards with on their side of the glass; she'd picked it up off the gallery floor on the way out, without telling him, because she hadn't been able to leave it — and she got up and went to the viewport, and in the bottom corner, small, where she'd see it from the chair, she wrote:

:: MATTIAS
:: JOSS

She looked at them. Then, underneath, she added:

:: BEN

And then, after a moment:

:: SEREN (THE LADY)

* * *

**Minute 41.**

*I'm going to tell them.*

She listened to that one sitting very still. He said it quietly, at the end of a long message about a rigger crew that had been caught by the Lace and turned to bones. Kindling Round. Three rounds away, for him. Everything. The Outside, the test, the Deputy Minister, the debt, the lock, the key, Ione. In front of the whole city.

She knew what it would cost him. She'd sat next to him at the canteen window in the Collar and watched him not answer *why*. She'd seen his face at the booth when he thought the Shear might settle things for him. She knew.

She played it again.

Then she pressed record and said, *Christ, Hale. Good.* And a lot of other things, most of which she'd forgotten as soon as she'd said them, except that she'd told him she was proud of him, and she'd meant it, and she'd been glad he couldn't hear it for a day.

* * *

**Minutes 42 to 128.**

He didn't send anything else for a long time.

She understood, doing the sum on the back of the fire regulations with a ballpoint she found in the desk, that for him it would be — what? Rounds and rounds. Days. Weeks. She didn't know what had happened. She sat in the chair and waited.

She read the fire regulations again. She did the sum again. She ate the last digestive. She went down the hatch and sat for a while with the lady in the blanket, just sitting, not saying anything, because it seemed wrong to leave her alone. She came back up. She dropped washers. She moved the chair once more, at minute seventy-three, when the washer by the door slid, and again at minute ninety-eight, when the one in the far corner did. The room was smaller now. Not much. You wouldn't notice, unless you were dropping washers. The calm part of it was a circle in the middle of the floor, about three metres across, and the chair was in it, and she was in the chair.

At minute a hundred and two, the hardline rang. She let it ring. It rang twenty times and stopped.

She talked to Ben. Out loud. She told him about Mattias on shift at Kovač, and about Joss, who'd wanted to be a pilot when he was five and a poet at fifteen and was now doing something with computers she couldn't pronounce. She told him about her husband, Anders, who'd dropped dead in the kitchen eleven years ago in the middle of making toast, and how she'd come home from a shift and found the toast still in the toaster, and how for a year afterwards she couldn't be in the room when anybody made toast. She told him Jonah would get the dog. She'd see to it. She'd buy the bloody dog herself.

At minute a hundred and twenty she realised she'd been crying for some time, and stopped, and blew her nose on a bandage from the first-aid kit.

* * *

**Minute 129.**

*Tweeeeeeeeeeee.*

It was the longest chirp yet. It went on for three breaths. Then a click, and another *tweeee*, shorter, and a third, and the amber light came on and stayed on for nearly four minutes while the suit chewed, and she sat forward in the chair with both hands gripping its arms.

*Ding.*

He'd been in a tank. Eleven rounds, in the dark.

He told her about the Commons. He told it plainly, flatly, the old way, the way he'd talked at the gate — a man reading a list — and she could hear what it was costing him to do it that way, because it was the only way he could do it at all. The truth, and the banner, and a man shouting *it's open*. Forty-seven. A little boy he'd pulled off a ladder. A father who'd said *I'll come back for him*. The sound that came up the well.

She sat with her fist pressed against her mouth.

Then the Quiet. Then an old man with a lamp. Then a big Warden with a rope. Then the Tollers' tower again, and the old woman, and the number she'd given him — *thirty-one thousand, three hundred and sixty* — and then, in a different voice, a lighter voice, almost wondering: *Teo, they're making lamps. The whole city. They're putting them out in the lanes. They're getting ready for the dark.*

And then:

*We're going up. Tonight. Spindle Four. Ione and me. The electronics will go, up there, Ben said they would, so this might be the last you get. If it works, the Lamp goes out. The Shear goes. Time in here will be the same as time out there. They'll be able to walk down. Thirty-one thousand of them. All at once, down fourteen wells, into the Works. Come and get them, Teo. Bring everyone. Bring blankets. Bring—*

A pause.

*There's another file. It's for Nell. It's everything I've recorded for her, since I got here. It's about three weeks. I don't know if I'll — if you get out, and I don't. Would you give it to her? Please. And tell her—*

Another pause, longer.

*No. It's in the file. It's all in the file.*

And then, very quietly:

*Thank you for the keel.*

*Ding.*

A second file arrived behind it. The suit showed it on her visor: a little icon, a size, a name. NELL. Three weeks. Three weeks of a man talking to his daughter, squashed into a chirp three breaths long.

She sat in the chair and looked at it for a long time.

* * *

**Minute 130.**

She recorded her answer.

She did it with the helmet off, holding the recorder close to her mouth, sitting forward in the chair with her elbows on her knees. She didn't plan it. She knew it would be the last one — knew it the way you know, sometimes, at the top of a flood barrier with the water coming up, that this is the last thing you'll get to say to somebody for a while, maybe ever. So she said it.

She didn't know, afterwards, all of what she said. Some of it she remembered. That she'd give Nell the file herself, by hand, and sit with her while she listened. That she'd tell Jonah about the dog. That he was to look after the rigger with the white streak in her hair, because it sounded as though nobody had for a while. That he was to come back and buy her the large one he owed her. That her sons were called Mattias and Joss and she'd written their names on the window where she could see them, and she'd written Ben's under, and Seren's, and if he didn't mind she'd write his, too, now.

That guilt was a lousy reason. That she'd said it before and she'd say it again because he was the kind of thick-headed bastard who needed to hear things twice.

That she was fine.

That she'd be here.

*Bring them out, Cas.*

She pressed send. The suit chirped, a tiny sound, a squeak, her whole minute gone up the ladder in a blink. She sat back.

She took the marker and went to the window and wrote, under SEREN (THE LADY):

:: CAS

She stood looking at it.

* * *

**Minute 131.**

The washer by the door slid.

Not a hand's width. It slid a metre, rattling across the floor, and hit the leg of the desk, and spun, and lay still. And the one by the far wall slid, toward the corner. And the room *leaned*.

She put her hand out to the chair.

The edge of the lee was coming across the floor. She could see it now. She hadn't been able to see it before, but she could now: a line on the floor, a sort of shimmer, a ripple in the air a hand's height off the ground, like heat over a road. On one side of it, the dust lay flat. On the other side it was moving, drifting, crawling very slowly toward the corners. The line was about a metre from the door. As she watched, it moved closer.

She did the thing you did. She took stock. Station air: good. Pressure: good. Suit: no keel. Exit: through the door, into a Lattice full of weather, four kilometres from the gate, or through the hatch, into the conduit, beside the lady in the blanket, where the Shear was forty metres up and the weather was whatever the weather was down there.

The line moved closer.

She took the chair and dragged it to the far corner of the room, the corner by the viewport, as far from the door as she could get, and set it down, and sat in it, and put her boots up flat against the wall.

The line was two metres away now. Then a metre and a half. The room swayed, and swayed back. On the other side of the viewport, in the conduit, the Shear trembled on the air as it always had, the ladder going up through it, jumping and smearing. Above it, far up, the pale bright disc of light.

Teo Lisk sat in her chair in the corner with her boots against the wall and her hands folded in her lap, and watched it come.

"Come on, Hale," she said.
