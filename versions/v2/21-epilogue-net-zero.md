> Tell me how it ends.
> — Perpetua Tally, Head Toller of Holdfast, last words

The hammer stopped on the fourth day.

Civil Defence found the flywheel on the third, in a vault in the Undercroft below the Lattice, still spinning — a disc of iron the size of a house, turning in the dark on its great bearing, driving its hammer down onto its keelstone anvil once a second the way Iris Hollowell had built it to, the way it had since 02:14 on the night of the Kindling. It took them a day to work out how to stop it. Nobody had ever designed a way. *When all else has failed*, Hollowell had written, *the station will say so*, and she had not imagined anyone wanting it to stop saying so.

They stopped it at nine minutes past eleven on the fourth morning.

In the Relief Camp on the Tallows flats, where thirty-one thousand people were sleeping in tents and prefabricated huts and the gutted shells of warehouses behind a fence of Civil Defence tape, it was the first silence in the history of Holdfast. People came out of the tents. They stood in the mud in the rain and looked at each other. Children cried. Old people sat down on the ground. For three nights afterwards, half the camp couldn't sleep.

The Tollers solved it. Tobiah, mostly. On the fifth night he sat down at a table in the Tollers' hut with a wooden spoon and began, very quietly, to tap. Once a second. On the tabletop. And the Tollers in the hut, and then the people in the tents round it, lay in the dark and listened, and slept.

They took it in turns after that. Every night, in the Relief Camp, somebody sat up and tapped. They were still doing it when Ione left. For all she knew, they were doing it still.

* * *

It rained.

The Canopy was gone — its generators had died with the grid on the second day, and there was no Draw now to bring them back — and the autumn monsoon came in off the southern ocean for the first time in sixty years without anything to stop it, and it rained on Averna as it had not rained in living memory. It came down so hard it bounced. It ran off the black terraces of the Stacks in sheets and poured down the dead escalators and filled the Line tunnels and flooded the lower tiers, and Civil Defence went into the lower tiers with ropes and rubber boats, and Teo Lisk went with them, and so did Ione.

She stood in it the first time for a whole afternoon.

She stood in the mud outside the Tollers' hut with her face turned up and her mouth open and let it come down on her. It was warm. It came down so hard it stung. She drank it. It tasted of nothing, mostly, and sometimes of dust, and sometimes, when it ran off the roof of the hut, of metal. Exactly the way he'd said.

"You'll catch your death," said Teo, from the door.

"I've never been wet," said Ione. "Not like this. Not from above."

Teo looked at her for a while. Then she came out and stood in it too.

* * *

There were colds.

Nobody in Holdfast had had one for a hundred and sixty-eight years. Nobody had had anything that wasn't already in the Vault when the Shear formed. The Commonweal's doctors knew it would happen, and screened them and quarantined them and vaccinated them against everything they could think of, and it happened anyway. Coughs. Fevers. Things that Outside children shrugged off in a week went through the Relief Camp like fire through dry grass. Eleven people died of them in the first month. Most of them were old. Two of them were babies.

One of the nurses who came to the camp in the second week was a slight, dark, quiet man of about thirty with very tired eyes, and on his third day Ione found him sitting on an upturned crate behind the medical tent in the rain, not doing anything, with a young dog asleep across his feet. A scruffy brown dog, all legs, with one ear up and one ear down.

She knew who he was before she asked. Teo had told her. Teo had gone to his flat on Tier Twelve on the fifth day with a box of things from a locker in the Tallow Works lab, and come back without the box and with a very strange expression, and when Ione asked her about it, said only: *I bought him the bloody dog.*

"You're Jonah," said Ione.

He looked up.

She sat down on the ground beside the crate, in the rain, and told him about the glass.

She told him all of it. The way Teo had told it to her, walking out through the Works, and the way she'd seen it herself, standing at the long window of B-7 with her hand on the glass. The hall. The algae. The beard. The rat called Minister. MINISTER II. *I HAVE HAD 8 BIRTHDAYS NOBODY CAME*, and Teo writing back *WE'RE HERE*. The map, and how it had got them out of the Carousel. *TELL JONAH. TELL HIM I SAID YES TO THE DOG.*

"He said," said Ione, "*had a good life. Mostly algae.*"

Jonah laughed.

It came out of him suddenly, a real laugh, shocked, surprised, the way a laugh comes when you're not expecting one, and he put his hand over his mouth, and laughed again — and then he wasn't laughing. He sat on the crate in the rain with his hand over his mouth and his shoulders shaking, and the dog woke up and put its front paws on his knee and licked his wrist, and he couldn't stop.

Ione sat on the ground beside him and didn't say anything. She'd learned that from Cas. From the hut. Sometimes you just sat there.

"What's the dog called?" she said, when he'd stopped.

He wiped his face with the back of his hand. He looked down at it.

"Minister," said Jonah.

* * *

Halloran Crane walked out of Gate Nine last, at eleven minutes past ten on the second night after Continuance Day, eighteen hours after Ione had walked out ahead of him.

He had stood at the top of Well One for nineteen hours. Every one of them. Every family, every child, every old man in a rope sling, every Warden and every rigger and every person who had ever called Seren Tally a heretic or stepped round her daughter in the lanes — he had seen them onto the ladder, one after another, and said their names, and when the last of them had gone down and the Commons behind him was empty and lit and silent, he had put out his lamp and gone down after them.

Ondine Rask was waiting for him at the gate.

Nobody knew what they said. They stood apart, on the concrete, in the rain, for about ten minutes — the Minister of Continuance in her dark suit and the First Steward of Holdfast in his black coat with its gold lamp — and nobody went near them. A Civil Defence sergeant called Kade said afterwards that she'd heard only one thing, as she passed with a stretcher, and that it had been the old man who said it, very gently.

*It's over, Minister. We're relieved.*

Rask resigned nine days later. The Directorate announced it as ill health. By then it didn't matter what the Directorate announced.

* * *

It came out.

It came out slowly, and then all at once, the way the Bells had come. Gaunt went first. The day after Gate Nine, Colonel Ansel Gaunt of the Office of Public Calm sat down in a room in the Collar in front of a Civil Defence recorder — not a Calm one — and began to talk, and didn't stop for three days. He talked about the Margin Review. About Adeyemi. About a revised report and a nice pen. About a chip with a silver seal, and orders with *any means* underlined. About a Continuance Day address with footage from Year Eighty. He brought documents. He had kept copies of everything, it turned out, for thirty years, in a flat on Tier Six, in biscuit tins.

"Things that are recorded," he said, on the recording, "have a way of becoming history. I used to think that was the danger. I find I've changed my mind."

Then the Log. Ruth Tally's Log, the whole of it, a hundred and sixty-eight years of it, the pencil getting smaller and smaller — the Tollers let the inquiry copy every page. And Aldous Crane's private journal, which Halloran Crane handed over himself. *I did not give it to the Lamp. I held it.* And the recordings: a month of a man talking into a machine in a stone room, in a loft, on a spindle, about rats and weddings and funerals and a game called Drop, and near the end, flatly, plainly, about the Margin Review and what he had signed and why. He'd recorded it for Teo. Teo gave it to the inquiry. She didn't ask anyone's permission.

It didn't fix anything. Ione had thought, somehow, that it might. That the truth would come out and the Commonweal would look at it and something would change, the way the Lamp had gone out and the Drop had become a road. It didn't happen like that. The Directorate was still the Directorate. The lights came back on in rotation, four hours a day per tier, from the old fusion plants at Kovač and Tessarine, and people queued for water and argued about whose fault it was. There were riots on Tier Four in the third week. There were strikes. There was an inquiry, and then another inquiry into the inquiry. The client states cut their trunk lines and sent angry envoys. The First Director gave an address about resilience and the spirit of the Tending.

But things were *said*. That was the difference. In the lanes of the Stacks, on the terraces, in the queues for water, in the sports halls where people were still being relocated for comfort — people said things out loud. They said *default*. They said *Adeyemi*. They said *Aldous Crane held the key*. They said *thirty-one thousand people*. They said *the night shift*.

On the big poster on the side of the Line station at the foot of the Tier Nine arterial — blue and gold, a child's face turned up toward a golden flame, THE FUTURE WILL PAY in letters two metres high — somebody had sprayed, in rough white paint, underneath:

:: NOT ANY MORE

Nobody took it down.

* * *

She took Ruth's letter to Madeleine Tally on a Tuesday in the fourth week.

Maddy lived with her grandmother on Tier Nine, in a flat that had been Ruth's mother's for thirty years, three streets from where Cas's Standby flat had been. Ruth's mother was sixty-seven. She had white hair and Ruth's mole below her left ear, and when she opened the door and Ione told her who she was — who she *was*, the chain of it, Ruth and Hal and Hal's daughter and her son and his, all the way down to a girl with a white streak in her hair standing on the landing with a steel box — she didn't say anything at all. She just looked at Ione's face. And then she stood back and let her in.

Maddy was on the floor of the front room, doing a jigsaw.

She was seven. She had dark hair in two plaits that someone had done badly, and her mother's eyes, and she looked up from the jigsaw at the strange short broad woman in the patched clothes and said, "Are you from the Draw? Nana says the Draw's where Mum is."

Ione knelt down on the carpet beside the jigsaw.

She'd thought about this for four weeks. She had thought about what she would say, and how, and she'd practised it, in the Tollers' hut at night with Tobiah tapping. She had it all ready. She looked at the little girl and none of it was any use.

"Your mum," she said, "wrote you some letters."

She gave her the last one. Not the Log. Just the one page, the last page, the old woman's shaking pencil, that Tobiah had copied out very carefully in large clear letters on a clean sheet so that a seven-year-old could read it, with the original folded inside it.

Maddy read it. It took her a long time. She moved her lips. Her grandmother stood in the doorway with her hand over her mouth.

"Eighty-eight," said Maddy at last. She looked up. "Mum's thirty-eight."

"She was thirty-eight," said Ione, "when she went to work that night. And then she lived for a very long time. In a place where time went faster. She lived her whole life. She got to be eighty-eight." She paused. "It was a good life. Mostly. She says so. You can read it. It's all in there."

Maddy looked at the letter.

"Is she coming home?" she said.

"No," said Ione. "I'm sorry. She died a long time ago. A long time ago for her. For you, it was the night she went to work." She didn't know how to say it better. There wasn't a better. "But she thought about you every day. Every single day. That's true. I know it's true. She wrote it down."

Maddy thought about that. She thought about it seriously, turning it over, the way Ione did, and Ione watched her do it and felt something go through her that she didn't have a name for.

"Was she happy?" said Maddy.

"Yes," said Ione. "She had a husband called Tomas who made her laugh. And three more children. You've got two brothers. One of them's called Hal. And a sister. They're all — they were all very old, a long time ago. And she had eleven grandchildren. And they had children. And I'm one of them." She held out her hand, the one without the splint. "I'm your — I don't know what I am. Tobiah worked it out. It's a very long word. I'm your family."

Maddy looked at her hand. Then she took it, the way children take hands, without any fuss.

"You've got a white bit in your hair," she said.

"Yes."

"Why?"

"I went somewhere a long way up," said Ione, "and I was there for a long time."

Maddy considered this. Then she turned back to the jigsaw. "Do you want to help? It's a horse. I can't find the sky bits."

Ione sat on the carpet with Ruth Tally's daughter for the rest of the afternoon, and helped her find the sky.

* * *

Nell Hale lived on Tier Nine too, now. Sabine had moved them back there when the relocation ended, into Cas's old Standby flat, because the Ministry had forgotten to reassign it and it was the only flat on the tier with a balcony that faced south. Nell had insisted. She'd said the stars were better that way.

Teo had been three times. The first time, on the fifth day, she'd gone to the sports hall on Tier Thirty with the recordings in her pocket, as she'd promised, and sat with Nell on a gym mat in the corner while she listened, and Nell had listened to all of them — a month of them, rats and weddings and funerals and Osk and *I ate a rat, don't tell your mother* — without stopping, for nine hours, with Teo beside her, and at the end of it she had not cried and she had not hit Teo. She had said, *can I keep them?* And Teo had said, *they're yours*. And Nell had said, *he found one. He found a star. I said he might.*

The fourth time, Teo brought Ione.

They went at night. It was one of the dark nights for Tier Nine — no power in the rotation — and the Stacks were black, and the sky over them was black, and in it, the stars. They came every night now. Averna had started, in the weeks since, to go out and look at them the way it used to go out and look at the Quota clocks. People sat on the terraces with blankets. Children lay on their backs on the arterial.

Nell opened the door. She was ten. She had her mother's grey eyes and a face that couldn't leave a question alone, and she looked at Ione in the doorway — at her, all of her, the short broad shape and the patched clothes and the rope, which Ione still wore out of habit, over her shoulder — without blinking.

"You're Ione," she said.

"Yes."

"He said I'd like you." She said it as if reporting a fact. "On the tapes. He said, *I think if you ever meet her, you'll like her. I don't know why I think that.*"

"He said that?"

"He said it on the day with the rat." Nell stood back from the door. "The stars are out. Do you want to see them? From the balcony? They're better from the balcony."

They sat on the balcony, the three of them, and Sabine brought tea and then went inside and left them. The balcony faced south over the dark Stacks, and the sky over them was full, the pale river of light going across it, the bright white one low in the east that shivered.

"That's Sirius," said Nell. "That's the Dog Star. It's the brightest. It's not the closest, though. It just looks like it is."

"He told me," said Ione.

"He *told* you?"

"On the Rim. In the gallery. He told me their names. I was—" She stopped. "I was very frightened, and he told me their names. So I'd have something to think about."

Nell looked at her. Then she looked back up at the sky.

"Is he dead?" she said.

She said it very plainly. The way she'd said *is it broken* on the phone, Teo had told her. The way you'd ask the time.

Ione had thought about this too. For four weeks. She'd thought about it harder than she'd thought about Maddy. She looked up at the sky, at the thousands of them, and said the only thing she had.

"No," she said. "He's in one moment. It's — it's hard to explain. It's only once. It's once, and it doesn't end. He's not waiting, or stuck, or — it's not like being in a room. It's not like the Quiet. It's just one moment, and he's in it, and it's always true." She was quiet. "It's only for ever for us. For him it's once."

Nell thought about that. Ione watched her do it.

"Is it a good moment?" said Nell.

Ione looked up at the sky.

"It's the best one I know," she said.

Nell was quiet. Then she moved along the bench, not far, a little, so that her shoulder was against Ione's arm. She just sat there.

After a while, Ione pointed.

"That one," she said. "Low down. Over there. On its own. The one with nothing near it. What's that one called?"

Nell followed her finger. And sat up, delighted.

"That's *Fomalhaut*," she said. "That's in the Southern Fish. It's the loneliest star. That's what they call it. Because there's nothing else bright near it, in all that bit of sky. It's just by itself."

"He said," said Ione. "In the gallery. He said, *Fomalhaut, which is lonely*."

"Did he have a favourite?" said Nell. "I asked him once. He said he didn't know. He said he'd never looked."

"He hadn't," said Ione. "So I picked one for him." She looked at the lonely star, low over the black Stacks, steady, golden, just there. "That one. I told him I'd pick one and look at it every night. That's his."

Nell looked at it for a long time.

"That's a good one," she said at last, seriously. "That's the right one for him."

"I think so."

"I'll look at it too," said Nell Hale. "Every night. Then there's two of us."

Teo, at the other end of the bench, had turned her face away and was looking very hard at the Stacks.

* * *

In the sixth week, she went back into the Vault.

The inquiry wanted a survey. Teo was leading it, because nobody else in the Commonweal would go in, and Teo asked her to come because she knew the spindles, and she said yes because she'd always been going to say yes.

It was dark. That was the thing she hadn't been ready for, coming up through the conduit into the Drop District with her lamp. Holdfast was dark. Every lamp in the lanes had burned out long ago. The shrines were dark. The scaffold towers stood up out of the black like the ribs of something dead, and the washing still hung on the lines between them, and the rat pens stood empty with their doors open, and in the Commons the Tollers' banner still hung from the gantry in the dark, Seren's line, white on black, and nobody to read it. It was so quiet. No Bell. No Lamp. No hum. The Anvil under her boots was cold.

It was ordinary. That was the other thing. It weighed what everything weighed. The chalk fell straight.

They went up Spindle One. There was no need for the Lees now, and no need for Four; Teo's team had rigged ropes up the inside of the lattice, and it was only climbing, the whole four kilometres of it. It took them two days. They slept in a cache in the old best Lee, and the chalk didn't hang in the air, and Ione lay awake on the planks for a long time.

At the top, below the Rim, the gallery was dark. No ghost-light in the windows. Nothing going round. The Carousel was just a corridor — a curving steel corridor round the spindle, with an algae trough along its windows gone brown and dry, and the walls covered in scratched fives, and a pallet of rags, and a pile of smooth stones. Ione walked the whole way round it. It came back to where it started, because it was a ring, the ordinary way. She went out of the inner door onto the Rim and it was the Rim.

And above the Rim, where the Crown had been, there was the thing.

Teo's instruments had been trying to see it for a week, from below, with lights and lasers and sounding gear. They'd got nothing. You shone a light up at it and the light went into it and didn't come back. It was a sphere. They'd worked that much out, from the shape of the nothing: a sphere about four hundred metres across, hanging in the air at the top of the Vault where the Lamp had been, enclosing the top of Spindle One and the Crown and part of the Rim. It didn't reflect. It didn't glow. It didn't pull or push; the chalk fell straight right up to its edge. The instruments said it had no mass. None. No energy. None. No temperature. They said, as far as they could say anything, that it wasn't there.

*Net zero*, the inquiry's physicists called it, in their report. The debt and the payment, sealed together, balancing. Summing to nothing.

Ione stood on the Rim in the lamplight and looked at it.

It was black. Not dark; *black*. A curve of perfect black rising out of the steel of the Rim in front of her, and going up, and curving over, and blotting out the whole top of the sky. The Rim ran into it and stopped. The stair to the Crown ran into it and stopped. Her lamp's light went into it and stopped. It was like standing at the edge of the night.

"Careful," said Teo, behind her.

"It's all right," said Ione.

She walked up to it. Along the Rim, past the place where the chalk arrows had been — they were gone; the Lace had taken them, or time had, or they'd been swept away by the thing that had happened here — to the foot of the stair that went up to where the hatch had been. The black began on the third step. She climbed the first two. She stood on the second step, a hand's width from it.

She took off her glove.

She'd thought about this too. She'd thought about it at night, lying in the Tollers' hut, listening to Tobiah tap. She hadn't known what it would be. Cold. Hot. Hard. Nothing. Whether her hand would go into it and not come back.

She put her palm flat against it.

It wasn't cold. It wasn't warm. It wasn't hard, or soft. It wasn't anything. Her hand stopped against it, flat, the way it would stop against glass — the way Ben's had stopped, on the other side of the window in B-7 — and there was nothing there to feel. Not even the feeling of nothing. It was like the instant before you touch something. Like the last moment before a door opens. Like being just about to.

She stood on the stair with her hand flat on the black and her forehead nearly touching it.

She didn't know if he could hear. She was almost sure he couldn't. *Nothing will pass into it.* She'd read the plate. But she'd thought about that too, at night, and decided — the way she'd decided on the ladder, coming down in the dark, *he saw me* — that it didn't matter whether he could hear. She'd decided you said things anyway. The way Ruth had written her letters. The way the Tollers had counted a little girl's night.

"They come back," she told him. "The stars. Every night. Teo said. I didn't believe her, the first morning, I thought they'd gone out. But they come back. Every single night." She was quiet. "I found you one. Fomalhaut. The lonely one. Nell says it's right. She's going to look at it too. So there's two of us."

Her palm was flat on the black.

"It rains," she said. "It tastes of nothing. And metal, off the roofs. You were right. Jonah's got the dog. He called it Minister. Teo bought it. Teo's all right. She's — she cried on Nell's balcony and pretended she didn't. Maddy's seven. She's doing a jigsaw of a horse. I helped her with the sky."

She stopped. She stood there for a while.

"I wrote the last page," she said. "In the Log. Gran said to. She said *tell me how it ends*. So I did. I wrote it all down. Everything. Properly. Every word." Her voice was quite steady. "It ends with you."

She was quiet then. Behind her, on the Rim, Teo stood with her lamp, and didn't come any closer, and didn't say anything either.

Then Ione Tally took her hand away from the dark, and put her glove back on, and came down the stair, and went home.
