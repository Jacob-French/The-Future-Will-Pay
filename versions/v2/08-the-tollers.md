---
title: The Tollers
---

> Let them count the Bells. Nobody ever died of counting. It's drawing the line that does it.
> — Perpetua Tally, Head Toller, in conversation

Holdfast at night was not dark. It couldn't be. The Lamp never set, and the Lace never dimmed, and the violet light lay over the city at every hour exactly as it had lain at noon. But the city made its own night. At the end of second shift a horn blew from the top of the Steward's House, a long low note, and all across Holdfast the shutters came down — clattering, banging, sliding, squealing on their runners, thousands of them, a sound like a whole city clearing its throat — and when it was over, the lanes were in shadow. Not darkness. A deep, violet, shuttered dusk, lit here and there by the little clay oil lamps in the shrine-niches, where the flames stood up very straight in the still, warm air.

The Wardens outside the Steward's House were not, in fact, drunk. They were asleep. Osk was asleep on his stool outside Cas's door with his hard hat in his lap and his chin on his chest, snoring like distant machinery, and when Cas eased the door open and edged past him in the dark, moving so slowly that each step took a breath, one of Osk's eyes opened.

They looked at each other.

Osk's eye closed again. The snoring resumed.

* * *

Pim had brought the rope-cart. Cas lay in it, under a tarpaulin, and the boy hauled it through the lanes by its long handle, grunting, with Steward Harrow riding on his shoulder, and the cart's iron wheels made a small patient rattle on the keelstone that echoed off the shuttered walls.

"It's not far," Pim whispered. "It's right by the Bell Column. Everyone knows where it is. Nobody goes, except Tollers." He hauled. "And me. And Ione. I bring them rats."

"To eat?"

"*No.*" He sounded scandalised. "To *keep*. Gran Perpetua likes the brown ones." A pause. "Well. Sometimes to eat."

The lanes twisted and climbed and dropped. Once they went under a walkway where someone was sitting in the shadows, smoking something that smelled of burnt grass, and the someone said, "Pim," and Pim said, "Brother Coll," and that was all. Once a Warden went by at the end of a lane with a lamp on a pole, and Pim stopped dead and held his breath and the cart was still until the light had gone. Once, through a gap between two containers, Cas saw the Commons — its broken roof, the girders black against the Lace — and heard, very faintly, singing still going on inside it. Some of them hadn't gone home. Some of them were still there, singing the Tending, in the middle of the night, for him.

He shut his eyes under the tarpaulin.

The Bell came as they arrived. He felt it start in the cart's wheels, rising, and it rose and rose and went on rising, stronger than he'd felt it anywhere in the city, until the tarpaulin over him was trembling and the cart's handle was humming in Pim's hands. It didn't fade after ninety seconds. It went on — lingering, ringing, a long reverberation that seemed to come up out of the ground and hang in the air around them like the note after a struck bell.

"There," said Pim, pleased, as though he'd arranged it. "That's why it's here. It comes up here first. You can feel it best here. Gran says it's the only place in Holdfast where the Bell's *honest*."

Cas pushed the tarpaulin back.

Toller's Tower stood at the edge of a small square, and it was the strangest building he'd seen yet. At its heart was a pillar — a single massive column of black keelstone, perhaps three metres across, rising out of the Anvil and up, bare and smooth, thirty metres or more into the air, where it ended in nothing. Some structural member of the old Vault, some support for a gantry long since stripped. Around it, clinging to it, the Tollers had built their tower: a scaffold frame that went up the column in a spiral, storey after storey, each floor a platform of plank and plate lashed to the pipe, each one hung with things. As Cas looked up at it in the violet dusk he saw pendulums — dozens of pendulums, hanging from the platforms on long wires and rods and chains, some of them swinging, most of them still. He saw great glass carboys of water on shelves, with pipes and taps and marks on their sides. He saw a huge wooden wheel, like a mill-wheel, turning very slowly. He saw, at the very top, a platform with a sort of long tube mounted on a swivel, pointing up at the sky.

And he saw that the whole column — the whole three-metre, thirty-metre pillar of black keelstone, as far up as he could see — was covered in white marks.

"Come on," said Pim, hauling at the cart. "She's waiting. She hates waiting. She says she's done enough of it."

* * *

The door at the bottom of the tower was open, and inside was a single round room built around the base of the column. It was lit by a dozen lamps. It was cluttered beyond belief — shelves and shelves of ledgers and papers and jars and instruments, a workbench covered in springs and gears, a pot of something steaming on a little stove — and it smelled of oil and paper and algae soup. There were people in it. Four or five, in the long grey smocks he'd come to recognise as the Tollers', all of them turning to look at the door as it opened.

And in a chair beside the column, with one hand resting flat on the black stone, sat a very small, very old woman.

She was tiny. Even for Holdfast, even hunched in a chair, she was tiny — a little bent bundle of grey smock and white hair, her feet in felt slippers not reaching the floor. Her face was so wrinkled it looked like crumpled paper, and her hands were knotted like roots. But her eyes, when they came up to Cas's, were black and sharp and bright as a bird's, and they went over him from head to foot in about a second.

"You're the Relief?" said Perpetua Tally.

"So I'm told."

"You look like you need a sit-down." She jerked her chin at the Tollers. "Tobiah. Chair. Before he falls on my instruments."

A young man with round spectacles made of instrument lenses brought a chair. Cas sat in it. It was the most grateful he'd ever been for a chair in his life. Pim, behind him, had pulled the cart inside and was busy letting Steward Harrow down onto the floor, where the rat ran immediately to the old woman's slippers and sat up on them.

"Hello, Harrow," said Perpetua, without looking down. "You're getting fat." Her eyes stayed on Cas. "So. Thirty-three hours."

"She told you."

"Ione told me what you said. At the well." Perpetua's hand moved very slightly on the column, the way you might stroke an animal. "Thirty-three hours and a bit. That was what you said? *And a bit.*"

"Yes."

"Hmph." She turned her head. "Tobiah. Last Bell."

The young man with the lens-spectacles went to a ledger on a stand by the column — a vast, thick, leather-bound thing, its pages dense with columns of figures — and ran his finger down the latest page.

"Bell one hundred and nineteen thousand, four hundred and eighty-nine," he read. "Interval eleven minutes fifty. Our reckoning, Outside—" he did something quick with a slide-rule "—thirty-three hours, eleven minutes, and twenty-nine seconds."

"Thirty-three hours, eleven minutes, twenty-nine seconds," said Perpetua Tally to the Relief. "*And a bit.*" She sniffed. "They send us a man from Outside after a hundred and sixty-eight years, and he can't tell the time."

Cas stared at her.

"You knew," he said.

"Of course we knew." She lifted her hand from the column and waved it, a small impatient gesture, at the walls, the ledgers, the pendulums, the whole cluttered tower. "What did you think we were doing in here? Knitting?"

* * *

They had always known.

That was the thing she told him, over the next hour, in the round room at the foot of the tower, with the lamps burning and the soup going round in tin cups and the Tollers perched on stools and shelves listening. They had always known. Ruth Tally had known — Ruth, who'd been a shift engineer, who had pulled the hammer herself, who'd understood exactly what it was and what it meant. *One Bell, one second*, Ruth had told her children. *We are living faster than the world. The Outside is not burned. It is only slow.* And her children had told theirs. And for seven generations the Tallys and those who married into them and those they took on as apprentices had counted every Bell, and marked it, and measured it, and kept the count in secret, and known.

"Look at it," said Perpetua, and pointed up at the column. "Go on. Look properly."

He looked.

The white marks on the black stone were tallies. Strokes, in chalk and white paint and something scratched, in groups — he stood up, painfully, with Tobiah's arm under his, and went close — in groups of five, the fifth stroke across the four. Rows of them. Twelve groups to a row: sixty strokes. And every sixty rows, a long ruled line right round the column, and a number painted beside it.

*1.*

*2.*

*3.*

He tilted his head back and looked up the column. Up and up, spiralling round it, rows and rows and rows of tiny white strokes, and every so often a ruled line, and a number. He could see them going up into the shadows of the upper floors. *15. 16.* Up past the pendulums. *22. 23.*

"Outside Hours," said Perpetua. "Three thousand six hundred Bells. One hour, Outside. The first Hour took twenty-seven years. The second took twenty-three. The one we're in now will take about a month. Ruth marked the first one herself, with a nail, in the stone. Tobiah — show him."

Tobiah held up a lamp to a place near the bottom of the column, just above head height, where the stone was dark and smooth and polished by hands. There, among the chalk strokes, was a line that hadn't been chalked. It had been scratched. Deep, wavering, careful, a line gouged into keelstone — which nothing could scratch, Cas thought, and then realised that it had taken her years, years of going back to it and going over it and going over it with a nail, until it was deep enough to last.

Beside it, scratched the same way, a single word.

*MADDY*

"Her daughter," said Perpetua. "Outside. She was seven."

Cas couldn't speak.

"We've been counting her night," said Perpetua Tally. "All of us. For a hundred and sixty-eight years. That's what the Tower's for. Not the Bells. The Bells are just how you count it. We've been counting the night of a little girl called Maddy Tally who went to sleep in her gran's flat on the Outside and hasn't woken up yet." She put her hand back on the column. "Thirty-three hours. She'll be awake by now. She'll be wondering where her mam is."

The Bell came.

It rose up through the floor, up through the column under the old woman's hand, and the whole tower hummed with it — the pendulums trembling on their wires, the water in the carboys shivering into rings, the lamps' flames bowing — and it hung there, ringing, for longer than Cas could hold his breath. Every Toller in the room had gone still. They were counting. He could see it in their faces. Silently, all of them, the way people pray.

When it had faded, Tobiah went to the column with a stub of chalk and made a stroke.

* * *

"The Stewardship knows," said Perpetua. "Obviously. We send the true count to the First Steward every seventh round, and have done since Founder Crane. He knows the Outside's alive. They've all known. They keep it quiet because the Founder's Word says the Outside burned, and if the Outside didn't burn then what are we doing here, and why haven't we gone, and who told us to stay?" She sipped her soup. "Holdfast isn't built on the Watch, Relief. It's built on not asking that question."

"They let people count the Bells," said Cas slowly.

"Oh, they *let* people count the Bells. There's the Public Tally in the Commons, big as a house, you'll have seen it. Bell number, interval. Everyone knows the Bells are quickening. You can't hide it. You feel it in your teeth every twelve minutes." She put down her cup. "Let them count the Bells. Nobody ever died of counting. It's drawing the line that does it."

"The line."

"Tobiah."

Tobiah brought a book. A big, flat, cloth-bound ledger, very old, and he opened it on the workbench under a lamp and turned it round so Cas could see. It was a graph. Hand-drawn, in ink, on paper ruled in faint squares, and it went on across the page and over onto the next and the next — a hundred and sixty-eight years of it, a column of intervals down the side and a row of Bell numbers along the bottom — and it was a curve.

He knew it the instant he saw it. It was the curve he'd drawn himself on the backs of envelopes, fifteen years ago, at night, in his flat. It fell away from the top of the page steeply at first, and then less steeply, and then less, a long smooth swoop sliding down toward the bottom of the page — Bell intervals of three days, then a day, then hours, then minutes — never quite reaching the bottom, always getting closer.

He took the sketchbook out of his coat. His hands were shaking a little. He found a clean page, and a pencil, and did the fit — three points, then five, then ten, off the Tollers' ledger — and worked the numbers, and there it was. An exponential. A decay, as clean as a textbook, with a time constant of — he converted it — five and a half, six hours. Outside hours.

"That's the debt," he said. "That's it coming due. That's the hill coming down."

"We don't know what it is," said Perpetua. "We know what it *does*."

"And where it goes?" He looked up. "Have you drawn it forward?"

There was a small silence in the tower.

"Seren drew it forward," said Perpetua.

* * *

She told it quickly. She didn't dress it. He thought afterwards that she had probably told it that way, quickly and plainly, many times, to herself.

Seren Tally had been Perpetua's daughter. A Toller, like her mother, but a better one: a genius with numbers, with a mind that could hold a curve and see where it was going. Seren had drawn the line forward. Seren had worked out that the Bells would come faster and faster, and at the rate they were going, sooner or later — in her daughter's lifetime — would come so fast they'd run together into a single note. She'd measured the Lace with the sighting tube at the top of the tower, round after round for ten years, and found it was coming down: not steadily, but faster every year. She'd worked out that it would reach the Rim — the ring round the Lamp at the spindles' tops — before the Bells ran together. And she'd guessed, without knowing why, that when it reached the Rim, that would be the end of everything.

"She took it to the First Steward," said Perpetua. "Halloran. He'd not been Steward long. He thanked her very kindly and told her it must not be spoken. She told him that people had a right to know how long they had. He told her that people had a right to be calm." She turned her cup in her knotted hands. "She painted it on the wall of the Commons. The line, and the date. Big. In the night, with Ione holding the ladder. Ione was fifteen."

"And they—"

"They gave her to the Drop."

Nobody in the room moved.

"It's what they do," said Perpetua, "for the ones who won't keep the Watch. It's very old. Founder Crane did it. It's not an execution. It's a *release*. You're free to go. Down the well. Nobody's ever stopped at the bottom." She said it flatly. "Halloran signed it. I suppose he had to. He came to see me, after. He sat in that chair you're in and he cried." She looked at Cas with her black bird's eyes. "I let him. I thought he'd earned it. It's the only thing I've ever given him."

Cas thought of the body at the foot of the conduit. The patched, mended clothes. The little yellow lamp worn smooth by fingers.

"When?" he said.

"Thirteen years ago."

He did the arithmetic. He couldn't stop himself. Thirteen years ago the Bells had come once every five or six hours; the ratio had been twenty thousand, not seven hundred. He ran it back along the Tollers' curve. Nineteen hours, outside. Nineteen hours before he'd climbed the ladder in 14-C. She had gone down the Drop at about the time he'd been in a hangar in the Collar, learning to walk in a keel suit.

"I think I saw her," he said.

Perpetua looked at him.

"At the bottom of the conduit," he said. "There was — someone. In mended clothes. With a lamp. Carved from a hard hat."

"Yellow," said Perpetua. Her voice had changed.

"Yes."

The old woman sat without moving. Then she took her hand off the column and put both her hands in her lap, and looked down at them, and her face did something he wasn't ready for. It crumpled — the whole of it, the paper-crumple of it, all at once — and her mouth opened, and she made a small, dry, high sound, like a bird. Tobiah was beside her at once, kneeling by her chair. She didn't look at him. She sat there with her hands in her lap and made that small sound, over and over, and the tears ran down the creases of her face and dripped off her chin onto the grey smock.

"I gave her that," said Perpetua Tally. "I carved it. When she was a baby. For her crib."

* * *

It was Tobiah who brought out the Log, in the end. Perpetua had gone quiet, sitting with her eyes closed and her hand back on the column, and nobody had spoken, and then Tobiah had got up and gone to a shelf and taken down a box — a heavy steel box with a Works stencil on its side, very old, very dented — and put it on the workbench and opened it.

Inside, wrapped in layer on layer of cloth, was a book.

It was a Ministry logbook. He knew the type: A4, hard-backed, blue cloth, with the Continuance flame stamped on the cover and HOLLOWELL DRAW — CROWN — SHIFT LOG in faded gold. The station had issued them by the thousand. This one was swollen to twice its thickness, crammed with extra pages sewn in, bristling with slips of paper, its spine cracked and mended with thread, its corners worn to round. It looked like it had been opened ten thousand times.

"Ruth's," said Tobiah quietly. "She had it in her hand in the lift. She didn't know she'd picked it up."

Cas opened it.

The first pages were the ordinary Crown log for the night of the test, in a neat engineer's pencil. *01:50 Dep. Min. Crane + crew arr. for Deepening footage. 01:52 Advised Dep. Min. of T6 bearing temp (+11). Advised protocol requires Chief Eng. Advised interlocks.* He could see her in the pencil: the neatness, the care, the way she wrote *advised*. *01:58 Dir. Obi has deferred to Ministry. Proceeding under protest. Note: under protest.*

Then a gap. A blank line.

Then, in the same pencil but a different hand — a hand that shook — *02:05 T6 unseated at Draw 135. Boundary failure. Interlocks failed. Draw would not zero. Time grad. across Crown, severe. Attempted Hollowell Clause. Dep. Min. Crane had the key and would not give it. At 02:09 approx gantry failed, key lost, fell out through window glass into the Lesion as if it were called. Clause not possible. 02:14 abandoned Crown. Pulled the Hammer.*

And under that, very small:

*I did it. I said 135. It was me.*

He turned the page.

After that, it was a different book. The log format was gone. The pencil went on, but it was a diary now, a history, a record. The first days in the Vault. The Drop — the first men who'd tried the conduits, and what had come back up. Aldous Crane under the Placard. The Sealing. Names, lists of names, the living and the dead. Rations. Water. The algae. The first birth. Page after page, and the handwriting getting smaller and smaller as the paper ran out, until it was so small he had to hold the book up to the lamp to read it, and in places the pages were sewn in from other things: the backs of forms, the insides of packets, a sheet of pale thin stuff that looked like it had been made from algae pulp. The years went by in the margins. *Year 3. Year 7. Year 20, Bell 2,560.* He turned pages and turned pages. It was Ruth Tally's whole life. It was all there.

And there were letters.

They came every few pages, all through the book, set off from the rest with a line drawn across the page. Every one of them began the same way.

*Maddy —*

He read the first one. It was dated *Day 4*.

*Maddy — I don't know if you'll ever read this. I'm going to write it anyway because otherwise I'll go mad. Mum is in a place a very long way away and I can't come home yet. It isn't because I don't want to. There's a big light here and it's got stuck and I have to help. I'm sorry I didn't say goodbye properly. I had to go to work early. You were asleep with your feet out of the covers. I tucked them in. Did you notice? Probably not. Be good for Nana. I love you more than the whole Draw. — Mum.*

He turned pages. *Maddy — today we planted the first beans.* *Maddy — the Bell only comes every three days now, and I've started to feel like it's your breathing.* *Maddy — I got married today. His name is Tomas and he works the Anvil pumps and he makes me laugh. I know that's strange. I hope you don't mind. You'd like him.* *Maddy — you have a brother. I've called him Hal.* *Maddy — twenty years. For you it's about forty minutes. I've done the sum a thousand times. You're still asleep. I lie awake and think about you asleep, and it's the only thing that helps.*

One entry, near the middle of the book — *Year 31* — wasn't a letter. It was set apart from the rest by a line above and below, and it was short, and he read it twice without understanding it.

*I have never told anyone this. At 02:09, after the key fell, I saw a man at the Keystone. Kneeling where I knelt. Dressed close, in grey, like a diver. A cord knotted round his wrist. His hand on a wheel that isn't there. He looked at me as if he knew me. He was so calm. Then there was no one. I have thought about him every day for thirty-one years. I think he was the witness. I think, whoever he is, he is still there.*

He turned the page. There were more letters.

He couldn't read any more. He sat with the book open on his knees and the lamp burning and his eyes blurred.

Then he turned to the last page.

The pencil was almost too faint to read. The hand was an old woman's hand, shaking badly, the letters large and wandering because the eyes that made them couldn't see small any more.

*Maddy — I am eighty-eight. Year 50. Bell 7,100. For you it is about two hours, so it's still the middle of the night, and you've probably turned over and pushed the covers off again.*

*I've had a whole life here, baby. It was a good one, mostly. I had your brothers and your sister and I had Tomas for forty years and I have eleven grandchildren and they count the Bells for me now because my eyes are gone. I made a lot of mistakes. The biggest one was the first one. I'm sorry for it every day.*

*I want you to know I thought about you every single day. Every one. Nineteen thousand days, give or take. I never once let myself forget you were there, asleep, in the same night. It was the thing that held me up.*

*If someone ever brings you this — and they will; I've told them to — then you'll be seven, and I'll have been gone a long time, and that's all right. Don't be sad. I had my life. You go and have yours. Be brave. Be kind. Tell the truth, even when it's hard. Especially then.*

*Love you more than the whole Draw.*

*Mum.*

He closed the book.

"She's seven," he said. He didn't mean to say it aloud. He heard it come out of him. "She's still seven. Right now. It's the day after Continuance Day. It's — she'll have had her breakfast. She's seven."

"Yes," said Perpetua Tally. She had opened her eyes. She was looking at him steadily across the lamplit room with her wet, bright, black eyes. "We know, Relief. We've always known. What we didn't know — what we never had — was anyone who'd *seen* it."

She reached out, with a knotted old hand, and touched his knee.

"Thank you," she said. "You've no idea. Thank you."

* * *

It was then that Ione came down from the roof.

He heard her before he saw her — a light, quick tread on the spiral stair that ran up round the column, coming down fast from the upper floors — and then she swung round the last turn and dropped the last few steps and was in the room. She had a rope over her shoulder and a notebook in her hand and chalk on her fingers, and she stopped at the foot of the stair and looked round at all of them: at Perpetua with her wet face and Tobiah kneeling, and the Tollers on their stools, and Pim with his rat, and Cas with the Log on his knees.

"You've read it," she said.

"Yes."

"Good." She came across the room and crouched in front of his chair so that her face was level with his, the way you'd crouch to talk to a child, or to someone sick. She looked at him for a moment with that unhurried, attentive gaze. Then she said: "The Lamp can be closed. There's a lock in the Crown. I have its key."

And she lifted the cord from round her neck and held out her hand.

The key lay on her palm.

It was black. It was the length of a finger. It had a flat bow and a straight shaft and a blunt, cross-shaped bit, and it was dense — he could see it was dense, it sat in her hand heavier than a thing that size had any right to — and it didn't reflect the lamplight. It took the light in and gave nothing back. Along the shaft, stamped into it in small square letters:

:: HOLLOWELL · KEYSTONE · 0001

He'd seen it in a mural, fifteen storeys high. He'd seen it in a pencil log, in the shaking hand of a woman who'd watched it fall. He'd seen it, he thought, somewhere else too — a picture in the training manual, a photograph in the back of Hollowell's withdrawn paper: *the Minister's key*. There had only ever been one.

"That's not possible," he said.

"No," agreed Ione.

"It fell. Into the Lesion. Ruth *saw* it." He heard his voice rising. "Your Founder — it's on a *wall*, it's on a wall fifteen storeys high—"

"I know."

"Then where did you *get* it?"

"I found it," said Ione.

She said it simply. Then she sat back on her heels and looked at the key in her hand, and a little of the unhurried patience went out of her face, and something else came into it. Something older. More careful.

"When I was seventeen," she said, "I went up. Past where anyone goes. And I got lost, up there, in a place called the Carousel. I'll tell you about it one day. It's not a story for tonight." She turned the key over. "There's a gantry in the Carousel. A walkway, across a gap. I walked across it — I don't know. Four thousand times? More? I knew every bolt in it. I knew every mark on it. And one time I walked across it and there was a key lying on it. Right in the middle. Where it had never been, not one time in four thousand. And then it was."

Cas stared at her.

"I picked it up," said Ione. "I've had it ever since."

The floor of the round room seemed to tilt under his chair. It wasn't the weight. It was something in his head. He was thinking of a paper he'd read as a student, an old, strange, half-joking paper from before the Long Heat, that his professor had assigned as a curiosity: two physicists asking what would happen if a time machine were real, and whether an object could go round a loop in time and come back to where it started. They'd called it a jinn. An object with no maker. An object that existed because it existed. That was never made, and never destroyed, and simply went round.

*You could find one lying on a walkway*, his professor had joked, *where it had never been. And you'd have no way to know where it came from. Because it didn't come from anywhere.*

"A jinn," he said.

"A what?"

"Nothing." He didn't take his eyes off the key. "Nothing. A word. Go on."

"There isn't any more," said Ione. "Except that I knew what it was, because I'd been watching the Crown. In the Carousel, you can see the Crown. The Crown on the night of the Kindling. For three years, I watched it." She looked up at him. "I watched Ruth try to open the lock under the floor. I watched Founder Crane refuse to give her the key. I watched the walkway go. I saw the key on his chain. I know the lock. I know the plate over it. I could draw you the lock with my eyes shut. And I came down, two days later, with this in my hand, and I told them all: *the Lamp can be closed*. There's a lock in the Crown and this is the key and if we get it up there we can put the Lamp out."

"And they called you mad," said Cas.

"They called me Unwatched." She shrugged very slightly. "It means the same."

"She's not mad," said Perpetua, from her chair. Her voice was hoarse but perfectly steady. "She's the only one of us who's ever *been* anywhere."

Cas looked at the key. Then at Ione's face. Then at the key.

"She isn't mad," he said.

Every head in the room turned.

"I've read the paper that lock was built from," he said. "The woman who built this place — Hollowell — she wrote it. The last thing she wrote before she died. The Ministry buried it. It's called the Clause. It's a way to seal the core — the Lamp — if it ever broke the way it's broken. Fold it shut. For ever. It's in the Crown, under a plate in the floor." He looked at Ione. "There are words on the plate."

"*Hollowell Clause*," said Ione at once, softly, as if reciting. "*For use in catastrophic boundary failure only. The sealing requires a witness.*"

"You've read it."

"I've read it over Ruth's shoulder a hundred and seventy-five thousand times." She held his eyes. "Do you know what it means? The last part?"

"No," said Cas. "Nobody does."

She nodded slowly, as though that too were something she had half expected.

* * *

"Why haven't you gone?" he said, later.

It was very late. The Tollers had drifted back to their instruments, or to sleep on pallets in the upper floors. Perpetua was dozing in her chair with her hand on the column and Steward Harrow asleep in her lap. Pim was asleep on the floor under the workbench, curled round nothing. The lamps had burned low. Ione sat cross-legged on the floor with her back against a shelf of ledgers, turning the key over and over in her fingers, and Cas sat in his chair with the Log on his knees because he could not bring himself to put it down.

"I've tried," said Ione. "Twice. After I came back. I went up again at nineteen and again at twenty-three. By the south spindles, both times. Not by the Carousel. I wasn't—" A pause, very slight. "I wasn't going back through the Carousel." She turned the key. "There's a place, above the Lees, before the Rim, where the Lace comes down. It's lower every year. You have to go through it to get to the top of Spindle One. And the Lace kills you."

"How?"

"The way it killed my mother, in the Drop." She said it without any particular weight. "Everything goes different speeds. You come apart. I watched a rat go into it once, on a rope. It went in a rat and came out—" she made a small gesture "—dust, and a bit of fur, and something still moving. I turned back both times. You can't get through the Lace without something to hold you together." She looked at the grey suit standing in the corner where Pim had helped him prop it, the keel humming quietly on its back. "I've been watching the wells for eleven years."

"Why?"

"Because once, up there, when I was seventeen, I saw someone coming down." She looked at him, and her gaze was very direct. "Far off. In a suit like that. With a thing on its back, humming. Coming down alone." She tipped her head at the corner. "That suit. Or one like it. So I knew one would come. Sooner or later. Up out of the floor." She almost smiled. "You took your time."

He didn't know what to say to that. He found that he was thinking — in the part of his mind that never stopped doing sums — *someone in a keel suit coming down alone. From the Crown. In the future, seen from the past.* And under that, a cold slow thought he didn't want to look at: *there are only three suits*.

"You're the first thing to come up through the floor in my lifetime," said Ione, "that could get through the Lace."

"And you've got the key."

"And I've got the key."

"And you know the way."

"I know the way," said Ione, "as far as the Lace. And I know the Crown. Every inch of it. I know which panel sticks and which lamp was flickering and where Ruth put her coffee cup down. And I know the lock." She held up the key between two fingers. "You know what the lock does. You know the paper. You know what it *means*. I've watched it for three years and I don't know what it means."

"Neither do I. Not all of it."

"More than me."

"More than you."

She looked at him. He looked at her. Neither of them said anything.

"Spindle Four," she said at last. "It's dead. Nobody uses it, nobody watches it. The Stewards think it's cursed because the lift fell in the Founders' time. It goes all the way up. I'll need to go up it first, on my own, and look at the Lees. They move. They've moved since I was up there. Everything's moved. And you—" She looked at him critically. "You need to be able to stand up."

"How long?"

"For the Lees? Ten rounds. Twenty. For you to stand properly—" She considered. "More."

"We don't have more."

"No," said Ione Tally. "We don't."

* * *

She took him up to the top of the tower before dawn.

It took a long time. He had to stop on every landing. She waited on every landing, not helping unless he asked, which he didn't, and then did, and she put her shoulder under his arm without comment and took most of his weight up the last three flights as though he were a bag of rope.

At the top was the platform with the long tube on its swivel. A sighting tube, she said. Seren had built it. It was pointed up at the sky at a steep angle, sighted on the top of Spindle Two, and there were marks scratched on the brass ring at its base — dozens of them, hundreds, each with a small number beside it.

"Look," said Ione.

He bent to the eyepiece. Through the tube he saw the top of Spindle Two, very far off, a dark lattice against the glare, and the Rim beyond it, a dark line. And coming down out of the sky above it, the long bright trailing threads of the Lace, like the tendrils of some vast drifting creature. There were crosshairs in the tube — two fine wires. The lowest tendril hung just below the crossing.

"Every round, at dawn, I sight the lowest thread," said Ione, "and mark it."

She showed him yesterday's mark on the brass ring, and the day before's, and the one before. He could see the pattern without her explaining. The marks crept down the ring. Close together, years ago. Further apart. Now each one was a clear finger's width below the last.

He put his eye back to the tube and looked at the gap between the lowest thread and the dark line of the Rim. He looked at the marks. He did the sum, and then he did it again with the marks spreading the way they were spreading. Sixty rounds. A few more, perhaps. Perhaps less.

Then, because he couldn't not, he turned it into his own time. A month of Holdfast to every hour outside, Perpetua had said, and the months getting shorter. Sixty rounds in here was about two and a half hours out there. Hour thirty-five. Thirty-six. He had stood in a room with gold carpet and told the Minister of Continuance *two days, three at the outside*, and the Outside's clock had been sitting at hour thirty-three when he climbed out of the well. The debt wasn't coming at the end of the week. It was coming this afternoon.

And in here, it was two months away.

He stood with his eye to the brass and understood, properly, for the first time, the one thing in this place that was on his side. Out there, on the Collar's clock, he'd have had a couple of hours to climb four kilometres and open a lock that had been shut for a hundred and sixty-eight years. In here, every round he spent — learning to stand, learning the Lees, learning anything — cost the world outside about two minutes.

It wasn't much time. It was more than anyone else was ever going to get.

Behind them, down in the city, the horn blew from the Steward's House for first shift. And all across Holdfast, in a great clattering rolling wave that started at the centre and spread outward to the edges, the shutters came up, and the violet light poured into ten thousand windows, and the city woke.

Ione took a nail from her pocket and scratched a new mark on the brass ring. A finger's width below yesterday's.

"There," she said. "Now you know how long."
