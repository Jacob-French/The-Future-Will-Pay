---
title: Keel
---

> The keel is not a shield. It will not stop the water. It will keep you upright in it.
> — MSE Type 4 Operator's Handbook (draft, unpublished), Tallow Works Laboratory

The suits arrived at the Collar at a quarter to eleven, on the back of a Civil Defence flatbed, lashed upright in their cradles under grey tarpaulins, so that as the truck came slowly down the ramp into the Gate Nine maintenance hangar they looked like three tall figures being brought to an execution with sacks over their heads.

A young man was sitting on the flatbed with them, holding on to one of the cradles with both arms. When the truck stopped he didn't let go. He stayed there, hugging the cradle, while the driver got out and the hangar crew came forward with the forklift, and he looked round the hangar with enormous eyes — at the Calm officers by the door, at the Civil Defence people, at Teo Lisk with her arms folded, at Cas — and said, to no one in particular, very fast:

"Hi. Hello. I'm Ben. Benedek. Farr. I'm the — I built — well, I didn't *build* them, a lot of people built them, but I built the keels, I mean I did the rotor bearings, and the housings, and the spin controllers, and most of the firmware, and I'm the only one left in the lab who still knows how they go together, because everyone else was reassigned when the programme got cut, and I wasn't, because nobody remembered I was there, which was, honestly, the best four years of my life. And someone phoned the lab at seven o'clock this morning and said you need three people to go into the Draw, and I said who's going to maintain the suits, and they said well, who *can*, and I said, well, me, I suppose, and they said right then." He took a breath. "So I'm coming. I'm coming? I'm coming. Right. Good. Fine." Another breath. "Is there a form?"

Teo Lisk walked over to the flatbed, looked up at him, and held out a hand.

"Come down, son," she said. "There's always a form."

* * *

He was twenty-six. He had a narrow, clever, anxious face, and hair that stood up at the back where he'd been running his hands through it, and he talked — Cas would learn over the next twenty hours — the way a pressure valve leaks: continuously, and more when the pressure went up. When he was working, though, he went quiet. When he stripped the tarpaulin off the first cradle and put his hand flat on the suit inside it, he stopped talking altogether, and stood there for a moment as though he were checking for a pulse.

They were not beautiful. Cas had half expected them to be. They were squat and grey and massive, armoured in overlapping matte plates like the shell of some deep-sea crustacean, with thick ribbed joints at knee and hip and elbow and a helmet that was mostly visor, a wide dark curve of glass. On each back, where a diver would carry tanks, sat a rounded housing the size of a beer keg, sealed and bolted, with a band of small indicator lights around its waist. Someone had painted a number on each housing by hand in white paint: *1*, *2*, *3*. The paint on the *3* had run a little before it dried.

"That's the keel," said Ben. He had his hand on the housing of number one. "Inside there's a rotor. Keelstone. Eleven kilos of it, in a disc about so big." He made a circle with his hands the size of a dinner plate. "Two millimetres thick. Doesn't sound like much. It isn't the weight, it's what it does when you spin it. Keelstone's — you know what keelstone is?"

"Collapsed matter," said Cas. "Pressure-stabilised by the Lattice fields when it's made. About ten times denser than lead."

"Right, right, sorry, you'd know, you're the — sorry." Ben flushed. "So the rotor spins. Fast. Really, really fast. Two hundred thousand revs a minute at full, and the rim's doing — well, you don't want to know what the rim's doing, the housing's rated for it but only just. And when you spin something that dense that fast, it drags spacetime round with it. Frame-dragging. Tiny effect normally. With a keelstone rotor it's not tiny. It gives you a sort of bubble, about a metre across, where the metric's — smoothed. Stirred flat. Like putting a paddle in a river so the water round you goes one way instead of every way at once." He looked at Teo, then at Cas, then back at the suit, and spoke to the suit. "In the Lattice, where the gradients are — you know. If you walk into a region where your feet weigh more than your head, or where your feet are living faster than your head, the bubble spreads it out. Averages it over the metre. So instead of being torn in half, you're just — very uncomfortable."

"How uncomfortable?" said Teo.

Ben considered this seriously. "Have you ever been in a car crash?"

"Three."

"Like the fourth one, but slower."

Teo grunted. It might have been a laugh.

"The bubble's only about a metre. Two people could share one if they were—" Ben made a small squeezing gesture with his hands "—closer than is professional. The suits are built for one. They're heavy. They've got exoskeletons, the exo will carry most of it, but you'll feel like you're wearing a cathedral. Power cells for the life support and the exo and the electronics are good for about four days. The keel doesn't need power once it's spinning. The bearings are magnetic, there's almost no friction — sat on the rig with nothing to push against, it'd spin for months. It's the *work* that slows it. Every time the bubble has to hold something steep, it costs spin. Hold a lot, and it runs down fast. As it slows, the bubble shrinks. You'll hear it. It hums. When the hum drops, that's you running out of keel."

"And spinning it up," said Cas, "takes—"

"Fourteen hours." Ben winced. "Sorry. It's a lot of mass. If you try to spin it faster you crack the housing, and then you've got eleven kilos of keelstone doing two hundred thousand revs on your back with nothing to hold it, and that's—" He stopped. "That's a bad day. That's a very, very bad day."

Cas looked up at the clock on the hangar wall. It said 10:58. His wristwatch said 10:51. The hangar was a kilometre inside the Collar; the time out here was already slipping.

Fourteen hours. They'd go in before dawn. Hour twenty-five, or thereabouts. He tried to think of what the hour-twenty-five version of the Vault might be like, if the ratio was what he thought it was, and decided not to.

* * *

It took Ben and the hangar crew until one o'clock to get the suits out of the cradles and onto the spin rigs — three low steel frames bolted to the hangar floor, humming with power cables — and when the rotors finally started to turn it was so gradual that at first there was no way to tell. Then, gradually, there was a sound. A long, low, rising tone, so deep you felt it in your back teeth before you heard it, the three suits singing in almost-unison under the hangar lights. The note crept upward very slowly. Ben said it would keep rising all night.

Cas stood and listened to it. He would hear that note for the rest of his life.

Across the hangar, by the big doors, Teo Lisk was talking to Ansel Gaunt.

Cas couldn't hear them. The hum and the hammer between them drowned it out. Gaunt was doing the talking, softly, with his head bent and his hands folded in front of him like a priest, and Teo was listening with her arms crossed and her face shut. At one point Gaunt took something small out of his cardigan pocket and held it out to her, and after a moment she took it and put it in the breast pocket of her fatigues. Then Gaunt patted her arm, and went out, and Teo stood there alone in the doorway looking out at the dark mass of the Works for a while before she came back.

When she passed Cas, she didn't meet his eye.

He filed it. He had spent fifteen years watching people not meet his eye; he'd got good at filing.

* * *

The afternoon was drills.

They put the suits on for the first time at three o'clock, with the keels barely turning, and Cas found out what Ben had meant by a cathedral. You didn't so much put on a keel suit as get admitted into it. The liner came first — a close grey one-piece in some slippery, cool fabric, wired with sensors and a web of fine tubes for heat and water, that fitted like a diver's wetsuit and made all three of them look, as Teo put it, like very depressed seals. Then the suit opened down the back like a clam, and you stepped in, and it closed around you with a series of heavy locking thunks, and for a moment you could not move at all, and then the exoskeleton woke up and took your weight and you stood up inside it and the world became a narrow bright picture framed in a dark curve of glass.

It was slow. Everything was slow. The exo took a quarter-second to decide what your legs meant before it did it; walking felt like wading. On his back the keel's hum came through the suit's frame into his spine like a second, larger heartbeat. In his ears, the helmet's speakers carried Teo swearing, steadily and inventively, as she tried to pick up a spanner in her gauntlet and sent it skittering across the floor for the ninth time.

"Don't grab," said Ben's voice. "It's got a grip-assist. It'll close harder than you mean it to. Just think about touching it."

"I *am* thinking about touching it." Teo bent — the whole suit bent, with a long hydraulic sigh — and closed her gauntlet on the spanner for the tenth time, very slowly, like a woman lifting a sleeping cat.

There was a short, flat crunch.

She straightened up and held it out to them. The spanner had folded in the middle like a sandwich.

"…Or that," said Ben. "That's the grip-assist. That's — yes. It's quite a lot of grip."

"Is this coming out of my pay?"

"It's the Ministry's. I really don't think they're counting spanners today." He was quiet for a second, and then, because he apparently couldn't stop himself: "Everybody does one. I did a coffee mug. And a technician's hand, a bit. He was fine. He was mostly fine."

Teo looked at the folded spanner. Then she laid it down on the floor, very carefully, as if it might go off, and picked up the next one as though it were an egg. It stayed a spanner.

"There," said Ben, so relieved that Cas nearly smiled.

They walked the length of the hangar and back. They climbed a ladder and came down it. They practised falling — the suits went over like felled trees and got up again with a hydraulic grunt — and practised hauling each other up, and practised the hand signals for when the radios failed, which Ben said they would, sooner or later.

"The electronics hate gradients," he said. "If a chip's got one end living faster than the other end, its clocks go out of sync and it just — it gets confused. The keel helps, it smooths it over the bubble, but the bubble's a metre and the Lattice is — well. The electronics will go first. The radios, the displays, the recorders. The life support's mostly mechanical, the exo's got a manual fallback, the keel's purely mechanical once it's spun. So you'll keep breathing and walking. You just won't be able to *talk* about it."

"Sounds like working for the Commonweal," said Teo.

Cas found that he'd laughed. It came out of him in the suit, surprised, and he heard it come back to him over the radio, and he realised he couldn't remember the last time he had laughed out loud at something another person had said. He couldn't remember the last time there had been another person.

Teo glanced across at him through two layers of visor. He couldn't see her expression. But she didn't say anything else for a while.

* * *

At seven in the evening they ate.

The Collar canteen had been evacuated at three in the morning in the middle of the night-shift meal, and nobody had been back. It was a long, low room with windows onto the Works, and the tables were still set: trays of food gone cold and stiff, cups with skins on them, a half-finished game of cards laid out on a table by the door. Somebody had been celebrating a birthday. There was a cake on the counter with most of its candles still in it and a paper crown lying beside it, and a card propped against the cake that said *HAPPY 40TH OSWIN — THE FUTURE WILL PAY (FOR THE CAKE)*.

Nobody touched the cake.

They found tins in the storeroom and heated them in the kitchen and ate at the table by the window, still in their liners, with the Works outside going on and on in the dark — cooling towers, gantries, the black cliff of a rectifier hall with red warning lights crawling across it — and the shimmer standing over all of it in the floodlights, and the hammer, and the hum of the keels from the hangar.

Ben had a napkin. He'd been drawing on it since the soup.

"Right," he said. "Right. So I've been doing the numbers. On our survival odds."

"Oh, good," said Teo.

"No, listen, it's interesting. So. Okay. Let's say the Works is about as bad as the drone said, which means the gravity's doing weird things but the keels can handle it. Let's say ninety percent for the Works. That's generous but let's say. Then the Lattice, we don't know, but the keels were *designed* for the Lattice, so let's say seventy. Then whatever's past the Lattice—"

"The Shear," said Cas.

Ben looked up. "The what?"

"If the core potential's as high as I think, the Lattice is holding the whole difference between the core's time and ours. Where the Lattice is solid, the difference sits inside the keelstone, and that's fine; nobody's walking through keelstone. But there are holes in it. Conduits, cooling trunks — the ways in. In a hole, the whole difference has to happen in the air. Over a few tens of metres, maybe. The time rate changing very fast over a very short distance." He moved his hand, flat, across the table. "A shear layer. Like the boundary between two currents."

Ben thought about that. He wrote something on the napkin. "Okay. The Shear. Let's say fifty. For the Shear. And then the Vault, and then the Crown, and then whatever you do in the Crown—" He looked at Cas again, and something in his face was very young. "What *are* you going to do in the Crown, Dr Hale? Reseat Six? Can you actually—"

"Keep going with your sum, Ben."

Ben looked at him a moment longer. Then he looked down and kept going. "So — the Crown, let's call it fifty, fifty for whatever. And then we have to get *back*, and that's all of it again, in reverse, so you square it." He did a fast calculation. "Which comes out at about — two percent. Each. Roughly. I mean, that's very rough, the error bars are — well, the error bars are basically the whole napkin."

There was a short silence.

"Two percent," said Teo.

"Roughly."

"Well." She pushed her plate away and sat back and put her boots up on the chair beside her. "That's better than Tessarine."

"What was Tessarine?" said Ben.

So she told them. The Tessarine floods, Year Seventy-Nine, the spring the Canopy failed over the southern delta for eleven hours and the storm surge came up the river mouth and put six metres of water through the lower tiers of the city in forty minutes. She'd been a lieutenant. Her unit had gone into the Tier Two underpass with ropes and a rubber boat to get people out of a flooded Line station, and the water had kept rising while they were down there, and they'd had to swim out through the ticket hall in the pitch dark with the current pulling at them and twenty-three people on the ropes behind them, and they'd come out into the street and the street had been a river.

"I did the numbers on that one too," she said. "Afterwards. On the odds. It came out at about one in a hundred for the unit. We lost two." She shrugged. "Numbers are for afterwards. You go in, you do the job, you do the numbers in the pub, and then you get very drunk."

"Did you get any of them out?" Ben asked. "The people?"

"Twenty-one." She looked at the window. "Two of them let go."

Ben folded his napkin in half, and in half again, and put it in the pocket of his liner.

It was quiet for a while. The hammer came up through the table. The coffee in their cups shivered into rings, and settled, and shivered.

"Why are you here, Hale?" said Teo.

He looked at her. She was watching him over the rim of her cup, perfectly relaxed, and her eyes were not relaxed at all.

"I'm the Standby."

"I know what you are. I've read your file. Gaunt gave me your file." She didn't look away. "I asked why."

He thought about it. He tried to think of an answer that was true and that he could say out loud in front of a twenty-six-year-old boy with a two-percent napkin in his pocket, and there wasn't one.

"Because it's my job," he said.

Teo held his eyes for a second longer. Then she snorted softly and looked back out of the window.

"That's what," she said. "Not why. But all right."

* * *

Afterwards, there were the letters.

Nobody said they were going to do it. It just happened, the way these things did. Ben went off to a corner of the hangar with his tablet propped against a toolbox and sat on the floor in front of it and talked to it for a long time, very quietly. Cas didn't try to listen, but the hangar echoed, and every now and then a phrase came across to him between the hum and the hammer. *— and the flat's in both names so you won't have to —* and *— your mum's recipe, I know I always said it was too salty —* and then, near the end, his voice breaking and going on anyway, *— Jonah, I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm really sorry, I should've said yes to the dog.*

Teo wrote. She sat at a workbench with a Civil Defence notepad and a pencil and wrote two letters in a small square hand, and folded them, and wrote a name on each: *Mattias. Joss.* She put them in an envelope and wrote on the envelope, and put it in her breast pocket, next to the small thing Gaunt had given her.

Cas sat at the other end of the bench with a sheet of paper in front of him for an hour and wrote nothing.

He tried. He tried to write to Sabine and got as far as her name and couldn't think what came after it that she didn't already know. He tried to write to Nell and found that everything he wanted to say to her was either a lie or so large that it didn't fit on paper. In the end he folded the blank sheet up and put it in his pocket, and took out instead the thing he'd brought from the flat without really thinking about it — a battered hardback sketchbook with a black cloth cover, a quarter full of old equations and older drawings — and turned it over in his hands.

Then he went and found a phone.

* * *

Sabine answered on the eighth ring.

"Cas." A pause. Behind her, a lot of noise: voices, children, a loudspeaker saying something about blankets. "Where are you? I've been trying your — they said your number was—"

"I'm at work."

A silence. He could hear her understanding it.

"At *work*."

"Where are you? What's that noise?"

"We're in a school. A sports hall. Tier Thirty. They came round the whole of Nine at lunchtime and said we were being relocated for our comfort. For our *comfort*, Cas. They said it was a precaution. There's four hundred people in here and one toilet." Her voice dropped. "Everybody's being very calm. It's horrible. Nobody's saying anything and everybody keeps flinching. Cas, what is that? That noise. That — thing. It's been going since two in the morning. Is it the Draw? Is it—"

"Is Nell there?"

"Cas—"

"Please, Sabine."

A breath. Then, away from the phone: "Nellie. Nell. It's Dad."

A scuffle, a clatter, and then Nell's voice, very close and very loud, the way she always talked on the phone, as if she thought she had to shout to make it reach.

"Dad! Dad, we're in a *gym*!"

"I heard."

"There's a girl here called Pip who's got a lizard. A real one. It's in a box. It's called Dennis." A breath. "Dad, are you at the Draw?"

"Yes."

"Is it broken?"

He closed his eyes.

"Yes," he said. "It's broken."

She was quiet for a second. She was ten. She had her mother's grey eyes and his own inability to leave a question alone, and she collected stars. She had never seen one. No one her age in Averna had. The city's light and the Canopy's glow had wiped them from the sky before she was born, before he was born, and she had got interested in them anyway, the way some children get interested in dinosaurs — because they were enormous and far away and gone. She had glow-in-the-dark stars on her bedroom ceiling in their proper constellations, placed with a chart and a ruler, and she knew their names the way other children knew football teams. Betelgeuse. Deneb. Fomalhaut. She said them like spells.

"Pip's brother says," she said carefully, "that if the Draw is broken, the lights will all go off. Like, *all* of them. Everywhere."

"They might."

"So we'd see them."

It took him a second. "The stars?"

"If the lights all went off. If it was dark. We'd see them. Wouldn't we? Properly?"

"I suppose you would."

"I *knew* it." She sounded so pleased. "I told Pip. He said they'd have gone. He said they probably weren't even there any more. I said they're *always* there, you just can't see them."

"You're right."

"I know I'm right." A pause. Then, in a smaller voice: "Dad, are you going in there? Into the broken bit?"

The hammer. The hum. Across the hangar, Ben still talking to his tablet with his forehead in his hand.

He had been going to lie. He had the lie ready. *No, sweetheart, I'm just here to look at the numbers, I'll be home soon.* It was sitting in his mouth, smooth and round, and it would have slid out of him like all the others.

"Yes," he said instead. "Tonight. Very early. With two other people."

"Is it dangerous?"

"Yes."

"Are you scared?"

"Yes," he said. "I'm very scared, Nell."

There was a long pause on the line. He listened to her breathe. He wondered what he'd just done — whether it was the worst thing he could have done to her or the only true thing he'd said to anyone in fifteen years, and whether those were different.

"That's allowed," Nell said at last. She said it firmly, the way Sabine said things, the way she'd been told it herself, probably, in a sports hall, an hour ago. "Mum says that's allowed. You're allowed to be scared, you just have to do it anyway."

"Your mum's right."

"Dad." She'd lowered her voice to a whisper, as though telling him a secret. "When you're in there. Will you look for stars?"

"Nell, there aren't any stars in there. It's a power station. It's all—"

"You don't *know* that," she said. "You haven't been. You don't know what's in there. There might be."

He opened his mouth. He shut it again.

"All right," he said. "I'll look."

"Promise."

"I promise."

"Okay." Satisfied. Then, abruptly, over her shoulder, "*Mum*, Dennis is out—" and a clatter and a shriek and Sabine's voice, distant, and then Sabine back on the line, breathless.

"Cas? Sorry. A lizard. Cas, what did you tell her? She's — she looks—"

"I told her the truth."

A long silence.

"Well," said Sabine eventually. "That'll be a first." But her voice had gone strange, and when she spoke again it was very quiet. "Cas. Come back. Do you hear me? She needs you to come back."

"I know," he said. And then, because it was the only true thing left, "I'm sorry, Bina."

He put the phone down before she could answer.

* * *

The Continuance Day address went out at nine that night, fifteen hours late.

They watched it on the canteen screen. The First Director of the Commonweal stood in Hollowell Square in front of the dark Quota clock with the city's lights behind him and spoke for eleven minutes about sacrifice and endurance and the Tending, and about a minor technical irregularity at the Draw which was being handled with the utmost skill and would be resolved in good time, and about the future, which would pay. Then they played the footage. The Crown, the Lesion like a gold star through the window, engineers at their boards in crisp white coats, a triumphant needle swinging over. The numbers climbing. The crowd in the square cheering.

"That's from Year Eighty," said Ben. "The First Deepening. My dad took me. I was fifteen, I thought it was the best day of my life. That's — look, that's the old console layout, they changed that in Eighty-Five." He stared at the screen. "They're showing old footage. They're showing old footage and pretending it's tonight."

Nobody said anything. On the screen, the gold star shone steady and perfect. Outside the window, over the Works, the shimmer rose into the dark. Once a second, the whole canteen shook.

Teo got up and turned the screen off.

* * *

At eleven Ben showed Cas the relay pucks.

They were small, flat, hockey-puck-shaped things in orange casings, a box of forty, and Ben had been fiddling with them for hours at a workbench under a lamp while the keels sang behind him.

"Radio repeaters," he said. "We drop them as we go. They'll bounce signal back through the Works to the Collar. And to each other. Only — here's the thing, here's the thing I realised this afternoon, it's actually really interesting—" and he was off, with his hands, the way he'd been off about the keels. "If there's a time gradient between two radios, the signal gets stretched. Like, if you're somewhere where time's running slow and I'm somewhere where it's running fast, then from where I am, your voice comes through slowed down, because it's — you're talking at slow-time speed, right? So from my side a one-second word from you takes, I don't know, a minute to arrive. It gets redshifted, it gets smeared out. And the other way round, if I talk to you, I'm talking at fast-time speed, so I get squashed — it arrives all at once, a whole speech in a blip. So I've written some code that buffers everything and re-times it. It records the whole stretched-out signal, however long it takes to come in, and then when it's finished it plays it back at the right speed. So we'll still be able to understand each other. It just might take a while."

"How much of a while?"

Ben shrugged. "Depends on the ratio. Ten to one, it's fine, it's like a bad phone line. A thousand to one, and your one-minute message takes—" he did it in his head "—seventeen hours to get to me. Doesn't matter though. It'll still get there." He grinned tiredly. "If one of us ends up in fast time, the others'll sound like whales. Big slow whale noises for hours. And then the suit goes *ding* and it's you, talking normally, saying 'Farr, you idiot, why did you bring the pucks in the wrong box.'"

Cas looked at the forty orange pucks. He thought about a ratio of tens of thousands. A one-minute message taking weeks to arrive. A voice spread so thin over time that it would come through as a single long unbroken note, like the hum of a keel.

"That's good work, Ben," he said.

Ben looked up at him, surprised, and then flushed right up to the roots of his standing-up hair.

"Thanks," he said. "I mean. Yeah. Thanks."

* * *

A little after midnight Cas went and stood by the spin rigs.

The note of the keels had climbed all night and was now high and pure and steady, a single sustained chord that filled the hangar the way light fills a room. The three suits stood in their frames in a row, grey and hulking, *1*, *2*, *3*, with their indicator bands glowing a steady green, and they looked, in the dim hangar with the Works dark beyond the doors, like three knights keeping a vigil in a chapel. He supposed that was what they were doing. He supposed that was what all three of them were doing.

Teo came and stood beside him. She had a mug of something in each hand and she gave him one. It was tea, stewed black, with an unbelievable quantity of sugar in it.

"Civil Defence tea," she said. "It's mostly a structural material."

They stood and drank it. The hammer went on.

"I've got two boys," Teo said, after a while, to the suits. "Mattias and Joss. Twenty-four and twenty-one. Mattias is an electrician in Kovač. Joss is at the university here, doing something with computers I don't understand. Their father died eleven years ago. Heart." She drank. "I used to tell them, when they were small and they were frightened of something — the dark, the dentist, whatever — I used to tell them, you do the thing, and you do it scared, and then it's done, and the scared goes away and the done stays. That's the deal. Scared goes, done stays."

"Nell's mother tells her something like that."

"Smart woman."

"Yes." He looked into the tea. "She is."

Teo was quiet for a while.

"You told your girl the truth," she said. "On the phone. I heard. Sorry. This place echoes like a church."

He had no answer to that.

"Good," said Teo. That was all. Then she knocked back the rest of her tea, and wiped her mouth with the back of her hand, and went off to try to sleep on a pile of tarpaulins in the corner.

He stood there a while longer. The keels sang. Out in the dark, beyond the doors, the shimmer stood over the station like a ghost of a column, like a tower made of nothing.

*You don't know what's in there*, said Nell in his head. *There might be.*

* * *

At three in the morning — hour twenty-five, by the count that had become the only count that mattered — Ben pronounced the keels spun.

At half past three they suited up. The liner, cool and close; the clam of the suit closing around them, thunk, thunk, thunk; the exo taking their weight. The keels were at full now and the note came into Cas's spine like a held breath. The world was a bright curve of visor. In his ears, the suits' radios hissed and clicked, and then Ben said, "Comms check, one," and Teo said, "One, loud and clear, unfortunately," and Ben said, "Two," and then, "Three?" and Cas said, "Three."

"Right," said Teo. "Right. Let's go and fix the fucking world."

They walked. Out of the hangar, down a service corridor, the three of them in a line, heavy and slow, their boots booming on the steel floor. The Collar was empty. Every door stood open. Every screen was on, showing the same message on a blue background — *MARGIN IRREGULARITY: REMAIN CALM* — and every light was on, and there was nobody, and their footsteps echoed down corridors that went on and on.

At the end of the last corridor was Gate Nine: a great steel door five metres high, painted in hazard stripes, set in a black wall. A small group was waiting in front of it. Rask, very straight, in the same dark suit. Gaunt, in his cardigan, with his hands folded. Petra Imre from the Lattice office, who came forward and, without saying anything, put her hand flat on the chestplate of Cas's suit for a second, and took it away, and stepped back with her eyes wet. A Civil Defence sergeant at the controls of the gate.

Nobody made a speech. Cas was grateful for that. Rask looked at each of them in turn, and nodded once, and said, "The Commonweal is grateful," and it was so obviously the wrong thing to say that it was almost moving.

"Open it," said Teo.

The sergeant pulled a lever. Somewhere in the wall a motor began to grind.

Gate Nine rolled back.

Beyond it was the Works, dark and vast and humming, going on forever. Warm air came out of it, and a smell of ozone and hot metal and something green and rank like pondwater. The light inside was wrong — a sort of faint, shifting, pearly glow that seemed to come from nowhere in particular.

And the hammer, which had been a sound for more than a day, was not a sound any more. In here it was simply the world. It came up through the floor and into the suits and through the suits into their bones, vast and slow and absolute, and all three of them stopped on the threshold as though they'd walked into a wall.

Once a second. Once a second. Once a second.

"Well," said Ben's voice, small and brave in Cas's ear. "That's not ominous at all."

They went in.
