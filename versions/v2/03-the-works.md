title: The Works
---

> SECTION 9 — IN THE EVENT OF CONTAINMENT LOSS: REMAIN AT YOUR STATION AND AWAIT RELIEF. DO NOT ATTEMPT TO TRAVERSE THE LATTICE. RELIEF CREWS WILL BE DISPATCHED. YOU WILL NOT BE FORGOTTEN.
> — Hollowell Draw emergency placard, standard issue, all levels

The placard was the first thing they saw.

It was screwed to the wall just inside Gate Nine, at head height, where every worker coming on shift for ninety-one years would have walked past it: a sheet of yellowed laminate in a steel frame, the words printed in the blocky black typeface the Ministry had used in the Year Twenties, with a little diagram of a stick figure standing beside a stick-figure console with its stick hands at its sides. The laminate had gone cloudy with age. Somebody, at some point, had drawn a moustache on the stick figure in marker pen, and somebody else had scrubbed most of it off.

Teo stopped in front of it. Her helmet light moved slowly down the lines.

"'You will not be forgotten,'" she read. "They'll put that on my headstone."

"They'll put it on everyone's," said Ben.

The gate ground shut behind them. The sound of it closing was very final. Then there was only the hammer, and the three notes of the keels, and their breathing in the suits.

* * *

Cas had been inside the Draw many times, in another life. As a student on placement; as a junior on the Lattice survey team; as a member of the Margin Review, walking the Works with a clipboard behind Dr Adeyemi, who had been sixty-eight and walked everywhere very fast with his hands in his pockets, stopping every so often to lay his palm flat on a wall and listen. He had thought he remembered it. He had thought that fifteen years of not being allowed in had simply made it smaller in his mind, the way the house you grew up in shrinks when you go back.

He had forgotten nothing. He had simply never understood it.

The Works went on for four kilometres between the Collar and the Lattice, and in all that distance there was not one thing that had been built as part of a plan. Hollowell's original station had been a sphere — the Lattice, the Vault, the Lesion — and a thin shell of machinery around it. Then the Commonweal had grown hungry, and every decade since, the Ministry had wanted more, and every decade the engineers had given it more the only way they could: by adding. Rectifier halls to convert the Draw's raw output, and more rectifier halls when the first ones couldn't keep up. Coolant galleries. Bioscrubber halls full of algae tanks to eat the carbon the crews breathed out, because it was cheaper to grow the air than to pump it four kilometres. Transformer vaults, cable tunnels, lift shafts, a spinal column of superconducting trunk lines as thick as a house. And all of it jammed in wherever it would go, on top of, around, and through everything that had been there before, until the whole thing had become less like a building than a reef — a vast, dim, dripping, humming structure that had grown by accretion for ninety-one years in the dark.

Their route ran inward along what the maps called Radial Nine. The maps were optimistic. For the first hour it was a corridor, wide and high and lit by emergency strips at floor level, and they walked it in single file with Teo in front and Ben in the middle and Cas at the back, the keels humming, dropping an orange relay puck every two hundred metres. Then the corridor ended at a stair that went down into a transformer vault the size of a cathedral nave, full of grey steel cabinets in rows like pews, and they crossed it, and on the other side the corridor carried on at a different level, in a different typeface, painted a different colour. The signs changed every few hundred metres. RADIAL 9. R-9 (OLD). RADIAL NINE — WORKS SECTOR 4 — AUTHORISED PERSONNEL. WORKS SECTOR 4 WAS HERE → with an arrow, in faded chalk, pointing at a wall.

Once, early on, they passed the door of a bioscrubber hall: a great grey pressure bulkhead in a yellow hazard frame, with a narrow personnel lock beside it and a brass maker's plate riveted to the steel. HAMMOND CONTAINMENT SYSTEMS. Ben patted it as he went by, the way you'd pat a dog you didn't trust.

"We had one of those on the keel test cell," he said. "Worst door ever made. It only opens from the inside, so nobody out in the corridor can let the algae out by accident. And you have to bleed the hydraulics before it'll crank, and it doesn't tell you that, it just sits there looking at you. I lost a whole afternoon to one. I kicked it. It won."

"Did you get it open?" said Teo.

"Eventually." He sounded pleased with himself. "I know all its secrets now. We're very close."

The smell was ozone and hot oil and algae and a faint sweetish undernote of rot, and the sound was the hammer, and under the hammer, everywhere, the Works itself: a vast layered murmur of machines that had not been told to stop. Pumps still pumping. Fans still turning. Somewhere far off, a relay clicking open and shut, open and shut, like a finger tapping on a desk. The rectifier halls were still live — there was power in them from somewhere, the Lesion's leaking output finding its own way through the old conductors — and as they passed the doors they could hear the halls singing, a high, thin, electrical choir.

And there was the light. The faint pearly glow Cas had seen from the gate was everywhere in here, in the corridors and halls and stairwells, sourceless and shifting. It came through gaps in walls and under doors. It lay on the floor in pale drifts like moonlight. When he held up his gauntlet in front of his visor, his hand cast no shadow.

"Where's that coming from?" said Ben.

"The core," said Cas.

"Through four kilometres of — through all *this*?"

"Not through it. Around it." He didn't really know how to explain it. He wasn't sure he understood it himself. "The geometry's bent. There are paths for light in here that shouldn't exist. Some of it's getting out along them."

"That's—" Ben started, and then, for once, didn't finish.

* * *

The first gravity squall hit them in the second hour.

They were in a long service gallery with pipes along both walls, walking on a grated catwalk above a drop into darkness, and Teo, in front, said, "Hold on—" and then the floor tipped.

It didn't. The floor stayed exactly where it was. But *down* moved. Cas felt it go the way you'd feel a boat begin to roll: a slow, sickening swing, his weight sliding sideways inside the suit, the whole world leaning, and then the far wall of the gallery was below him and he was falling toward it. He grabbed. The suit's gauntlet closed on the catwalk rail harder than he meant it to, crushing the steel, and held. His legs swung out. For a long moment he hung there, the three of them hanging there, dangling from a handrail over a wall that had become a floor, while the pipes around them groaned and a loose toolbox went tumbling past, end over end, and hit the far wall, and slid along it, and fell into the dark.

Then down swung back. Slowly, like a pendulum. They dropped to their knees on the catwalk.

"*Christ*," said Teo.

"Everybody okay?" Ben's voice was high. "Everybody — is the keel — your keels okay? Check your — the indicator bands, are they—"

"Green," said Cas. "Green. I'm fine."

"Green," said Teo. "What the hell was that?"

"Gravity weather." Cas got up. His legs were shaking inside the exo; the exo held them. "The Lattice is still there, it's still pulling, but it's lost its shape. And the Lesion's pushing. And every piece of keelstone in the Works is pulling in its own direction. They're all out of balance. It'll come in gusts."

"*Gusts.*"

"And shears. And lees. Places where they cancel out and it's calm." He looked back along the gallery. "We should expect more."

They did get more. Over the next hours the Works taught them its weather the hard way. There were gusts like the first, where down swung sideways or backwards or, once, for three terrible seconds, straight up, so that they found themselves pressed against the ceiling of a stairwell looking down at the stairs. There were places where weight simply piled on — a stretch of corridor where each step was heavier than the last until the exo was groaning and Cas's knees were on fire even inside it, and then, abruptly, it was gone. There was a stairwell where, for three steps in the middle, gravity doubled, and they learned to take those three steps at a run. There was a long, wide turbine hall where down was very slightly *forward*, so that crossing it they seemed to be walking downhill on a floor that was dead level, the three of them leaning backward against nothing like people walking into a gale.

They learned to watch for it. Loose things were the tell. A cable swinging when there was no breeze. Dust drifting across a floor. Teo started carrying a handful of washers in her gauntlet and dropping one every few metres, watching where it fell.

And their clocks began to drift.

Each suit had a small display at the bottom of the visor with two times on it: OUTSIDE, fed by a signal from the Collar through the relay pucks, and LOCAL, the suit's own clock. At the gate they'd kept step, second for second. An hour in, LOCAL had gained four minutes. Two hours in, nineteen. Ben started reading out the ratio every half hour like a man calling depths on a ship.

"One point one."

"One point three."

"One point six. We're living sixty percent faster than the Collar."

"Meaning?" said Teo.

"Meaning for every hour we spend in here, only about forty minutes go by out there." Ben considered this. "I mean. Which is sort of good? We've got more time than we thought."

"It's good for the city," said Cas. "The debt's on the Collar's clock. Every hour we spend in here only costs them forty minutes of it." He looked at the two numbers at the bottom of his visor. "It's not good for us. The air in these suits is on our clock. So's the power. So are the keels. So are we."

"So we've got more time," said Ben, "and we use it up faster."

"Yes. And it gets stronger further in." Cas looked along the corridor, inward, into the pearly dark. "By the Crown, an hour of the Collar's could be days of ours."

Ben thought about that for a while.

"That's either really good," he said, "or really bad."

"Yes," said Cas.

* * *

They found the first of the night shift in the fourth hour.

They'd seen signs before that. Hard hats in corridors. A jacket hanging on a valve wheel. A lunch box, open, a sandwich still inside it, going green. An emergency locker torn open and emptied. Once, a set of muddy footprints in the dust of a floor that had been dry for years, going the other way, toward the gate, running. They were the prints of people who'd got out. The Collar had counted them: about four hundred had come out through the gates in the first hour after the rupture, from the outer Works, before the gradients got bad.

The rest hadn't.

It was a stairhead, where a stair from the lower levels came up through the floor into a junction of four corridors. The night shift had come up that stair, running for the gate, maybe twenty of them. Cas never knew what had happened exactly. He guessed that it was a shear — a place where the gravity gradient had gone suddenly steep, a cliff of force, and had moved, perhaps only a few metres, perhaps only for a second — and that they had been coming up the stair into the junction at the moment it passed over them.

They were on the wall.

That was the only way he could ever put it, afterwards, to himself. They were on the far wall of the junction, the way paint is on a wall. Their shapes were still there. You could see where each of them had been — this one's arm flung up, this one's hard hat, the pale sole of a boot. You could see that one of them had been holding someone else's hand. But they had been pressed into the wall, by a weight no body could bear, with such force and so suddenly that they had not so much been crushed as *printed*. The wall behind them was bowed inward. The blood was black and had run nowhere, because at the instant it happened there had been nowhere for it to run.

Ben made a sound and turned away and went down on his knees in the middle of the junction and was sick in his helmet. The suit's scrubbers caught it and whined. He stayed there, bent over, his gauntlets on the floor, breathing in harsh, wet gulps over the radio.

Cas stood very still. He found that he was looking at the hand. The two hands. He could not stop looking at the two hands.

Teo walked past them both.

She walked right up to the wall. She stood in front of it for a moment. Then she took off her own jacket — she'd brought a Civil Defence field jacket, strapped to the thigh of her suit, for when they reached somewhere she could take the suit off — and unfolded it and reached up and laid it over the part of the wall where the faces had been. It didn't cover much. She looked around, and found a tarpaulin in a maintenance alcove, and a couple of high-vis vests hanging on a hook, and a dust sheet, and she hung them all. She did it methodically and without hurry, while Ben knelt on the floor being sick and Cas stood staring, and when she'd finished there was a patchwork of cloth on the wall and you could no longer see anything under it but shapes.

Then she came back and put her gauntlet on Ben's shoulder.

"Up you get, son."

"I can't—"

"You can. You'll do it scared. Up."

He got up.

Teo looked at Cas through two visors. He couldn't see her face. He could hear her breathing, over the radio, harsh and slow and very controlled.

"How many more of those," she said, "are we going to find?"

"I don't know."

"Right." She turned toward the corridor that ran inward. "Then let's not stand about."

* * *

He saw the filament first.

It was in a hall they had to cross in the fifth hour — a long, high, gloomy space that had been a coolant-pump hall once, before the pumps were moved, and was now a storeroom, full of pallets and crates and racks of spares going back to the ceiling in the pearly dark. Their route crossed it diagonally. They were halfway across when Cas saw something at the far end and stopped, and said, "Wait," and the others stopped.

It was a thread of light.

It came out of the far wall of the hall about ten metres up and crossed the space in front of them and went into the floor. It was as fine as a hair and as bright as the filament in a bulb — the same white-violet as the Lesion, the colour that wasn't quite a colour — and it wasn't straight. It branched. It went a little way and split in two, and each of those split, and each of those, so that what came out of the wall as a single line went into the floor as a fine, spreading fan of threads, a luminous root system, a river delta drawn in light. And where it branched, the branches branched, smaller and smaller, finer and finer, until the eye lost them and they became a sort of glow, a faint shimmering mist of edges around every edge. When he tried to look at any part of it closely, his eye slid off. When he looked away, he could still see it on the inside of his eyelids.

It made no sound.

"What," said Ben, "is that."

"Part of the Lesion," said Cas. He was surprised how steady his voice was. "Part of the boundary. It's — when it broke, it didn't break into pieces. It broke into more edge. The edge is growing outward. That's one of its threads."

"It came through four kilometres of—"

"It doesn't come *through* things. It's not in the Works the way we're in the Works. It's where the Works has been — bent." He didn't have a better word for it. "Don't go near it."

They didn't need telling. But they couldn't help looking. And what they saw, the closer they looked, was worse than the light.

Around the filament, for a metre or two on every side, the hall was different. The racks it passed near weren't grey steel any more; they were brown and orange and flaking, rusted through into lace, sagging on themselves, and in one place a whole rack had simply crumbled and lay on the floor in a heap of rust-red powder with the plastic crates it had held collapsed into brittle shards on top. A section of cable tray that ran close to one branch was grey-white and furred. The paint on the wall beside the place where the thread came out had peeled and curled and fallen and lay in drifts. And on the floor, close to the fan of threads, there was a potted plant.

Someone must have brought it in. A night-shift worker, making the storeroom a bit nicer. A spider plant, the kind that grew in every office in Averna, in a green plastic pot. Except that it had grown. Its pale leaves had gone up in a fountain higher than a man's head and spilled out across the floor in long runners, and the runners had put down roots in the dust of the floor and grown new plants, and those had grown, and all of it — the whole great sprawling green-white tangle, three metres across — was dead, and dry, and grey, and dusty, as though it had grown for years and years and then withered and then stood for years more after that. In a hall where, by the Collar's clock, only twenty-nine hours had passed.

"It's fast," said Cas. "Near the thread. Time's fast."

Ben already had his instrument out — a small sensor on a pole that measured the local clock rate — and was holding it out as far toward the threads as he dared, his arm at full stretch. The number on its display leapt as he moved it. Two. Nine. Forty. He took one step closer and it read four hundred.

"Back up," said Teo.

He backed up. The number fell. He lowered the pole very slowly and stood looking at the dead plant.

"It's only near it," said Teo. "A couple of metres. The rest of the hall's fine."

"The hall's open." Cas nodded back at the doors they'd come in by. Both of them were standing wide, wedged with fire-stops, the way the night shift must have left them. So was the pair at the far end. "It's got somewhere to thin out into. The whole Works. It's like a heater in a room with the windows open — hot near the bars, nothing across the room." He looked at the thread. "Shut those doors and it'd be a different story."

"That thing," said Ben quietly, "is going to be everywhere, isn't it. Further in."

"Yes," said Cas.

They went round the far side of the hall, as far from the filament as the racks allowed. Nobody talked. When they reached the far door Cas looked back once. From here the thread was just a pale line crossing the gloom, a scratch on the air. It could have been a trick of the light. It could have been nothing. Except that he could see, very faintly, in the glow around its finest branches, that it was moving. Growing. Very slowly branching into itself.

And as he watched, it jumped. A new fork ran out from one of its branches all at once, fast as frost racing across a pane — a hand's length in a blink — and then it was back to its slow crawl, as if nothing had happened. He'd seen it do that once before, while they were crossing. Half an hour of crawling. Then a jump.

* * *

They found out what Cas had meant about shut doors twenty minutes later.

It was a locker room — a small one, off a side corridor, the kind a pump crew would change in at the start of a shift. Its door was shut. It was a steel door with a small square window of wired glass at head height, and the window was wrong.

Ben saw it first. "Why's it doing that?"

The glass was moving. Not the pane; the light in it. It rippled, like the air over a road in summer, like the shimmer over the Pit — a slow trembling that never stopped and never went anywhere, held inside a pane of glass the size of a book. All round the door, in a band a finger wide, the paint on the frame had crazed into a fine web of cracks. Nowhere else.

Teo shone her light through the window.

The room beyond was a ruin. Everything in it was grey. The lockers had rusted to brown lace and slumped against each other; a bench had folded in half; a jacket on a hook had gone to threads and the threads had gone to powder; and the floor was deep in a soft grey dust that lay over everything like snow. And through the middle of the room, from the ceiling to the floor, ran a single thread of white-violet light, fine as a hair.

There was a clock on the wall above the lockers. Its hands weren't moving. They were a faint grey disc.

Ben had his pole out. He held it up to the door, not touching. "One point seven," he said. He brought it closer to the window, very carefully, until the sensor was a finger's width from the pane. "One point seven. One point—" He let the tip touch the glass, just barely, and the display went mad: a string of digits climbing too fast to read, and then a row of dashes. He snatched it back.

"It's in the glass," said Cas.

"*What* is?"

"All of it. The whole difference." He could see it now that he knew what he was looking at: the ripple, the crazed paint, a line drawn round the room in the only place the difference could live. "The thread's in a shut room. There's nowhere for the fast time to thin out, so the whole room's come up to the thread's speed. And the walls and the door and that window are holding the drop between it and us. All of it. Out here it's one point seven. A centimetre away it's whatever the thread is. Hundreds. Thousands."

"It's a voltage divider," said Ben slowly. He was staring at the window. "Sorry — it's a circuit thing. Resistors in a line, the biggest drop's across the biggest resistor. The air's the wire. The wall's the resistor. So all of it sits across the wall." He looked at Cas. "Is that right? Is that stupid?"

"It's not stupid. It's right."

"So the Lattice—"

"Is the biggest resistor there is." Cas looked along the corridor, inward. "And if there's a Shear, that's why."

Teo was looking at the door handle.

"And if somebody opened that," she said.

"Then the drop's got nowhere to be but the doorway. All of it, in the gap. Through whoever's holding the handle." He didn't say what that would do to the hand on the handle. He didn't have to. "And then out down the corridor, a front of it, thinning as it spreads. You wouldn't want to be standing where we're standing."

Nobody touched the door.

"A blister," said Ben. "It's a blister. There's a skin, and all this — stuff — inside, and you mustn't pop it." He swallowed. "Sorry. I name things when I'm frightened."

"You name things all the time," said Teo.

"I'm frightened all the time."

"Right, then." Teo straightened. She said it the way she'd have briefed a crew on a flood barrier. "Rules. One: we don't open a shut room with a thread in it. Ever. Two: we don't shut ourselves in a room with one." She looked at each of them in turn. "Anybody not got that?"

Nobody said anything.

They went on. Cas looked back once at the little square window, trembling in its door, and thought about the placard inside Gate Nine. *Do not attempt to traverse the Lattice.* People had walked past that sign for ninety-one years thinking it was a rule about machinery. It was a rule about this. There was no way round a drop; you could only spread it out, and nobody had ever built a road long enough to spread out the kind of difference he thought was waiting at the core. Nobody had needed to. The station had been built to run on ordinary time, with the Lattice holding back a few seconds a day.

*You will not be forgotten*, he thought, and didn't like how it sounded.

* * *

At the end of the fifth hour they found a lee and stopped to rest.

It was a small control room for a bank of pumps that had long since been removed, a box of a room with a desk and two chairs and a window onto nothing, and when they came into it the gravity just — went soft. After hours of gusts and shears and weights coming and going it was so calm, so ordinary, that all three of them stood in the doorway for a moment, swaying, like sailors come ashore. Teo dropped a washer. It fell straight down and bounced.

"Oh, thank God," said Ben, and sat down on the floor.

They didn't take the suits off. They couldn't, here. But they sat, and drank from the suits' water lines, and ate the paste that came out of the suits' ration tubes, which tasted, Ben said, like somebody had described chicken to a machine. Their clocks said LOCAL 05:02 — five hours since the gate — and OUTSIDE 29:07, twenty-nine hours since the hammer began. The ratio in here was one point nine.

Teo sat on the desk. For a while she was silent. Then she reached into the chest pocket of her liner, under the suit's neck-ring, with two fingers, working carefully in the gauntlet, and brought out something small. She held it up.

It was a data chip. A little black wafer the size of a fingernail, with the Office of Public Calm's seal on it in silver.

"Gaunt gave me this," she said.

Ben looked up.

"Orders," said Teo. "Sealed. For my eyes. I read them last night." She turned the chip over in her fingers. "They say that the mission objective is the restoration of Continuance. They say that the technical lead, Dr Casimir Hale, has a documented history of unreliability on the question of Continuance. They say that in the event that Dr Hale attempts any action which would result in — let me get it right—" she half closed her eyes "—'the permanent default of the Hollowell Draw', I'm authorised to prevent that action by any means." She looked at Cas. "Any means. They underlined it."

Cas said nothing.

"You knew," said Teo.

"I saw Gaunt give you something."

"And you didn't say anything."

"I thought you'd tell me. Or you wouldn't."

Teo looked at him for a while. Then she looked at the chip.

On the floor of the little control room, along one wall, ran a coolant channel — an open steel gutter about a hand's width across, with a steady, fast stream of cloudy water racing along it and vanishing through a grating into the dark under the floor. Teo leaned down and held the chip over it.

"Tell me I'm not going to regret this," she said.

"You're probably going to regret this."

"Tell me the truth, then." Her voice was flat. "All of it. Right now. Or I'll keep it."

So he did.

He told her Six couldn't be reseated. That it had never been possible — that a Lattice with a fractal core had no geometry left to seat anything in, and every engineer in the crisis room had known it and had said *yes, Minister* anyway, and so had he. He told her about the Clause, and the Keystone, and that the only thing in the Draw that could stop what was coming would also end the Draw for ever, which was why nobody in the Ministry would say its name. He told her the key had been around the neck of Deputy Minister Aldous Crane when the Lesion broke, and that he didn't know where Crane was, or whether he was alive, or whether the key still existed. He told her that he didn't know what the words on the plate meant. He told her that he didn't know, even if they reached the Crown, even if they found the key, even if they got the Keystone open, whether the thing would work, or whether anyone could survive working it, or whether Hollowell had been a genius or a madwoman or both.

"So," said Teo, when he'd finished. "Let me get this right. We're walking into hell on a maybe."

"Yes."

"Good." She dropped the chip into the coolant channel. It hit the water and was gone, whisked away down the gutter and through the grating into the dark, and she didn't even watch it go. "I hate certainties. They always want something from you."

Ben was staring at her.

"Major," he said. "Major, that's — I mean, isn't that—"

"Treason? Probably." Teo stretched, as far as the suit let her, and her joints cracked over the radio. "I don't give a damn about the Draw, Farr. Never have. I care about the six hundred million people up there who are about to be very dead, and I care about my boys, and I care about getting a drink afterwards with a man who owes me one." She tipped her visor at Cas. "That's you, by the way. You owe me one. For the chip."

"I owe you one," said Cas.

"Good. I'll have a large one."

Ben looked from one of them to the other. Then he laughed — a thin, shocked, helpless little laugh, like air going out of a tyre — and put his helmet in his gauntlets and kept laughing, and after a while it wasn't clear that it was laughing any more, and Teo reached over and put her hand on his back and left it there until it stopped.

* * *

They moved on at the start of the sixth hour.

The map said that Radial Nine ran on for another kilometre and a half to the Lattice, but the map also said it went straight through a bioscrubber hall — B-7, one of the big algae halls from the Year Fifties — and when they got to the hall's outer door the light coming around its edges was brighter than anywhere they had been.

Ben went ahead, up a short stair to an observation gallery that ran along one side of the hall, and they heard him stop.

"Dr Hale," he said. "You should see this."

It was a long gallery with a wall of thick glass, looking down into the hall. The hall was enormous — three hundred metres long, forty wide — and filled with algae tanks: great clear columns of water, rank after rank of them, each as tall as a house and full of drifting green, lit from within by their own grow-lamps, so that the whole hall glowed a deep, underwater emerald. Pipes ran between them. Walkways ran between the pipes. It was beautiful in a way nothing else in the Works had been. It looked like the inside of a lung. It looked like the bottom of the sea.

And along the far wall of it, from floor to ceiling, ran a filament.

This one was bigger. Not a hair; a vein. And it wasn't in the hall. It was in the wall — in the thickness of it, under its skin, the way a vein runs under the skin of a wrist: a great branching tree of light glowing up through the grey concrete from inside, brightest where the panels had cracked. Its finest twigs reached almost to the wall's face and stopped there, pressed up against the inside of it like fingers against a window. In front of it, the first few ranks of tanks had gone strange colours: black, brown, a thick, clotted, vivid green; one had simply cracked and drained and stood empty with a scum of dried grey slime up its sides. Past those, the tanks looked the way tanks should. In the nearest ones, below the gallery, the bubbles rose at the lazy speed that bubbles rise at.

"It's in the wall," said Ben. "It's not in the room. That's — that's good? That's good, isn't it. The wall's holding it."

"The wall's holding most of it." Cas watched the ruined tanks at the far end. "Some of it leaks through. That end's fast. This end isn't."

"And if it comes out of the wall?"

Nobody answered him. Nobody needed to. They'd stood in front of the locker room door half an hour ago.

Teo was reading the map on her visor. "The route goes through the hall. In by the big door down there, down the south side, out at the far end. Three hundred metres." She pointed. "The other way round's the long way. Three hours. Back past the transformer vaults and round — through that weather."

Nobody wanted the weather. They'd skirted the edge of it on the way in, a stretch where the floor had gone sideways three times in a hundred metres, and Cas had listened to all three keels labour through every gust. Every hour of that would cost the rotors spin. Spin they were going to need at the Shear.

"And the hall's shut," said Teo. "It's a bioscrubber. It's sealed by design." She looked at Cas. "Rule two."

"Rule two," said Cas.

They'd be ten minutes crossing it. Ten minutes inside a sealed room with a thread in its wall — exactly the thing they'd said out loud, half an hour ago, that they would never do.

"Then we open it first," said Ben.

They looked at him.

"Vent it. Before we go in." He was leaning on the glass, looking down at the hall's main door at the foot of the gallery stair: a grey pressure bulkhead three metres high, in a yellow hazard frame, with a personnel lock beside it. "That's a Hammond. That's the one I kicked. If we crack the big door and leave it open, the hall's not shut any more, it's just part of the Works. Then if the thread does come out of the wall, it's the storeroom. Fast at that end, fine at this end. And we walk down the south side and out, and it doesn't matter what the thread does."

"And to crack the big door," said Cas, "somebody has to be inside."

Ben's face did something complicated. "Yeah," he said. "That's the — yeah. It only opens from inside. That's the whole point of it. You go in through the personnel lock, and the lock won't open its inside door till its outside door's shut, so the hall stays sealed while you're doing it. Then you walk ten metres to the control pillar, and bleed the hydraulics, and crank. Four minutes. Five. Then it's open, and you two come through."

"Five minutes shut in a room with a thread in it," said Teo.

"In its *wall*. It's in the wall. It's been in the wall the whole time we've been standing here."

"Then I'll do it."

"You don't know the sequence. There's a thing you have to do with the bleed valve first or it just sits there, I told you, I lost a whole afternoon—" He turned to Cas. "It's faster if it's me. It's a lot faster. And faster's the point, isn't it? Faster's the whole point."

They both looked at Cas.

He was the technical lead. Rask had said so, in a room with gold carpet, and he'd said yes, and this was what it meant. He looked at the tree of light in the wall. He did the arithmetic, because he always did the arithmetic. The thread in the storeroom had crawled for half an hour between its jumps, and most of its jumps had gone nowhere much. This one had sat under its skin of concrete, not moving, for as long as they'd been watching. The chance that it would break out into the air in the next five minutes, rather than the next hour, or the next day, or never — he couldn't know it. He could guess. He guessed it at one in twenty. Less. Against three hours of weather and a keel's worth of spin.

*It'll probably be fine*, he thought.

The sentence sat in his head, smooth and round and reasonable. It did not occur to him that anyone had ever thought it before.

"Five minutes," he said. "In and out. If anything changes — anything — you come straight back through the lock."

"Five minutes," said Ben, and grinned at them suddenly, hugely, like a boy let out of school. "Right. Right, you absolute antique. Let's have you."

He went down the stair two at a time, his keel humming, his boots ringing on the steel treads, and the outer door of the personnel lock swung open for him, and he went into it, and it swung shut.
