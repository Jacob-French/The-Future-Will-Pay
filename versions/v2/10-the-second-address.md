---
title: The Second Address
---

> Tell them the Drop isn't open. Say it twice. They'll only hear it once.
> — Perpetua Tally

They spent the three rounds before the Bell getting it right.

Not the speech. He didn't write a speech. He tried, the first night, in the loft, and Ione watched him cross out three pages and then took the pencil out of his hand and said, "Don't. You'll make it pretty. Say it the way you said it to me." So he didn't write it. But they planned everything around it.

The Tollers would be on the roof of the Commons. The Commons roof was open in long strips between the girders, and there was a ridge of old gantry running along the top of it that the riggers used for stringing lamp-lines on high days; Tobiah and three others would be up there with a banner. It was already painted. Perpetua had had it painted on the first night, on a sheet of canvas thirty metres long, and it lay folded in the upper floor of Toller's Tower like a sail: Seren's line. The Tollers' curve, drawn huge, in white on black, with a hundred and sixty-eight years of Bells falling away along it and the line carried forward past the end of the count to the place where it met the bottom of the canvas. And beside that place, in letters two metres high, the number Seren had worked out thirteen years ago and the Tollers had been refining every round since.

"When they see it," said Perpetua, "they'll know it's true. Nobody in Holdfast would believe a word from the Outside. They'll believe a Tally. They've been counting their whole lives."

The riggers would be at the doors. Ione had asked them. Not all of them — the riggers were as split as anyone, and some of them went to the Commons on the seventh round and knelt at Muster Point B — but a few dozen, the ones who'd known Marrow Tell and his crew, the ones who'd climbed Six the round after and seen the bones. They would be at the doors and on the ladders, with rope. Nobody had said what the rope was for.

And Perpetua told him, on the last night, sitting in her chair with her hand on the column:

"Tell them the Drop isn't open."

"I will."

"Say it twice. They'll only hear it once." She looked at him with her black bird's eyes. "You're going to tell twenty thousand people that the Outside's alive and that a man came up out of the floor from it. Some of them are going to hear that and nothing else. You understand? Nothing else. You could talk for a shift. They'll hear *the Outside is alive, and he came up the Drop*." She took her hand off the column and laid it on his knee, the way she had the first night. "Say it twice, Relief. Say it three times. It won't be enough. But say it."

* * *

Kindling Round fell three rounds later — the first round of the year One Hundred and Sixty-Nine, a hundred and sixty-eight years to the round since the Lamp was lit — and the whole of Holdfast had been getting ready for it for a week.

There had never been a Kindling Round like it. That was what everyone said. Every year of the Watch, on this round, Holdfast remembered the night the Founders stood under the Placard and counted the first Bell. And this year — in a year when the Bells came more than a hundred a round, when you could feel them while you ate and while you slept — *the Relief had come*. It was a sign. It was the greatest festival in the history of the Watch. The lanes had been hung with lamp-strings for three rounds: little clay lamps strung on wire from wall to wall, hundreds of them, so that even in the day the lanes glittered. Every shrine had been repainted. Children had been making paper lamps. The Public Tally board in the Commons had been hung with a garland of green algae-cloth and the numbers repainted in gold.

The Commons was fuller than it had been the night he arrived. He hadn't thought that was possible. They packed the floor to the walls. They filled the girders — people had climbed up into the roof trusses and sat along them in rows, their legs dangling, like birds on a wire. They stood outside in the lanes so thick that a Warden trying to get through had to be passed over their heads. The noise was the sea. When the Wardens carried Cas in on the litter — he'd asked to walk; Crane had said, kindly, *no* — the sea rose up into something like a storm, and it was a long time before it went down.

He stood on the platform. Osk was at his elbow. Crane beside him, in his black coat, with a new lamp embroidered on its breast in gold thread for the occasion. The Public Tally board behind them: BELL 121,944.

He looked for Ione. He couldn't see her.

He looked up. Along the ridge of the gantry, high up in the open roof, in the violet glare of the Lace, there were four small figures in grey smocks, sitting in a row with something long and folded across their knees.

Crane led the singing. The Tending, slow and worn. Twenty thousand voices, and more outside, the whole city singing, under the Lace. *Tend the Lamp, tend the Lamp, till the Relief comes after.* Cas listened to every word of it. He made himself. He stood there on the platform and let it wash over him and thought about a children's choir on the Tier Nine lampposts at dawn, and about what those children had been singing about, and what these people were.

Then the call and response. *Who keeps the Watch?* The Bell came during it — 121,945 — rising up through the floor, and the whole Commons fell still and silent and counted it, and then went on. *Until Relief.*

Then Crane turned to him, and smiled, and stepped back.

BELL 121,946.

He had about ten minutes before the next one.

* * *

"I lied to you," said Casimir Hale.

He didn't raise his voice. He'd learned, the first night, that he didn't have to. The Commons was so silent when he spoke that the smallest sound carried to the back.

"The first night I came. I stood here and I told you it was going to be all right. I don't know that. I didn't know it then. I said it because I looked at your faces and I didn't want to be the one who took it away." He could see the front row. There was a little girl there — not the same one, a different one. He looked at her. He made himself go on looking at her. "That's what people do where I come from. They say what's easy. They've been doing it for ninety years. It's why you're here."

A murmur. Very faint. Like wind starting.

"So I'm going to tell you the truth now. All of it. It's going to be hard to hear, and I'm sorry. And some of it you're not going to believe, and that's all right. But I need you to listen to all of it. Not just the start. All of it. Please."

He took a breath.

"The Outside is alive."

The murmur became a sound.

"It isn't burned. It never was. It's a city. A big one, bigger than you can imagine. It's called Averna. It has six hundred million people in it and all of them are alive right now, today, this minute. And it's the same night there. It's still the same night that the Lamp was kindled." He waited for the sound to go down. It didn't, quite. "You know that. Some of you know that. The Tollers know it. They've always known. Every Bell you've ever counted is one second of the Outside's time. A hundred and twenty-two thousand Bells is thirty-four hours, near enough. That's all it's been, out there. A day and a half. The Outside isn't gone. It's slow."

"*Lies!*" Somebody, at the back. A man's voice, high and cracked. Then another voice, shouting him down. Then others. The sound rose.

"There's a little girl in the Outside," said Cas, into it, "called Maddy Tally. She's seven. She went to sleep on the night of the Kindling in her grandmother's flat, and her mother went to work at the station and didn't come home. Her mother was Ruth Tally. Your Founder. The one who counted the first Bell." The sound faltered. "Ruth wrote her letters for fifty years. She never stopped. She died in here, an old woman, with eleven grandchildren. And Maddy's still seven. Right now, while I'm talking. She'll be having her lunch."

It went quiet then. Really quiet. Not the silence of listening; something else. A silence like the moment after a glass is dropped, before it hits the floor.

"The Kindling," said Cas, "was an accident."

He felt Crane move beside him. He didn't look round.

"The station — this place, the Draw — makes power. It sends it Outside. The night of the Kindling, there was a test. The people who ran the Outside wanted more power, and they wanted to film the station making it, for a festival. The engineers said it wasn't safe. The man in charge made them do it anyway. His name was Aldous Crane."

The sound the Commons made then was something he'd never heard a crowd make. A kind of groan. A kind of gasp. Twenty thousand people drawing in breath together.

"He was your Founder. He was a — he wasn't a bad man. He was a frightened one. Ruth Tally tried to shut the Lamp off, that night, in the Crown. There was a lock, and there was a key, and he had the key round his neck, and she asked him for it, and he wouldn't give it to her. And then there was an accident, and the key fell, and it was lost." He made himself say it. "He didn't give the key to the Lamp. He dropped it."

"*Heresy!*" A woman's voice this time, very near the front, shrill with horror. "Heresy, it's — *Steward!* Steward, stop him—"

"Let him finish."

It was Crane.

Cas turned his head. The old man was standing quite still beside him, very straight in his black coat with its new gold lamp, and his face was the colour of the paper in the Log, and his long hands were clasped very tightly in front of him. He wasn't looking at Cas. He was looking out at the Commons.

"Let him finish," said Halloran Crane again, and his voice carried to the back of the hall.

* * *

He told them the rest.

He told them what the Lamp was. He did it the way he'd done it for Crane — a debt, a loan from the emptiness, and the emptiness always wants it back — and he watched their faces, and he saw some of them understand, and some of them not, and some of them refuse to. He told them what the Bells meant. That the Quickening was the debt coming due. That it was going to come faster and faster. That the Lace was part of it, and it was coming down, and that when it reached the Rim at the top of the spindles, it would all be over. Not just Holdfast. Everything.

"Marrow Tell and his crew," he said, "weren't taken into the Lamp's crown. They weren't blessed. They died. The Lace touched the top of Six and they grew old and died in the time it takes to draw a breath. The riggers who went up after them found their bones." He looked out at the hall. "The Quickening isn't glory. It's a clock. And it's nearly run down."

He heard someone crying. He heard someone praying, in a low fast mutter, the words of the Placard. *Remain at your station. Await relief.*

BELL 121,947 came while he was talking. He stopped, and waited, and everyone waited, and it rose and hung and fell, and when it had gone he said:

"There's one chance."

He looked for her again. And this time he found her. She was at the back, where she'd been the first night, by the great doors, in the gap in the crowd, with her arms folded and her back against the wall. The riggers were around her. She was looking at him. She wasn't patient now. She was very still, and her eyes were very bright.

"There's a way to close the Lamp," he said. "The woman who built this station put it there. It's in the Crown, at the top of Spindle One. It's a lock. It's been there all along. It needs a key." He lifted his hand and pointed, over twenty thousand heads, at the back of the hall. "And the key is here. Ione Tally has it."

Every head in the Commons turned.

"She found it, eleven years ago, up there. The key your Founder dropped. She came down with it and told you the Lamp could be closed. And you called her mad. And her mother, Seren Tally, drew the line of the Bells and told you how long you had left, and you gave her to the Drop." He heard his voice shaking and let it. "They were right. Both of them. They were right all along."

And up on the roof, on the gantry, in the glare of the Lace, the Tollers stood up and let the banner fall.

It came down out of the open roof between the girders like a great black sail unfurling, thirty metres of it, and snapped taut, and hung there over the whole of the Commons. The curve. White on black. A hundred and sixty-eight years of Bells, falling away in a long smooth swoop, and the line going on past the end of the count, down, down, to the bottom edge of the canvas. And beside it, in white letters two metres tall, a single number, and a single word.

:: ROUND 48
:: THE LACE MEETS THE RIM

It was the first round of the year.

He heard the Commons read it. He heard twenty thousand people do the sum.

He'd done his own, at the top of the Tollers' tower. Forty-seven rounds. Out there, it was about two hours. Hour thirty-six, on a clock he'd once thought had days left on it. In here, it was all the time there was.

"We're going to go up," he said, very loudly now, over the sound that was starting. "Ione Tally and me. To the Crown. To try. And if it works—" he had to shout "—*listen to me*. If it works, the Lamp will go out."

The sound stopped, a little. Enough.

"The Lamp will go out, and it will be dark. Really dark. Darker than you've ever seen. And you mustn't be afraid of it." He was looking at the little girl in the front row. She was holding her mother's skirt in both fists. "When the Lamp goes out, it means we did it. It means the debt's closed. It means the Shear's gone and time in here is the same as time out there. And it means the Drop will be a road. You'll be able to walk down it. All of you. Out. Into the Outside. Into the same night."

He held on to Osk's arm. He was very tired suddenly.

"But not before," he said. "Listen. *Not before*. The Drop is not open now. Nobody goes near the wells. Nobody. Not until the Lamp goes out. If you go down now, the Shear will kill you, the way it killed everyone who ever tried. The way it killed Seren. I came up through it in a suit made for it and it nearly killed me. The Drop is not open. Do you hear me? The Drop is not open."

*Say it twice.*

"The Drop is *not open*."

And then, into the silence, because it was the only thing left and because it was the truest thing he knew:

"Nobody's coming. There's no Relief. I'm not it. There never was one. I'm just what came."

* * *

For a moment, nothing happened.

Then the Commons came apart.

He'd never know, afterwards, how it started. He'd go over it again and again, lying in the dark, and never be able to find the first moment. It was as though the hall had been holding a single breath for a hundred and sixty-eight years and had just let it out. Everyone was shouting. Everyone was moving. People were on their feet, on their knees, clinging to each other, pushing, surging toward the platform, surging away from it. Someone was screaming *heresy, heresy* over and over in a voice like a torn sail. Someone else had climbed up onto the brass rail of Muster Point B and was reading the Placard aloud at the top of his lungs. A knot of men at the side of the hall were fighting — fists, and then staves, the Wardens going in — and a woman near the front had her arms round a stranger and was sobbing into his coat, and the little girl in the front row was crying with her face in her mother's skirt.

And there was the sound he'd been afraid of.

He heard it start at the back, near the doors. Near Ione. A man's voice, high and wild, carrying over everything the way a single voice sometimes can:

"*The Outside's alive! He came up the Drop! He came up it — it's open — we can go down, we can go down—*"

"*No!*"

That was him. He was shouting. He was at the front of the platform, shouting over twenty thousand people, *no, no, it isn't open, it isn't—*, and nobody could hear him. He could see the words not reaching. He could see it happen at the back of the hall: the knot of people round that one voice, turning, pushing toward the great doors. And the doors opening, and the lanes outside, and the people in the lanes turning to see what was happening, and hearing it — *it's open, he came up it, we can go down* — and turning to run.

He saw Ione. He saw her step out into the doorway in front of them with her arms out, shouting, and the riggers beside her. He saw the crowd hit them like a wave hitting a wall. He saw the riggers go down under it, and Ione go down, and the wave go over them and out into the lanes.

"*Osk.*"

The big Warden was already moving.

* * *

Afterwards, what he remembered was pieces.

The cart. Pim, white-faced, dragging it through a side door, and Osk lifting him into it bodily, and then Osk himself between the shafts, hauling it at a run through the glittering lamp-strung lanes with the cart bouncing and banging and Cas clinging to its sides. People running beside them, past them, all going the same way. Toward the Drop District. Toward the wells.

The well. The same well, the one he'd come up. The WATCH · REMAIN · AWAIT circle painted round its mouth. There were people all round it. A crowd, pressing up the ramp to the platform, pressing against the scrap-metal wall. And on the platform, round the rim of the conduit, a line of them, going over. Going down the ladder. One after another, their hands on the rungs, their faces set and wild and joyful, climbing down into the dark toward the Outside.

He was out of the cart. He didn't remember getting out. He was on the platform — Osk had cleared a way, Osk was a wall — and he was at the lip of the well, grabbing at people. Grabbing at coats, at arms. *No. No, it isn't open, please, it isn't—* A man shook him off without even looking at him. A woman slapped his hand away. *He came up it*, someone said, *he came up it, he's lying, he wants to keep it for himself —*

There was a father with a child.

A young man, stocky and wide like all of them, with a little boy of four or five on his back, the boy's arms round his neck. He was at the ladder. He had one foot on the first rung.

Cas got the boy.

He didn't decide to. He simply had him. He had the child under the arms and was pulling, and the little boy was screaming, and the father was screaming, twisting round on the ladder, reaching back, *give him to me, give him, he's mine —* and Cas held on, and fell backwards onto the platform with the child on top of him, and the father hung there on the ladder with his face working, looking at his son, looking down into the dark.

"Come back up," said Cas. He was crying. "Please. Please. Come back up. It isn't open. I swear to you. I swear it."

The father looked at him for a moment with an expression he would never forget. Then he looked at his son. Then he looked down again, into the well, toward the Outside, and his face changed. It filled with something. Hope. Joy. The same joy Cas had seen in the Commons the first night, turned up at him like light.

"I'll come back for him," the father said. "I'll come back up for him. When I've seen it."

And he went down.

* * *

The screaming started about a quarter of an hour later.

It came up the well. Faint, at first, and very far, from three hundred metres down, where the conduit came out of the bottom of the Anvil into the Shear. It came up the shaft the way the Bell did — stretched, somehow. Slowed. Strange. It didn't sound like screaming. It sounded like the lowing of cattle. Like the long slow groan of Teo's voice through the suit. It went on and on, and more voices joined it, one by one, as more of them reached the bottom. It went on for a long time. Nobody who was on that platform ever forgot it.

The people still on the ladder stopped. They hung there, on the rungs, listening. Then they began to come back up.

The riggers came. Ione came — with blood on her face from a cut over her eye and her left arm held against her body — and a dozen riggers behind her with rope and knives. They went to every well in the District. There were fourteen. At every one they cut the tops of the ladders free and hauled them up so that nobody else could go down. It took most of a shift. At some of the wells they had to fight to do it.

The Tollers counted. That was what Tollers did. They went from well to well with their ledgers and asked the people on the platforms who had gone down, and wrote down the names.

Forty-seven.

He was sitting on the platform of the well with his back against the scrap-metal wall and the little boy asleep in his lap when Osk came to arrest him.

He had been sitting there for hours. The boy had cried himself to sleep. Nobody had come for him. Nobody had known who he was. Cas had sat with him, and held him, and listened to the sounds coming up the well until they stopped, and then listened to the silence, and then to the Bell.

Osk stood over him without saying anything. His hard hat was gone. There was a long scrape down one side of his face.

"The First Steward's asked for you," he said at last.

"I know."

"I'm to bring you."

"I know."

Osk looked down at the boy.

"I'll take him," said a voice, and it was Pim — Pim with his face streaked black and grey, Pim who had been everywhere, who had carried water and run messages and dragged the cart — and he knelt down and held out his arms. "I know his gran. On Pump Lane. I'll take him to her."

Cas gave him the boy. The child didn't wake. Pim staggered a little under the weight and settled him, and looked at Cas, and didn't say anything, and went.

Osk put out a hand. Cas took it. The big man pulled him up — gently, the way he'd taught him, *stack your bones* — and stood holding his arm.

"I told them," said Cas. "I told them three times."

"I know," said Osk. "I heard."

Across the platform, at the top of the ramp, there was a disturbance. Wardens — six of them, eight — with staves. And in the middle of them, struggling, Ione. She'd seen Osk take his arm. She was fighting to get to him. She got one arm free and a Warden caught it and she twisted, and the one holding her bad arm wrenched it — not hard, but it was the bad arm — and she cried out, and dropped, and rolled, and was up, and was over the scrap-metal wall of the platform in one long movement, the way she'd gone over it the first day, and gone.

The Wardens shouted and ran to the wall and looked over it and didn't follow.

"Good," said Osk very softly, to no one.

* * *

The Quiet was a coolant tank.

It was underneath the Steward's House, down a long iron stair into the roots of the building, where the old lift terminal had had its machinery. A steel drum, three metres across, three metres deep, set into the keelstone floor, with a round lid on a hinge like the lid of a well. There was a ladder down into it. When Osk took him down the stair and opened the lid, the smell that came up was old water and old rust and something else, something human, very faint, that had soaked into the metal over a hundred and sixty-eight years.

"Down," said Osk.

He went down. It wasn't far. At the bottom his feet touched a steel floor, damp. He looked up. Osk's face in the round of the lid, with the lamp-light behind it. For a moment Osk looked as though he were going to say something.

Then the lid came down.

It was the darkest dark he had ever been in. Not the dark of a room at night, where there's always some light, somewhere, a line under a door, a glow from a window. The Lamp had never set on Holdfast; he hadn't seen real dark for nearly twenty rounds. This was total. He held his hand in front of his face and saw nothing. He shut his eyes and opened them and it made no difference at all.

And it was silent. The Steward's House was above him, and the city above that, and the city was — he knew it was — in chaos, in grief, in the streets, the whole of Holdfast awake and shouting. He couldn't hear any of it. The steel walls and the keelstone and the stone of the House lay over him like a hand over a mouth, and there was nothing, nothing, only the sound of his own breath and his own heart.

He sat down on the damp steel floor with his back against the curved wall.

He sat there.

Some time later — he had no idea how long — it came up through the floor. Rising. Humming in the steel. Filling the tank like water filling a glass, until the whole drum was ringing faintly with it, a single deep note in the dark. It hung. It lasted. It went away.

The Bell. The hundred and twenty-one thousand, nine hundred and forty-eighth, or the forty-ninth. He didn't know. He'd lost count, for the first time since his flat on Tier Nine. The first time since 02:14.

He found that he was counting again. Not the Bell.

Forty-seven.
