---
title: Lamp-Weather
---

> Remember that the Lesion is not a place. It is a condition of the place around it. Near it, even the weather is a kind of arithmetic.
> — Iris Hollowell, *On the Sealing of Debts* (withdrawn)

Above the best Lee on Spindle Four there was a place the riggers called the Breach.

Ione showed it to him from below, the morning they left the cache — if it was morning; the city under them had only just begun, very slowly, to open its eyes. She pointed up through the narrowing lattice. Two hundred metres above them, the spindle's structure changed. The struts that had braced it all the way up, the great black beams that made the Lees, simply stopped. For a stretch of perhaps sixty metres, one whole side of the spindle was open — the lattice there had fallen or been stripped in the Founders' time, or had never been finished — and there was only a single spine of keelstone running up through the gap like the backbone of a fish, with the rungs of an old ladder bolted to it, and on either side of it, nothing. Violet air. The Lace.

"No Lees in the Breach," said Ione. "Nothing to hold against the Lamp. You go up it in one go. Fast. You don't stop. There's a hut at the top, in the last big Lee, under the gallery. You don't stop till the hut."

"How long?"

"Fast? A quarter of a shift. For me." She looked at him. "For you, in that—"

"Longer."

"Longer." She began to uncoil her rope. "And you don't look at the Lace. It's close up there. It's very close. You look at the rungs."

* * *

He looked at the rungs.

He looked at them. He looked at them as they came down past his visor one after the other, black iron, pitted, bolted to the black keelstone spine, and he looked at his gauntlets closing on them, and he looked at the rope going up from his harness to Ione, climbing above him with the coil on her shoulder, fast and neat and steady. He didn't look out. He didn't look down. He could feel the open air on both sides of him the way you can feel someone standing behind you in a dark room. He could feel the weight — three times what he'd weighed on the Tier Nine arterial, three and a half, the exo screaming in every joint, the keel's note straining high in his spine.

And he could feel the Lace.

Not see it. He didn't look. But he could feel it, off to the left, where the spindle was open: a kind of pressure on the side of his face. A prickle. The sense of something enormous and delicate hanging very near.

"Chalk," said Ione above him, and he heard the soft puff of her blowing it, and a pause. "Crooked. Left. That's fine. Keep coming."

He kept coming.

"Chalk," said Ione, a while later. A pause. Longer. "*Very* crooked. Keep coming. Faster."

He went faster. His arms were two things he had borrowed from someone who hadn't wanted them back. The rungs came down past his face.

"Chalk," said Ione.

Silence.

He looked up. He couldn't help it. She was twenty metres above him, braced on the spine with her boots on a rung and her free hand held out flat in front of her, palm up. The puff of chalk had come off it. It hadn't fallen. It hadn't drifted sideways. It was going *up*. A little white cloud, rising slowly off her hand, straight up, toward the Lamp.

*When chalk falls up, pray.*

"Cas," said Ione, very calmly. "Hold on."

* * *

It hit them like a wave hits a pier.

First the weight went. All of it, all at once — the crushing three-and-a-half that had been on him for a thousand metres simply *vanished*, and he was hanging on the rungs weighing nothing, and his stomach came up into his throat and his body, braced against a weight that wasn't there, threw itself away from the ladder. The exo jerked. His gauntlets clamped. He hung there, floating, attached to the spine by his fists.

Then it came back. From the side.

Down swung. Not gently, the way it had in the Works. It *whipped*. One moment there was no down, and the next down was off to his right, out through the open side of the spindle, into the violet air, and it was not three and a half, it was more — five, six, he didn't know — and he was hanging off the side of the spine by his hands like a flag in a gale, his legs streaming out sideways over nothing, the whole weight of him and the suit trying to tear his arms out of their sockets and fling him out into the Lace.

He heard the keel scream.

And above him, he heard the rope go.

It wasn't loud. A sort of flat crack, like a whip, and a hiss. He looked up. Ione's rope, where it ran from her harness down to his, had been lying across the edge of the keelstone spine — the raw broken edge where the lattice had fallen away. Keelstone didn't break. But when it did, the edges were like glass. The rope had sawn itself across the edge in the swing, and parted, and the end of it came whipping down past his visor.

And Ione came off the spine.

She'd been braced with her boots on a rung and her free hand out for the chalk. When down swung, she'd grabbed. Her splinted hand had closed on the rung. And he watched it not hold.

She came off. She went out, sideways, into the air on the open side of the spindle — the weight flinging her, the way it was trying to fling him — and she didn't fall, she *flew*, out and down and past him, a small dark shape tumbling, her arms out, the white streak in her hair, her mouth open, and she was two metres from him and three and she was going out into the violet air toward the Lace—

He let go with one hand.

He never remembered deciding. His right hand came off the rung and went out and the suit's gauntlet closed on the loop of rope that still hung from her harness, a metre of frayed end, and the grip-assist clamped on it so hard that he felt the fibres crush. The weight of her came onto his arm like a steel bar. His left hand, alone on the rung, screamed. The exo screamed.

He pulled.

The keel's bubble was a metre. That was all. A metre of stirred-flat spacetime round his body, where down was one direction instead of every direction at once. He hauled on the rope with an arm that had nothing left in it and dragged her in through the air toward him, and she came, and she hit him — hit the suit, chest to chest, her face against his visor, her arms coming round him — and was inside it.

And the weather stopped.

Not outside. Outside it went on — he could see it, over her shoulder: the violet air, the Lace, the open side of the spindle all tilting and swinging, the chalk-cloud Ione had blown whirling past in a spiral. But inside the bubble, inside the metre, down was simply down. The keel's note was a scream in his spine but it held. It averaged it all out. Down was one way, and it was heavy, very heavy, but it was *one way*, and they were both in it, pressed together against the spine of Spindle Four, his left hand locked on a rung and his right arm round her and her arms round him, holding on.

"*Don't let go*," she said against his visor. Her voice came through the helmet thin and close. "Don't let go, don't let go—"

"I've got you."

"—*don't let go*—"

"I've got you, Ione. I've got you."

It went on for a long time. He never knew how long. It came in gusts. Down would swing round the outside of the bubble like a wind round a house, and he'd feel the keel strain to hold it, and the metre around them would flex and warp — the rungs in front of his face stretching, bending, as though seen through water — and hold, and settle. The weight surged and dropped. Once it dropped to nothing and he felt her start to float away from him, just slightly, and pulled her back hard, and she made a sound, and held on harder. Once it surged so hard that the spine of keelstone they were clinging to *groaned*, a vast deep sound through the metal, and a rung below them sheared off its bolts and went spinning out into the air and was gone.

Her face was against his visor. He could see her eyes. They were very close and very wide, and for the first time since the top of the well there was nothing patient in them at all. They were frightened. They were a frightened seventeen-year-old's eyes, in a thirty-year-old's face, looking at him from three centimetres away through a sheet of glass.

He held her. He didn't have anything else to do.

* * *

When it passed, it passed all at once. Down came back to down — straight down, the old crushing three-and-a-half, the Lamp pressing on them from above. The violet air outside stopped swinging. The chalk was gone.

They hung there on the spine, the two of them, for a while longer. Neither of them moved.

Then Ione said, into his visor, very quietly: "The hut's forty metres up."

"All right."

"I can't let go of you. If I let go of you I'm out of the bubble and I'll weigh more than twice what I weigh at home and I've only got one hand."

"Then don't let go of me."

"You'll have to climb with me on you."

"All right."

She looked at him through the glass for a moment longer. Then she shifted — got her legs round his waist, her good arm round his neck-ring, her splinted hand fisted in the webbing of his harness — and put her cheek against the side of his helmet, and held on.

He climbed.

He didn't know how. He never knew, afterwards, how. Forty metres. A hundred and thirty-odd rungs, with the whole weight of both of them — less in the bubble; not less enough — on arms that had nothing in them, the exo whining in a high thin continuous note like a dentist's drill, the keel shrieking in his spine. He climbed with her pressed against him, her breath hot on the side of his helmet, and he counted rungs because it was the only thing he knew how to do, and somewhere past a hundred he stopped being able to count, and just climbed.

At the top of the Breach the lattice came back. Great black struts closing round them. Chalk falling crooked. A Lee — a deep one; the weight lifted off them like a coat taken off their shoulders — and in it, wedged into the angle of three massive beams, a hut.

He got them onto its platform. He didn't remember that either. He remembered lying on his back on old planks inside a low black room with Ione still wrapped round him, both of them shaking, and the keel's note slowly coming down from its scream, and her face in the crook of his neck-ring, and her voice, muffled:

"You let go of the rung."

"Yes."

"With one hand. In lamp-weather. In the Breach."

"Yes."

A long pause.

"Nobody's ever done anything that stupid for me," said Ione Tally. And then, after another pause, very quietly, into the neck-ring of his suit, she began to cry.

* * *

The hut was keelstone. Riggers had built it — generations ago, in the Founders' time or just after, when the riggers were still going up the spindles to tend the old lamp-lines — out of off-cut slabs of keelstone laid against the three great struts, so that the walls pulled with the struts and the Lee inside was deep and quiet and almost flat. There was a door, of iron plate. There was a window, a slit, looking out through the lattice at the Lace. There was a shelf with things on it that crumbled when you touched them. There was a water-catcher outside the door, and water in it.

And it was calm. Down was down, and not very much of it — about what it had been in the city, Ione said. After the Breach it felt like floating.

He took the suit off.

He'd been in it since the foot of the spindle, days ago — four sleeps; five — and when it opened down the back and he stepped out of it in the grey liner he nearly fell, again, the way he had at every big Lee. His legs had forgotten how to carry him. Ione caught him. They sat down together on the planks, hard, and stayed there, backs against the black wall, not talking.

The suit stood in the corner of the hut, the keel humming. It had dropped again. He could hear it. The storm had cost it.

After a while he got up and went to it, and looked at the display on the inside of the helmet. The clocks said LOCAL 03:14 and OUTSIDE 11:62. There was no such time as 11:62. As he watched, OUTSIDE said 04:0? and then a string of symbols and then nothing. The radio icon was grey. The little green light on the recorder, in its chest compartment, was still lit.

"It's going," said Ione, behind him.

"The electronics. Yes. Ben said they would."

"Will it still—" She stopped.

"The keel's mechanical. The exo's got a manual fallback. The air's mostly mechanical." He put the helmet down. "It'll still get us through the Lace."

*Us*, he thought. He didn't say anything else.

* * *

She washed his face. He didn't ask her to. She got water from the catcher outside in a tin that didn't crumble and a strip of cloth from her pack and knelt in front of him where he sat against the wall and washed his face, carefully, the way you'd clean a wound, and then his neck, where the neck-ring of the suit had rubbed it raw. Then she re-bound her own wrist, one-handed, with her teeth, and wouldn't let him help.

"Is it broken?"

"It was broken before. It's more broken now." She tied it off. "It'll do."

He watched her. The stitches over her eye. The white streak. The rope-scarred forearms. The splint.

"Can I tell you something?" he said.

She looked up.

"Nobody knows it," he said. "Except a man in a cardigan who works for the secret police. And Teo, a bit. And the man in the cardigan only knows what I did, not—" He stopped. "Not the rest."

She sat back on her heels. She didn't speak. She didn't nod. She just waited, the way she'd waited at the top of the well, the way she'd waited through a hundred and seventy-five thousand cycles, as if there were nothing he could say that she hadn't already seen, and she'd be interested to see it again.

So he told her.

He told her about the Margin Review. Year Seventy-Six. About Dr Adeyemi, sixty-eight, who walked everywhere very fast with his hands in his pockets, who used to put his palm flat on the walls of the Works and listen. About the nine months they'd spent on it, the two of them and a handful of analysts, going through seventy-five years of Rollover figures. About the night he'd first run the projections himself, alone, in the Review's borrowed office on Tier Two, and watched the Margin line go down past one and keep going, and sat there until dawn. About the report. *A structure of obligation that cannot, even in principle, be discharged.* Adeyemi had written that. Cas had read it over his shoulder and thought it was the truest sentence he had ever seen.

He told her about the meeting. The Ministry. A long table in a room with gold carpet. Very courteous people. Nobody raised their voice. They explained that the projections were, of course, only projections. That the Review had not taken into account the planned improvements to the Lattice. That publishing a figure like that — a figure like *negative margin* — in the current climate, with the export treaties being negotiated, would be irresponsible. Would cause panic. Would hurt people. Real people, now, today. That the future would find solutions; the future always had. That all that was needed was a revised conclusion. A small one. A sentence. *The Margin remains stable within projected parameters.*

"Adeyemi said no," said Cas. "He stood up and said no, and walked out. They put him on leave. He died two years later. Heart, they said. He was seventy." He looked at his hands. "They asked me."

"And you said yes."

"They didn't make me. That's the thing. That's what nobody — they didn't threaten me. They didn't mention my family. They just asked. They put the revised report in front of me and a pen next to it and they asked, very politely, whether I'd be willing to sign. And I thought about it for — I don't know. Ten seconds. And I thought: *it'll probably be fine*. And I thought: *somebody else will fix it*. And I thought: *who am I to say*." He was quiet. "And I noticed that it was a nice pen. That's the thing I remember. I remember thinking what a nice pen it was. It wrote very smoothly."

He heard the sentence as he said it. *It'll probably be fine.* He'd thought it again, not so long ago — at the top of a gallery stair in the Works, looking at a thread in a wall — and a boy had gone down the stair two at a time, grinning. He hadn't noticed, then, that it was the same sentence. He noticed now.

Ione said nothing.

"They gave me the Standby. A flat by the Pit and a salary and a phone that never rang. And I sat in it for fifteen years." He found that his voice had gone strange. "I didn't do anything. I didn't — I lost Sabine, because I couldn't talk to her, because the only thing I could have talked about was the thing I couldn't say. I saw Nell every other weekend and took her to the museum and didn't say anything to her either. I got up every morning and did nothing and went to bed. The same day. Every day. Fifteen years. You said — you said about the Carousel. That it was only horrible for whoever was watching. I've been watching one day for fifteen years. The day with the pen in it."

He looked at her.

"When the Draw broke," he said, "I sat on my bed and listened to the hammer, and I didn't feel frightened. I felt—" He made himself say it. "I felt relieved. I thought: *at last*. I thought: *now somebody will have to send me in*. And I thought I'd go in and it would — settle it. Somewhere in here. The Shear, or the Crown, or wherever. I thought I'd die in here and it would be—" He stopped. "It seemed tidy."

He'd never said it out loud before. He'd never said it to himself.

"I came in here to die," said Casimir Hale. "That's why. Teo asked me, at dinner, the night before, and I didn't answer her. That's why."

* * *

Ione was quiet for a long time.

Outside the slit of window, the Lace hung in the violet air, very close, its threads moving slowly like weed in a current. The keel hummed in the corner. Far below, somewhere, the city was still in its night.

"You think it's a debt," said Ione at last.

He looked at her.

"What you did. With the pen." She was sitting with her back against the wall opposite him, her knees drawn up, her splinted wrist resting on them. "You think it's a debt, and you've been paying it. For fifteen years. Every day. And you came in here to pay the rest of it off. All at once. With yourself." She tilted her head. "Is that it?"

"Something like that."

"And has it got any smaller?"

He opened his mouth. He shut it.

"No," said Ione. "It hasn't. Has it. You've been paying it every day for fifteen years and it's bigger than it was. Because that's how your kind of debt works." She said it without any cruelty at all, the way she'd explained the Lees. "You told us. In the Commons. You borrow, and you pay it back, and it's always more. You pay the interest, and you pay the interest on the interest, and it grows, and it grows, and you can never, ever get to the bottom of it. That's what you people *built*. You built a whole city on that. You built this whole place on it." She moved her hand, taking in the hut, the spindle, the Lamp. "And then you went and built a man on it too."

He didn't move.

"I watched a woman make the worst choice of her life," said Ione, "a hundred and seventy-five thousand times. Ruth. At the board. *One-thirty-five.* She closes her eyes for half a second first. Every time. And every time I sat next to her and I thought, *don't*. Every time. A hundred and seventy-five thousand times I thought *don't*, and a hundred and seventy-five thousand times she did it anyway. Because it was already done. Because it was just light. Because she only ever got to do it once, and she'd done it, and there was nothing anyone could ever do about it, not her, not me, not anyone." She looked at him steadily. "She never got to make another choice. Not in the gallery. The ghost never does. It just does the one thing, for ever.

"But the real Ruth did. She went down the lift, and she made the next choice, and the one after. She got married. She had children. She wrote letters to a little girl for fifty years. She counted the Bells. She *built the Tollers*, Cas. My whole family. Everything I am. She made that out of the worst nine minutes of her life. She didn't pay it off. You can't pay it off. She just — went on. She made the next thing."

She leaned forward.

"You're not a ghost," she said. "You're not the light in the gallery. You signed it once. That's all. Once. It's true, and it'll always be true, and there's nothing you can do about it. But nobody makes you do it again every morning. That's you. You do that. You've been standing in the gallery watching yourself sign it for fifteen years, thinking *don't*. You can walk out. You can make the next thing." She sat back. "That's all. That's all I know."

He couldn't speak. He sat against the black keelstone wall with his hands open on his knees and looked at her and his eyes stung and blurred and he let them.

After a while she got up and came across the hut and sat down beside him, against the wall, close, her shoulder against his shoulder. She didn't say anything else. She just sat there.

* * *

Later — much later — she said, "Nobody's touched me in eleven years."

She said it to the window, not to him. Her shoulder was still against his.

"Since I came down. The Unwatched. You don't get touched. People won't hand you things. They put them down and step back, so you have to pick them up. Gran touched me. And Pim, he doesn't care, he's eleven, he climbs on everyone. That's all. Eleven years." She was quiet. "In the Carousel, I used to lie next to Ruth. At the board. With my head on her shoulder. And she'd go right through me."

He turned his head and looked at her.

She turned hers. They looked at each other, close, in the violet light from the slit of window.

"You held on," she said. "In the Breach. You had me by the rope and you pulled me in and you held on. And I could feel you. Through the suit. I could feel your arms."

"Ione—"

"I know," she said. "I know. You're from Outside and I'm from here and we're going up there, the two of us, to something that probably kills us. I know all of it. I've thought all of it. I've thought it since the top of the well." She put her hand, her good hand, flat on his chest, on the grey liner, over his heart. "I've had eleven years of thinking. I'm tired of thinking."

He put his hand over hers.

* * *

It was clumsy. That was what he remembered, afterwards, and was glad of: how clumsy it was.

The liner was a one-piece with a seam down the back that had been designed to be opened by a machine, and neither of them could work out how to get it undone, and she laughed at him trying, and then he laughed, and then she was laughing so hard she had to put her forehead against his shoulder and stop. Her clothes were a rigger's clothes, a tunic and a harness and a belt and trousers with keelstone-cloth knees, and every piece of them was knotted in some rigger's way he'd never seen, and she wouldn't show him, she made him work them out, one after another, and he did. Her wrist got in the way. His back, where the keel had sat for five sleeps, had a bruise across it the size of a dinner plate, and when she put her hand on it he hissed, and she took her hand away, and he put it back.

The weight in the Lee was Holdfast weight. Heavy. Everything heavy — her body on his, his on hers, their hands, their breath — as if the world were pressing them together. She was strong. Much stronger than him, in that weight, and very sure, and then, suddenly, not sure at all, holding very still with her face against his neck and her breath going fast, and he held still too, and waited, and she said *it's all right, it's all right, I'm just—* and didn't finish, and didn't need to.

Afterwards they lay on the planks of the hut under his coat and hers, tangled, her head on his chest, and he could feel her heartbeat and his, in the heavy stillness, not quite together.

"Your heart's too fast," she said drowsily.

"It's the weight."

"It's not the weight." She moved her head. "I've been listening to it since the top of the well. It's always too fast. Outside hearts must all be too fast."

"Maybe."

"Mm." She was quiet a while. Her fingers moved on his chest, very slightly, tracing something. He realised after a while that it was a knot. The riggers' hitch. Over, under, round, through. "Cas."

"Yes."

"When it's — whatever it is. Up there." She stopped. "I don't want to talk about it. I only want to say that I'm glad. That's all. Whatever happens. I'm glad it was you that came up the well."

He put his hand on her hair. The white streak, under his fingers, was softer than the rest.

"So am I," he said.

* * *

She slept. He didn't, for a while.

When he was sure she was asleep, he eased out from under her — very slowly, very carefully, putting his rolled-up coat under her head — and went across the hut to the suit in the corner, and opened the chest compartment, and took out the recorder. Its little green light was still on. He sat down on the planks with his back against the suit's leg and held it.

Then he pressed the button. The second channel.

"Nell," he said, very quietly, so as not to wake her.

He didn't know what came next. He sat in the dim violet light and listened to Ione breathe and the keel hum, and let it come.

"I've been thinking about what you said. About being scared. That it's allowed, and you just have to do it anyway." He paused. "I think I got something wrong. For a long time. I think I thought — I thought being brave meant not minding what happened to you. I thought if you didn't mind, it couldn't hurt. So I stopped minding. About anything. For years. I thought that was — I don't know. Being grown up. Being sensible." He stopped. "It isn't. It's just being scared all the time and calling it something else."

The keel hummed.

"So I want to tell you something. And I want you to hear it from me, even if it's a long time from now, even if it's from a machine. Because I've never said it. Not to you, not to your mother, not to anyone. Not even to me."

He looked across the hut at Ione asleep under the coats, the white streak in her hair, her splinted hand curled under her chin.

"I want to live, Nell," said Casimir Hale. "I want to live. I don't know if I'm going to. But I want to. I want to very much."

He held the button down a moment longer, letting it record the silence after, and the keel, and her breathing.

Then he let go.
