> Then we are relieved.
> — The Common Office, final response

The first thing she noticed was that she was going to float away.

She stood on the stair in the black with one hand on the rail and the keel humming round her, and her body told her — very clearly, very urgently, the way your body tells you that you've missed a step in the dark — that there was nothing holding her down. That she weighed nothing. That if she let go of the rail she'd drift up off the stair like a puff of chalk and be lost.

She held on to the rail. She stood very still. She waited for it to pass.

It didn't pass. And after a while she understood that it wasn't going to, because it wasn't a gust. It wasn't lamp-weather. It was just the weight. The Lamp had been pushing on her from above every minute of her life, every minute of every Holdfast life for a hundred and sixty-eight years, and it had stopped. The Anvil was still there, pulling. The world was still there, under the Anvil, pulling. But the push was gone. And what was left was—

*Ordinary*, she thought. *This is what they weigh. Outside. This is ordinary.*

She let go of the rail. She didn't float away. She stood on the stair and weighed what a woman weighs, which was less than she'd ever weighed in her life, and it was so strange that she laughed out loud in the suit — a short, cracked, frightened laugh that went out into the dark and didn't come back.

Because there was no echo. There was no sound at all.

She hadn't known there was a sound until it stopped. The Lamp had made one. The Lace had made one. It had been there all her life, under everything — under the Bell, under the voices in the lanes, under the wind in the ropes — so faint and so constant that nobody in Holdfast had ever heard it any more than they heard their own blood. A hum. A singing. It had been getting louder on the Rim, at the end, the singing in the gold light. And now it was gone, and the silence where it had been was so enormous that she could hear her own heart in the suit, and the keel's hum, and nothing else in the world.

She looked up.

Nothing. Black. Not dark — dark was the Quiet, dark was the tank with the lid shut. This was more than dark. It went up and up above her and didn't stop, and there was nothing in it anywhere. No Lamp. No Lace. No Rim. No Crown. She tried to see where the Crown should be — up there, at the top of Spindle One, where he was — and there was nothing to see. Black on black. It could have been a hand's width from her face. It could have been the edge of the world.

*Somewhere in it*, she thought. *Once.*

She stood on the stair for a long time, looking up at nothing, with the soft band of light round the keel on her back glowing on the rungs.

Then she took out her chalk, and held her hand out flat, and blew.

The chalk went off her palm in a little cloud, lit by the keel's glow. And fell. Straight down. In a thin white curtain, straight as a plumb line, down past the stair and on into the dark.

True chalk. On Spindle Four, three kilometres up. Where there had never, in a hundred and sixty-eight years, been anything but crooked.

She watched it go until she couldn't see it.

"There," she said to him, out loud, into the dark, in case he could hear. He couldn't. She said it anyway. "There. It's done. You did it."

Then she started to climb down.

* * *

It took her nine hours. She didn't know it was nine hours; the suit's clocks were dead, and she hadn't brought anything else. She knew it afterwards, because the Tollers told her. At the time it was only stairs.

Stairs in the dark. Ladders in the dark. The keel's little glow on the rungs, a metre of soft light round her and black beyond it. She'd climbed Spindle Four a dozen times since she was nine, and she'd never climbed it like this — never with nothing pushing, never without the Lees. There were no Lees now. The struts were still there, the great black beams, and she went past them and felt them pull, faintly, the way the Anvil pulled; but there was no push for them to pull against, and so there was no calm, and no storm, and no difference between one place and the next. Only down. The same down everywhere. Straight chalk everywhere.

It was easy. That was the horror of it, at first, and then the wonder. It was easy. Her legs were Holdfast legs, built for half as much again, and in the ordinary weight they felt like springs. She went down the ladders in the suit faster than she'd ever gone down them in her life. She stopped at the hut and didn't go in. She stopped at the best Lee, the one with the four struts, where they'd slept and the chalk had hung in the air like a cloud — and blew chalk, and watched it fall straight, and stood there for a while, and went on.

She thought about him. She thought about him the whole way down. She tried not to, and then she stopped trying.

She thought about the needle. About his hand on the brass wheel, turning it, a little this way and a little that. About him looking down through the great window at a small light on Spindle Four. About what he'd seen, at the end. Her light, past the Breach. Her light, stopped on a walkway. Her arm raised.

*He saw me raise my hand.*

She had to stop on a ladder when she thought that, and hold on, and wait until she could see again.

She didn't know if he'd seen it. She didn't know if his eyes had been on her or on the needle, whether he'd looked up in time. She'd never know. She understood, holding on to the ladder in the dark, that she was going to spend the rest of her life not knowing, and that she was going to have to decide what she believed, and that nobody would ever be able to tell her if she was right.

She decided. Right there, on the ladder.

*He saw me.*

She went on down.

* * *

At about two kilometres, she saw the city.

She'd been looking down for it for hours. There'd been nothing — the dark below as black as the dark above, the keel's glow on the rungs and nothing past it. And then she came round the corner of a flight of stairs onto a walkway on the spindle's inner side, where you could see down through the open lattice to the Anvil, and there it was.

It was lights.

Not the Lamp's light. Not violet, not white, not one great glare over everything. Little lights. Points. Tiny and warm and yellow, thousands and thousands of them, spread out across the black floor of the world. Scattered through the scaffold towers. Strung along the lanes. Clustered round the spindles' feet. Some of them still, some of them moving — carried, she thought; lamps being carried through the lanes. Each one so small, alone, that it hardly lit anything. And all of them together making the shape of the city, laid out in the dark below her like a map drawn in fire.

The lamps. The oil lamps. The ones they'd been making in their kitchens for eleven rounds, pressing algae, twisting wicks, while he was in the Quiet and the Stewardship was saying the Relief was a demon from the Drop. They'd lit them.

She stood on the walkway and looked at it.

*Points of light*, he'd said. In the loft, lying on the floor, when she'd asked him what the stars were like. *Little points of light in the dark. Thousands of them. They don't do anything. They're just there.*

"Oh," said Ione Tally.

She stood there a long time.

* * *

Pim was at the bottom.

She heard him before she saw him. She was on the last ladder down into the wreck of the lift terminal at the foot of the spindle, and there was a lamp below, and voices, and then a high cracked voice shouted, "*There — there! There's a light! On the — it's the suit, it's the suit, it's the Relief, he's —*"

She came down off the ladder into the wreckage, into the lamp-light.

Pim was there, and Osk, and Bett from the riggers' tower, and a dozen others. Holding lamps. They'd been waiting at the foot of Spindle Four, in the dark, in the wreck. Pim was at the front. He had a clay lamp in each hand and a rat on each shoulder and his face was streaked black with soot and wet with crying, and he was running at her — at the suit — with his arms out and the lamps swinging and the rats clinging on—

And then he saw her face.

He stopped. Just stopped, a metre away, his arms still out. The light from his lamps went up into the visor. She saw him see her. She saw him look at her face, small in the big helmet. And then look at the suit. And then look past her, at the ladder, up into the dark. And then back at her face.

He didn't say anything. He was eleven, and he'd been everywhere, and heard everything, and carried the Relief's recorder out through a vent. He looked at her. And he put the lamps down on the ground, very carefully, one and then the other. And he came and put his arms round the suit's leg and held on to it and pressed his face against the plating and didn't say anything at all.

Osk had taken off his hard hat.

He stood there in the lamp-light at the foot of the spindle, the huge slow man, and held it against his chest the way he'd held it on the first day, at the top of the well, when he'd knelt. He didn't kneel now. He looked at her. At the suit. And he bowed his head.

Behind him, the others did the same.

She opened the suit. She hadn't known she was going to. She found the clasp at the neck and the seals and it opened down the back the way it had opened for Cas a hundred times, and she stepped out of it in her rigger's clothes onto the rubble at the foot of Spindle Four, and nearly fell, because she was so light — and Osk caught her. The way he'd caught Cas, the first day. Under the arms. And held her up.

"Stack your bones," said Osk, gently.

"I know how," said Ione.

She stood. She looked at them. At the lamps. At the dark.

"He's up there," she said. "He's — it worked. It's done. He did it. The Lamp's closed." Her voice was quite steady. "He's not coming down. He's the witness. He's in it."

Nobody said anything.

"The Drop's a road," said Ione Tally. "He told you. When the Lamp goes out, the Drop's a road. It's out. The Drop's a road."

Somewhere behind Osk, in the dark, somebody started to cry.

And then — faintly, far off, from somewhere out in the city, carried on the still dark air — somebody else began to shout it. *The Drop's a road.* And another voice took it up. And another. She heard it go out across Holdfast, voice by voice, lamp by lamp, the way the shutter-wave went out at dusk. *The Drop's a road. The Drop's a road. He said. When the Lamp goes out. He said.*

* * *

She went to the Tower first.

She knew she should go to the Commons. She knew there'd be thousands there, and fear, and the Stewards, and Crane. She knew there were people in the lanes who hadn't believed him — she could hear them, now and then, among the other voices: screaming, praying, somewhere a woman shrieking *demon, demon, he's killed the Lamp* over and over. She knew all of it.

She went to the Tower first.

The square round it was full of lamps. The Tollers had put them out on every ledge and step and platform of the tower, all the way up round the column, so that the whole spiralling scaffold was outlined in little yellow flames, and the black keelstone pillar in the middle of it, with its hundred and sixty-eight years of chalk, gleamed in their light like wet stone. People were standing in the square, looking up at it. Just standing. Holding their own lamps.

And the Bell was coming.

She felt it before she heard it. It came up through the soles of her boots, through the keelstone of the square — not a swell. Not a tide. Not the long slow rising pressure she'd felt all her life, a little more often every year, until lately it had come every ten minutes. A *knock*. Short and sharp and deep, a single blow, like a fist on a door far below. And then silence. And then—

Another. And another.

Once a second. Once a second. Once a second.

She stood in the square with her hand on her chest and felt it, and the people standing round her with their lamps were feeling it too — she could see it in their faces, turned up toward the tower, toward the column, toward the Tollers on their platforms. Every face had the same look. A kind of fear. A kind of wonder. A kind of listening, the way you listen to a heartbeat when you've put your head on someone's chest.

*Knock. Knock. Knock.*

She went into the Tower.

The round room at the bottom was full of lamplight, and pendulums, and Tollers — every Toller there was, crammed in, standing round the walls, silent. Tobiah was at the column with a stick of chalk in his hand. He wasn't writing. He was just standing there, with his hand raised and the chalk in it, crying behind his lens-spectacles, because there was no way to write fast enough. Every second, another. There was no way to keep up.

And in her chair, with her hand flat on the stone, was Gran.

She was so small. She'd always been small, but she was smaller now, sunk down into the chair, her felt slippers not reaching the floor. Her face was grey. Her breathing was short and quick and shallow, and there was a blanket over her knees, and Steward Harrow was asleep in her lap. Her eyes were closed.

Ione knelt by the chair.

The old woman's eyes opened. Black and bright as a bird's. They went over her, in about a second, the way they went over everything.

"Hmph," said Perpetua Tally. "You're back."

"I'm back."

"On your own."

"Yes."

Gran's eyes held hers. They didn't ask. They didn't need to. They held her for a long moment, and something in the old face shifted, very slightly — not surprise; Gran was never surprised — and the knotted hand came off the column and found Ione's hand and held it.

"Did he?" said Gran.

"Yes."

"Good." She breathed. "Good. Good lad. Good."

The Bell came up through the column. *Knock.* And again. *Knock.* Gran's head tipped a little, listening.

"Listen to it," she said. "Listen. One a second. Hear that? One a second." Her mouth moved; it might have been a smile. "That's how fast they live out there. That's their *time*, Ione. That's the Outside's own time, coming up through my column. We've been counting their night for a hundred and sixty-eight years and now we're in it. Now we're *in* it." She shut her eyes. "One a second. No wonder they never get anything done."

Ione laughed. She couldn't help it. It came out of her as a sob.

"Now," said Gran. "The box."

Tobiah brought it. The dented steel box with the Works stencil. He put it on the old woman's knees, on top of the blanket, beside the rat, and she laid both her hands on its lid.

"Ruth's Log," she said. "And the letter. The last one. For the girl. For Maddy." She pushed it, very slightly, toward Ione. "You take it. Out. You take it out to her. That's the Tollers' job. It's always been the Tollers' job. Somebody from this family carries that letter to that little girl. We promised Ruth." Her breath rattled. "And you write the last page. In the Log. You write what happened. You write it properly. Every word."

"I will."

"Not now. Later. When you can." She opened her eyes. "You'll know when."

"Gran—"

"Hush." The knotted fingers squeezed. "Hush now. I'm listening."

They listened. The Bell came up through the column. *Knock.* The pendulums on the platforms above them trembled on their wires. *Knock.* The lamps on the ledges bowed their flames. *Knock.*

"Go on, then," said Perpetua Tally, very quietly, with her eyes closed. "Off you go, when it's time. Go and see if the Outside's all it's cracked up to be. Take them all with you."

*Knock.*

"Tell me how it ends," she said.

*Knock.*

And between that Bell and the next, with her granddaughter's hand in hers and her other hand on the rat asleep in her lap, the Head Toller of Holdfast stopped breathing.

Ione knelt by the chair. She didn't move. The Bell came up through the floor, and the column, and the chair, and Gran's still hand in hers, once a second, once a second, the way it would go on doing now for as long as anyone was there to feel it.

After a while, Tobiah went to the column and made a single mark in chalk at the bottom of the last row. Not a stroke. A short line across, and a word beside it.

*PERPETUA.*

* * *

The Commons was full.

Not with the sea of faces she'd seen from the back wall on the night the Relief came. With lamps. Thousands of them, held up, filling the great hall from wall to wall, so that the broken roof overhead was lit from below and the girders were gold and the strips of open sky between them were black — black, where the Lace had always been — and the Tollers' banner still hung from the gantry, Seren's line, white on black, lit by all those little flames.

And they were quiet. That was what she noticed when she came in by the great doors with Osk beside her and Pim holding her hand. Twenty thousand people and more, and they were quiet. Some of them were crying. Some of them were holding each other. Here and there, someone was praying, very softly, the words of the Placard. But they were waiting. They'd been waiting, she understood, for hours. In the dark, with their lamps. The way they'd been told to.

*When the dark comes, don't be afraid.*

The crowd saw her. She felt it go through them — a turning of heads, a stir — and they parted for her. Not stepping back from her, the way people had stepped back from the Unwatched for eleven years. Parting. Making a way. She walked down it toward the platform, and hands reached out as she went by and touched her arm, her shoulder, the rope on her shoulder, and she didn't know what to do with it, so she let them.

Halloran Crane was on the platform.

He was standing in front of the Placard. They'd brought it from the chapel — someone had carried it here, in its keelstone frame, and set it up on the platform on an easel, with a lamp before it. The yellowed laminate. The stick figure by its stick console. He was standing beside it in his black coat with the gold lamp on the breast, very straight, very old, holding a lamp of his own, and he watched her come down the aisle through the crowd, and when she reached the foot of the platform, by the brass rail of Muster Point B, he looked at her face.

He didn't ask either.

He turned to the Placard. He lifted his lamp so that its light fell on the words. And in a clear, carrying voice — the voice that had said *let him finish* — he read it aloud.

"Section Nine. In the event of containment loss. Remain at your station and await relief. Do not attempt to traverse the Lattice. Relief crews will be dispatched. You will not be forgotten."

He lowered the lamp.

"Who keeps the Watch?" said Halloran Crane.

The hall didn't answer at once. Then, from somewhere at the back, raggedly — and then from everywhere, twenty thousand voices, quietly, uncertainly, the way you'd answer a question you'd answered all your life and suddenly didn't know the meaning of:

"*We keep the Watch.*"

"Until?"

"*Until Relief.*"

"And when Relief comes?"

The hall was silent.

"Then," said Halloran Crane, "we are relieved."

He set his lamp down on the platform in front of the Placard.

"Relief has come," he said. "It came up out of the Drop, as we were always told it would. It wasn't what we were told. It was a man from the Outside, in a suit, who stood here and told us the truth, which we did not want. And then it went up the spindle with Ione Tally and closed the Lamp. The Lamp is out. The Watch is over." He looked out at the lamps. "We are relieved. Every one of us. You are all relieved. You have kept your station for a hundred and sixty-eight years and you may leave it now."

The sound the hall made was not a cheer. It was not a groan. She'd never heard it before and she never heard it again. It was the sound, she thought afterwards, that a great many people make when they have been holding something up for a very long time, and are told they can put it down.

Crane waited for it to pass.

"There's one more thing," he said, "and then I'll be done."

He looked down at her. At the foot of the platform, by the brass rail. He held her eyes.

"Thirteen years ago," said Halloran Crane, "Seren Tally painted the truth on the wall of this hall, and I gave her to the Drop for it. I had the order drawn up before she painted it. She was right, and I knew she was right, and I did it anyway, because I thought your calm was worth more than her life. I was wrong." His voice didn't shake at all. "I have nothing to offer her daughter. I'm not asking her for anything. I only wanted you all to hear me say it, once, in here, where she said it first."

The hall was very quiet.

Ione looked up at him. At the old man on the platform, in the lamplight, beside the Placard.

She'd thought about this. For thirteen years. She'd thought about what she would say to him, if she ever had the chance, and it had been different every time — screams, curses, a knife, silence. She'd thought she would feel something enormous.

She found she felt tired. Very tired. And the Bell was coming up through the floor once a second, and her Gran was dead in her chair in the Tower, and somewhere above her in the dark, once, a man was looking at her light.

"You'll walk out last," she said.

Crane looked at her.

"When they go down," said Ione. "All of them. You'll stand at the top of the wells and see every one of them onto the ladders. Every single one. The children and the old ones and the Wardens and the ones who called my mother a heretic and the ones who spat at me in the lanes. Every one. And when they've all gone down, you'll go down last."

It wasn't forgiveness. She knew that. She didn't know what it was. Something drier. Something that would do.

Halloran Crane bowed his head.

"Yes," he said. "I will."

* * *

They went down the Drop.

It took all night — or what would have been night; there were no nights now, and no days, only the dark and the lamps and the Bell. The riggers re-hung the ladders at every one of the fourteen wells, the ladders they'd cut free and hauled up on Kindling Round. They strung ropes down beside them, and lamp-lines, clay lamps hung on wire every ten metres all the way down the shafts, so that looking down a well was like looking down a throat lined with little fires. And Holdfast went down them.

Family by family. Street by street. Wardens at the tops of the ladders, riggers on the ropes, Tollers with their ledgers counting names. Children on their parents' backs. Old people lowered in rope slings, three riggers to each. Pim's rats in a wire cage lashed to his shoulders, all of them, the whole Stewardship, squeaking. Bundles and bags and bedrolls. Lamps. Everyone had a lamp. The Tanks, the market, the Commons, the Crèche, all left behind, empty, lit, in the dark.

People sang, going down. Not the Tending. She listened for it, and didn't hear it once. They sang the riggers' song, the hauling song, the one with no words but *haul* — a deep slow rhythm, one voice calling, all the voices answering, going down the lines of ladders into the dark. *Haul. Haul. Haul.* And under it, through the rungs, through the keelstone, once a second, the Bell.

Ione went down Fourteen.

She went first. Before anyone. Osk tried to stop her, and then didn't. It was the well she'd pulled him out of. It was the ladder he'd come up. It was the well her mother had gone down, thirteen years ago, and not looked back. She went down it in her rigger's clothes, with his suit strapped to a rope and lowered beside her — Osk had insisted; the Relief's suit didn't get left — and the dented steel box with Ruth's Log in it lashed to her back, and her lamp hanging from her harness.

Three hundred metres through the Anvil. Rung after rung, in the lamp-light. It was only a ladder. It was so easy in the ordinary weight that she kept thinking she'd missed something.

Then the bottom of the Anvil. And below it, the place where the Shear had been.

She stopped on the ladder, there, and hung on it, and looked.

Nothing. A shaft. Round, black, ribbed with old pipe-brackets. A ladder going down it. The air was still. There was no curtain, no trembling, no wrongness. Nothing jumped or smeared. She could see straight down the shaft forty metres, fifty, sixty, to the bottom, where there was a glow — a light, a lamp, not hers — and a round window, lit.

She went down through the place where the Shear had been, and felt nothing at all.

At the bottom of the conduit, at the foot of the ladder, there was a shape covered in foil.

It lay crumpled against the wall. Someone had covered it — carefully, tenderly, from the head down, the silver foil tucked round it at the edges — with a blanket from a first-aid kit. The foil crackled a little in the air from the shaft. It shone in her lamp's light.

Ione stood at the foot of the ladder and looked at it.

She knew. She'd known since the Tower, the first night, when he'd said *there was someone at the bottom of the conduit, in mended clothes, with a lamp carved from a hard hat*, and Gran had made that sound like a bird. She'd known. She knelt down beside it on the floor of the conduit and put her lamp down.

She lifted the edge of the foil. Just a little. Just at the neck.

There was a cord there. Twisted fibre, algae-thread, the kind Holdfast made. And on it, lying against the patched cloth, a little yellow lamp. Carved from the plastic of an old hard hat. Worn smooth and soft as soap. For a crib.

She didn't lift the foil any further. She didn't need to.

"Hello, Mam," said Ione Tally.

She put her hand flat on the foil, over where her mother's heart would have been. She knelt there for a long time. The Bell came up through the floor, once a second.

"I found the edge of the world," she said. "It wasn't what I thought. I went up to find you. You weren't there. I was, though." She was quiet. "You were right. About the line. About all of it. Everybody knows now. It's on a banner in the Commons. Crane said it. In front of everyone." She was quiet again. "Gran's gone. Just now. She was listening to the Bell. It goes once a second now, Mam. Like a heart. You'd have liked that. You'd have wanted to count it."

She tucked the foil back round the little yellow lamp.

"Somebody covered you up," she said. "A lady. From Outside. She couldn't stand you lying there. She didn't know who you were. She did it anyway."

And behind her, from the lit window in the wall of the conduit, from the booth beyond it, there was a sound. A hatch opening. Boots on a ladder, coming down, slow and stiff, as if they'd been sitting for a long time.

Ione stood up and turned round.

A woman was coming down the hatch-ladder from the booth into the conduit. A broad, weathered woman in her fifties, in a heavy grey suit with its helmet off and nothing on its back where a keel should have been, with cropped iron-grey hair and a face like a well-used tool. She had a torch in one hand. She reached the bottom and turned and put the torch on Ione, and stood there looking at her. At her face. At the white streak in her hair. At the rope on her shoulder, and the box on her back, and the empty grey suit hanging on its rope from the ladder above her.

Her face did something complicated.

"You must be Ione," said Teo Lisk. Her voice was hoarse. "He never shut up about you."
