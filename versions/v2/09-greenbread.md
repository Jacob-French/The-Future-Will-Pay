---
title: Greenbread
---

> I'll hold your rope. You hold mine. And if the one of us falls, the other one hauls.
> — Holdfast riggers' wedding vow

He learned to stand the way the babies did: badly, and in public, and with a great many people watching.

Osk taught him. It was not, Cas gathered, a thing Osk had been told to do. It was simply a thing Osk had decided needed doing, on the first morning, when he opened the door of the little stone room and found the Relief of Holdfast on his hands and knees on the floor, trying to get up, and failing, and trying again.

"No," said Osk. "Not like that. That's how you break a knee."

He came in and knelt down beside him. He was so big that kneeling, he was nearly Cas's height standing.

"Babies," he said, "in the Crèche. First thing we teach them. You don't push up. The weight wins if you push. You *roll*." He demonstrated, a huge slow shifting of hips and shoulders. "Get your bones under you. Stack them. Like scaffold. Then the weight goes down through the bones into the floor and the floor holds it, not you. You're not holding it. You're just *stacked*." He looked at Cas. "Try."

Cas tried. It took him four attempts. On the fifth, something shifted in his hips and he found his feet under him and came up — not pushing, somehow, just unfolding upward like a ladder being raised — and he was standing, and it didn't hurt, or not as much, and he was so surprised that he laughed.

Osk nodded, grave as a judge.

"Good," he said. "Now do it a hundred times."

* * *

On the second round, a little after the start of second shift, his suit began to groan.

He was in his room in the Steward's House, lying on the rope bed. The suit stood in the corner where it always stood, humming its keel-note to itself, and without any warning it made a sound. A low sound. A long, slow, deep sound like a foghorn heard across miles of water, or like a whale — *Farr said whales*, Teo had said, *so I get birds* — it came out of the suit's helmet speaker and filled the little room, vast and slow and falling, and it went on, and on, and didn't stop.

He sat up. He knew what it was. He'd known what it was the instant it started.

It went on for the rest of that round and into the next.

He didn't have to listen. The suit was catching every second of it, and at the end it would give the whole thing back to him at the proper speed. He listened anyway. He sat on the floor of the stone room with his back against the bed and his knees up and stayed with it, and slept for some of it, there on the floor, and woke to it still going, and he didn't go to the Tollers that night, and when Osk opened the door once in the middle of it to see what in the name of the Lamp the noise was, Cas just held up a hand, and Osk looked at the suit, and at him, and went out again and shut the door without a sound.

It was a voice. He could hear, if he listened, that it was a voice — the rise and fall of it, the rhythm of speech slowed so far down that it was just a shape — and he sat there and let it pour over him, a single human sentence stretched over most of an hour, and tried to guess the words, and couldn't.

At the end of it the suit went *ding*.

And then, suddenly, perfectly clearly, as though she were in the room:

"Right," said Teo Lisk. "Right. Your first chirp's come through. You want to know what it sounds like this end? It sounds like a budgie fell into my radio. About three minutes after you went up that ladder. Then the suit sat there chewing on it for a minute and a half and went *ding*, and then it was you. Talking about rats."

He put his hand over his face.

"You've been gone five minutes," said Teo, "and apparently you've found a city, and they've made you a prophet, and they've got the Section 9 placard in a church. I leave you alone for *five minutes*, Hale." A pause. He heard her breathe. "Thirty-one thousand. Christ. Thirty-one thousand people. Up that ladder. I keep looking at the ladder. I can see it from the chair, through the window. It just looks like a ladder."

A longer pause.

"You lied to them. Okay. You told me that like it's the end of the world. It isn't. You're not the first man who ever stood up in front of a crowd and told them it'd be all right because it was easier than breaking their hearts. I've done it. I've done it on the top of a flood barrier with the water coming up, and I'd do it again, and so would you." Another pause. "But you'll tell them. Not today. Not till you've got something to give them instead. But you'll tell them the truth before you go up that spindle, Hale, or I'll come up that ladder in this chair and tell them myself."

He laughed. He couldn't help it. It came out wet.

"Also," said Teo, "I'm fine. You'll want to know that. The chair's fine. The lee's fine. It's not moving. I found a biscuit in the first-aid kit. It's a bit old. Nobody's called. The Collar'll be wondering where we are. Let them wonder." A small snort. "Prophet inside an hour. That's a Civil Defence record, that."

A pause.

"I put the blanket from the first-aid kit over her," said Teo, more quietly. "The lady at the bottom of the ladder. With the little lamp. I couldn't stand her lying there. I went down through the hatch and put it over her. I hope that's all right. Whoever she was."

He couldn't breathe for a moment.

"Right," said Teo. "That's three minutes of me you've had. That'll have taken you — I don't know. Most of a day? Don't answer that, I can't work it out, Farr was the one for sums." And her voice caught, just slightly, on the name, and went on. "Send me more. Send me all of it. I've got nothing to do in this chair. Tell your congregation there's a woman down here says hello." A breath. "And look after yourself, you daft sod."

*Ding*.

He sat on the floor for a long time.

Then he got up — rolled, stacked his bones, stood, the way Osk had taught him — and took out the recorder, and pressed the button, and told Teo about Osk, and learning to stand like a baby, and a tower full of pendulums, and an old woman who'd corrected him to the minute, and a Log full of letters to a little girl called Maddy who was seven. And he told her that the lady at the bottom of the ladder was called Seren, and that she'd been the cleverest Toller in two hundred years, and that she'd painted the truth on the wall of the Commons, and that her mother had carved the little lamp, and that it had been for her crib. And he told her *thank you for the blanket*, and found that he couldn't say anything else for a while, and just sat there with the button pressed, not saying anything, letting it record the silence.

After that he sent her something every round. Whatever the round had held, into the recorder, last thing at night: a round's worth of Holdfast at a time. He didn't wait for answers. He'd done the sum. To her, down there in her chair, they'd be arriving about every four minutes, like a tide coming in.

* * *

He stood up a hundred times a round, for sixteen rounds, the way Osk had told him to, and in between he was the Relief.

That was the other thing he learned. How to be it.

It was a job. Crane made it clear, gently, from the first morning, that it was a job and that it had duties, and that the duties were public. The Relief was carried through the lanes on the litter, on high rounds, so that people could see him. The Relief sat in a chair on the steps of the Steward's House for an hour each first shift, and people came up the steps one by one and knelt and touched his hand, or his coat, or just looked at him, and went away weeping. The Relief blessed things. Nobody had told him he was going to bless things. A woman came up the steps with a new baby in a sling and held it out to him and said *bless her, Relief*, and he looked at Crane standing beside the chair and Crane looked back with no expression at all, and so he put his hand on the baby's head — so small, so warm, so heavy for its size — and said *bless you*, and the woman burst into tears, and after that there was no stopping it. He blessed babies. He blessed a new rope for a rigger crew. He blessed the Tanks, standing on a gallery over the great algae vats where the dead of Holdfast went to feed the living, looking down at the slow green water, saying *bless*, *bless*, and thinking of Ben.

He did not say it would be all right again. He never said it again. But he didn't have to. He had said it once, and they had heard it, and it was in every face that looked up at him from the foot of the Steward's steps.

Crane watched him do all of it with that tired, courteous half-smile, and at the end of each round thanked him, and never once asked him how he felt.

* * *

At night he went to the Tollers.

Osk slept outside his door every night on the stool, and every night, when Cas went past him in the shuttered dusk, Osk slept a little more deeply, and never once woke. On the fourth night, Cas found that someone had oiled the hinge of his door so that it didn't squeak.

Pim brought the cart the first few nights. After that, Cas walked. It took him most of a shift to get across the city the first time on his own feet, stopping every twenty steps with his hand on a wall. By the tenth night it took him an hour. By the fourteenth, he could do it without stopping, if he didn't mind that his heart was going like a hammer when he got there, and his knees shook, and he had to sit on the step of the tower for a while before he could climb it.

They didn't only work at the tower. More often, as the rounds went on, he found himself in Ione's loft.

She lived at the top of a scaffold tower in the Drop District, not far from the well he'd come up — a rickety, swaying, rope-lashed stack of platforms twenty storeys high, where the riggers lived who worked the spindles, and who were, Pim told him with great satisfaction, the roughest people in Holdfast. Her loft was the very top platform, walled with canvas and scrap and open on one side to the sky, so that from the hammock that hung across it you could lie and look straight up past the black mass of Spindle Four into the Lamp. It was full of rope. Rope hung from every beam in coils, in hanks, spliced and whipped and knotted, a hundred kinds. There were harnesses on hooks, and chalk in jars, and plumb bobs of every size hanging on strings, and a workbench, and a little stove, and a shelf of books — station manuals, mostly, their covers long gone, and a few Holdfast things: hand-copied, hand-bound, the pages made of pale algae-paper.

They worked there. That was what they told themselves they were doing, anyway. There was a great deal to do. The suit's harness had to be re-rigged for climbing; its hardpoints weren't built for rope, and she spent three nights splicing a web of rigger's strapping round it that would let her belay it — him — from above. There were supplies to make: greenbread baked hard as brick, water in skins of rendered algae-leather, cakes of cricket fat. There were the Lees.

The Lees were the heart of it. She explained them to him on the fifth night, lying on her back on the loft floor with a plumb bob hanging on a string from her fingers, watching it.

"Look," she said. "Down here, the Lamp pushes down and the Anvil pulls down and it all goes the same way. Straight. The bob hangs straight." It did. "But go up a spindle. The further up you go, the harder the Lamp pushes. You weigh more and more. By two kilometres up you weigh half as much again as you do here. By three, twice. By the Rim—" she shrugged "—nobody knows. Nobody's been to the Rim and come back except me, and I wasn't counting."

"So how did you get there?"

"The Lees." She moved the bob. "The spindles are full of keelstone. Struts, braces, cross-members, great thick beams of it. And keelstone pulls, you know that. Like the Anvil pulls. So near a big strut, the strut's pull goes against the Lamp's push. They cancel. Not all the way. But some. So there are places up there — pockets — where you weigh less. Sometimes much less. You can rest in them. You can climb from one to the next." She let the bob swing. "That's a Lee. The riggers have always known them. The lower ones. I know the high ones."

"And they move."

"The Lamp's getting weaker. You know that. The Lightening." She held the string still. "So the balance moves. Every year, the Lees move up. Or sideways. Or close up and open somewhere else. I haven't been past the lower ones in five years." She looked at him. "I'll have to go and look."

She went on the eighth round.

She went in the night, alone, with two coils of rope and a bag of chalk and a skin of water, and he stood at the foot of Spindle Four in the shuttered dusk and watched her climb until she was a speck and then nothing. She'd said two rounds. She was gone three. He spent those three rounds being the Relief, blessing babies on the steps of the Steward's House and not hearing a word anyone said to him, and at night lying on the rope bed in the stone room staring at the ceiling, until on the third night Pim's pebble hit his shutter and he went down to the lane and found her sitting on the Tollers' step with her hands bandaged in strips of cloth and a rope burn across one cheek, eating greenbread.

"There's a way," she said. "It's not a good way. But there's a way."

He sat down beside her on the step. His knees shook. He didn't know if it was the climb down the lane.

"You said two rounds," he said.

She looked at him sideways. And for once there was something in her face that wasn't patient at all. It was surprised.

"You were worried," she said.

"Yes."

She went on looking at him for a while. Then she looked away, down the lane, and ate her greenbread.

"Nobody's been worried about me," she said, "in about eleven years."

* * *

He ate rat on the eleventh round.

It was at the market in the Commons Lanes, where the stalls were built into the shells of old cargo containers with their doors swung open, and everything that Holdfast grew or made or salvaged was sold for tokens stamped out of old sheet metal. Ione had taken him there — in the middle of a round, openly, with a hood up over his face because by now there was not a soul in Holdfast who wouldn't have known him. Greenbread, stacked in dark bricks. Cricket flour in sacks, and the crickets themselves in wire boxes, chirping. Mushrooms of a dozen pale kinds, grown on bales of old insulation in the dark under the Anvil's edge. Algae in every form: cakes, noodles, a thick green paste in jars, a fermented green wine that Ione said would take the skin off your throat. Salt. Oil. Thread twisted from algae fibre. Needles filed from wire.

And at one stall, turning on a spit over a little charcoal fire, rats.

"Go on," said Ione.

He looked at her.

"You've been eating it for ten rounds," she said. "It's in the soup. The Tollers' soup. You had two bowls."

"I—" He stopped. "Did I?"

"Two bowls. You said it was very good." Her mouth twitched. "Go on."

It was very good. It tasted, as everyone who has ever eaten rat has said, like chicken — like very rich, slightly sweet, dark-meat chicken, with a smoky crackle to the skin — and he ate a whole one standing in the lane with the juice running down his wrist, and Ione watched him do it with an expression he'd never seen on her face before: a kind of wicked delight, quickly hidden.

Pim, who had appeared out of nowhere as he always did, was scandalised.

"Not *mine*," he said. "Not Steward Harrow. Not Steward Crane. Not *any* of the Stewards. Only the *market* rats."

"Pim names his after the Stewardship," Ione explained gravely. "All of them. He has a First Steward. He has a Warden-Captain. He has three Toller-Seconds."

"I have a Relief," said Pim. "Now." He produced it from inside his shirt: a small, fierce, grey rat with a torn ear, which bit him. "He's new. He's not very nice yet."

"Relief," said Cas.

"He came up out of a drain," said Pim, "so."

Ione laughed. It was the first time Cas had heard her laugh — really laugh, out loud — and it surprised him so much that he just stood there in the lane with the rat bones in his hand and listened to it. It was a short, low, rough laugh, as if it didn't get used much and had got stiff, and it stopped almost as soon as it started, and she looked away quickly, as though she'd been caught at something.

* * *

He saw a wedding on the thirteenth round.

Riggers. Two of them, a man and a woman, both short and broad and scarred about the forearms like Ione, married on the rooftop of the riggers' tower in the Drop District under the open sky. The whole tower came up — forty or fifty people, crammed onto the platforms and the ladders and the ropes — and a Steward in a black coat stood between the two of them and said words, and then the riggers said their own. They tied their wrists together with a length of rope, the woman's left to the man's right, in a knot Cas didn't know, and they said it together:

"I'll hold your rope. You hold mine. And if the one of us falls, the other one hauls."

And everyone on the platforms and the ladders shouted *haul!* and stamped until the whole tower swayed.

Afterwards there was green wine, and it did take the skin off his throat, and there was music: a drum made of an old coolant drum, and a sort of fiddle strung with wire, and someone playing a pipe cut from conduit. And they danced — the Holdfast dance, which was slow and heavy and stamping, all bent knees and planted feet, a dance made for people who weighed half as much again as they should, and which shook the tower to its lashings. Cas sat on a coil of rope with his back against a beam and watched it. Ione sat beside him.

"You don't dance?" he said.

"I dance." She drank. "Not at weddings. It makes people nervous. The Unwatched at your wedding. It's like a crow at a christening."

"What's a christening?"

She looked at him. "It's in one of the old books. I don't know. I assumed you'd know."

"I don't know either."

She looked at him a moment longer, and then something in her face gave, and she laughed again — shorter this time, into her cup.

"The knot," he said. "On their wrists. What is it?"

She put her cup down. She took his left hand — without asking, the way she'd checked his seals at the well, practically — and took a length of thin cord from her pocket and, very quickly, tied it round his wrist. Her fingers moved too fast for him to follow. Over, under, round, through. When she took her hands away there was a knot on his wrist: flat and neat and interlocking, like two hands clasped.

"Riggers' hitch," she said. "Holds under load. Won't slip. Won't jam. Comes off in a second if you know where to pull and never comes off if you don't."

He looked at it.

"That's the only one you'll need," said Ione, and took it off again with one tug, and put the cord back in her pocket.

* * *

He saw a funeral on the fourteenth. An old man, a Toller, one of Perpetua's cousins. They carried him through the lanes on a board, wrapped in a white sheet with the lamp painted on it, and the Tollers walked behind, and at every Bell the procession stopped and everyone stood still and silent until it passed. At the Tanks they laid him on a sort of stone slope running down into the green water, and a Steward said: "He's clocked off. His shift is done. We thank him for his watch." And they tipped the board, and he slid down into the vat, and the green closed over him, and everyone said *clocked off, clocked off*, softly, like a breath going out.

"Clocked off," Cas said afterwards to Ione, walking back. "That's — the station used to say that. When you went off shift."

"We know," said Ione.

He saw children playing a game in a lane. They stood in a line with their backs to a wall, and one of them stood in front of them, facing them, and shut her eyes and fell straight backwards, stiff as a plank. The others caught her. Every time. They shrieked with laughter. Then the next one.

"What's it called?" he asked Pim.

"Drop," said Pim, as if it were obvious. "It's called Drop."

* * *

On the fifteenth round, when he'd sent Teo the round's news, he switched to the second channel and said:

"Nell. I'm going to tell you about a day. Just an ordinary day. Nobody's ever — I don't think I've ever told anyone about an ordinary day. I don't think I had any, for a long time. I think they were all the same day, and I didn't want to tell anyone about it.

"This morning I stood up by myself. There's a man here called Osk who's teaching me. He's the biggest man I've ever seen and he talks like gravel. He says I'm nearly as good as a baby now.

"Then I sat on some steps and people came and touched my hand. They think I'm — it doesn't matter what they think I am. I'm not it. I let them think it. I'm not proud of that.

"Then I ate a rat. It was delicious. Don't tell your mother.

"Then I went to a place where a very old lady counts time. She's funny, Nell. She'd make you laugh. She says rude things about everyone and then gives them soup. She had a daughter who was very brave. She lost her.

"And then I went to see my friend. She lives at the top of a tower full of rope, where you can lie in a hammock and look straight up at the Lamp — the star, the one I told you about. She's never seen a real one. A real star. I tried to tell her what they're like. I told her about the ones on your ceiling. Betelgeuse. Deneb. I told her they're far away and they don't move and there are thousands of them. She asked me what colour they are. I said white, mostly. Some are blue. Some are a bit red. She asked me which one was my favourite and I didn't know. I've never looked. I've never once looked, Nell. You'd have known in a second."

He was quiet for a while.

"I think," he said, "that if you ever meet her, you'll like her. I don't know why I think that. You'll never meet her. But I think you would."

* * *

On the sixteenth round, the Lace touched Spindle Six.

He didn't see it happen. He was on the steps of the Steward's House, blessing a rope. But he heard it — everyone in Holdfast heard it — a sound from the sky like a great sheet of glass being drawn across stone, high and thin and long, and then a flash, very brief, very bright, from the top of Spindle Six on the far side of the city, and then silence. People stopped in the lanes. Everyone looked up. On the steps, the rigger who'd brought the rope to be blessed dropped it.

Six was the spindle nearest the Lace's lowest tendril. There had been a rigger crew working it that round — they went up every round, to tend the old lamp-lines and the water-catchers the riggers had strung on the spindle's upper lattice, a kilometre and a half up. Six of them. Ione knew them all. So did the rigger on the steps.

At the end of the round the Stewardship put up a notice in the Commons, beneath the Public Tally, written in beautiful careful letters on a board:

:: MARROW TELL AND HIS CREW
:: HAVE BEEN TAKEN INTO THE LAMP'S CROWN
:: A BLESSING ON THE WATCH
:: THE QUICKENING IS GLORY

Ione went up after them the next round, with two others from the riggers' tower. She came back at the end of it and said nothing, and went up to her loft, and when Cas climbed the twenty storeys that night — it took him an hour; he did it — she was sitting in her hammock with her knees up, looking at the Lamp.

"What did you find?" he said.

"Rope," she said. "Six harnesses. Their tools. The water-catchers they were tending, all rusted through to lace. Marrow's knife. I gave that to his daughter." She didn't look at him. "And bones. The kind of bones you'd find in a Founder's grave, if the Founders had graves. Old and white and dry. And a bit of Tamsin's hair, still in her clip. She'd have been thirty next round." She was quiet. "The Lamp's crown. A blessing on the Watch."

He sat down on the loft floor.

"Crane wants another address," he said. "On Kindling Round. Three rounds from now. The whole city in the Commons. He says they need to see the Relief."

"Of course he does."

"He wants me to tell them the Quickening's glory. That Marrow Tell's crew are in the Lamp's crown." He looked at his hands. "He hasn't said it like that. But that's what he wants."

Ione said nothing. The hammock creaked, very gently, as she moved.

"I'm going to tell them," said Cas.

She turned her head then and looked at him.

"All of it," he said. "The Outside. The test. Aldous Crane. The debt. How long. The lock and the key and you. All of it. In the Commons. On the Bell."

She looked at him with those unhurried grey-green eyes. And for the first time since the top of the well he couldn't tell — he really couldn't — whether she had known he was going to say it.

"They'll put you in the Quiet," she said.

"Probably."

"Do you know what the Quiet is?"

"No."

"It's a coolant tank. Under the Steward's House. They seal the lid. No light. No sound but the Bell." She said it very evenly. "My mother was in it for eleven rounds before they gave her to the Drop."

"I'm still going to tell them."

Ione Tally looked at him a moment longer. Then she lay back in her hammock and looked up at the Lamp, and something happened at the corner of her mouth — so small he almost didn't see it.

"Good," she said. "I know how to get people out of the Quiet."
