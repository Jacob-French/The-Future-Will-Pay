---
title: The Lace
---

> Zero isn't nothing. Zero's balanced.
> — Ione Tally, on the Rim

She stood on his boots.

That was how they did it, in the end. They tried it the way they'd done it in the Breach — her legs round his waist, her arms round his neck-ring — and found he couldn't see past her to walk, and she couldn't see behind him to guide, and the bubble, a metre across, barely held the two of them in that shape anyway. So she turned round, and stood with her back against his chestplate, and stepped up onto the toes of his armoured boots, and he put his arms round her, one across her chest and one across her waist, and held her there, pressed back against him, her head under his chin. The exo took the extra weight with a grunt.

"My mother used to dance me like this," she said. "At Kindling. When I was small. On her feet."

"I never did that with Nell."

"No?" She tipped her head back against his visor to look up at him. "You should."

The keel hummed. He could feel the bubble round them, now that he knew to feel for it — a sort of tightness, a stillness, a metre-wide egg of calm. With her inside it, pressed against him, it was full. There was nothing to spare. If she leaned out, even a little, even her hand, it would be outside.

"Don't lean," he said.

"I know."

"Don't put your hand out. Don't touch anything. If you need to point, tell me."

"I know, Cas." Her voice was very steady. She was looking ahead, along the Rim, at the forest of light hanging down across it. "Left foot."

He moved his left foot. She moved with it.

They walked into the Lace.

* * *

The first thing was the light.

Outside it — on the Rim by the gallery, on the spindle, in the city — the Lace had been a thing in the sky. Beautiful, terrible, far. In it, it was the air. The threads hung down all round them, fine and bright, some as thick as a wrist and some so fine they were only a glitter, branching and branching into themselves, reaching down from the sky to the steel of the Rim and spreading across it in fans and roots and deltas of white-violet light. They moved. Slowly, the way weed moves in a current. Close to, he could see them grow — see a branch put out a smaller branch, and that one a smaller, and that one a smaller, until the eye lost them and they became a kind of mist, a glow, a haze of edges on edges round every edge.

And between them, on the Rim's steel, there were arrows.

White arrows. Chalk. Drawn quickly on the black steel plate, one every few metres, pointing ahead and a little left, between two great hanging threads.

Ione went stiff against him.

"Those are mine," she said.

He looked at them. They were rigger's arrows — the same quick, slanting, three-stroke arrows she'd scratched on the struts of Spindle Four, the same hand. He'd have known them anywhere now.

"You've been here," he said. "Before. When you tried—"

"No." Her voice had gone very flat. "I never got this far. Not either time. The Lace was lower on the south side, I never got onto the Rim at all." She was staring at the nearest arrow. "I've never been here. I've never drawn these."

"Then—"

"They're fresh," said Ione. "Look. The chalk's still loose. You could blow it off."

They stood there on the Rim with the Lace moving round them.

"Follow them," he said.

"I haven't made them yet."

"I know," said Cas. "Follow them anyway."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "Left foot," and they followed them.

* * *

He would never be able to tell anyone, afterwards, how far they went, or how long it took. The Rim was a hundred metres, perhaps, from the gallery door to the stair that went up to the Crown. It should have taken a few minutes. They walked it step by step, her feet on his boots, following the chalk arrows between the threads, and it went on, and on, and he lost the sense of it; time went strange, the way it had gone strange in the Shear, and he stopped trying to count.

The threads were close. Some of them hung so close that they passed within an arm's length of the bubble, and he could see — *not look*, he told himself, *don't look* — he could see into them.

He looked.

He couldn't help it. It was like the Lamp; it pulled the eye. A thread as thick as a finger hung just beside his visor, and he looked into it, and it wasn't a thread. It was a tunnel. It opened, the way a pinhole opens when you put your eye to it, into a space — a vast space, bright and violet, and in it, very small, very far, a sphere. A dark sphere, with a point of light at its heart and eight black pillars rising from its floor, and a tiny tangled city at the pillars' feet, and a ring round the top of it, and on the ring, a forest of light, and in the forest, two minute figures, pressed together, looking into a thread.

He looked into the thread they were looking into.

The Vault. The pillars. The city. The ring. The forest. Two figures. A thread.

And into that one.

It went down. It went down for ever. Every thread held the Vault, and every Vault held the ring and the forest and the two of them, and every one of those held a thread, and every thread — he was falling, he was falling down it, down the tunnel, the Vault and the Vault and the Vault opening and opening and opening in front of him, smaller and smaller, finer and finer, and none of them ever getting any smaller at all, every one exactly as large as the last, because size meant nothing here, because there was no bottom, because it was *edge all the way down*—

"*Cas.*"

Her voice. Her head, knocking back hard against his visor.

"Don't look into them," said Ione. "I said. Don't look. Look at the arrows."

He looked at the arrows. His whole body was shaking inside the suit.

"How did you know—"

"Because I did it too," she said. "Just now. Before you. I looked into one." Her voice was very steady and very small. "Left foot."

* * *

They heard themselves before they spoke.

It happened near the middle of the Rim, where the threads were thickest. They were standing still, between two great hanging roots of light, while Ione looked for the next arrow — and her voice said, clearly, close beside his ear, "*Right. Right, and then straight.*"

She hadn't spoken. Her mouth hadn't moved. He was looking at the side of her face; he'd have seen.

A second later, she said, "Right. Right, and then straight." And stopped. And went rigid against him.

"Did you hear—"

"Yes."

They stood there. The threads moved slowly round them.

And then another voice — his voice, his own voice, tinny and close as if from inside his own helmet — said, "*Ione, I can't see the*—" and stopped, cut off. He hadn't said it. He hadn't said anything.

"The sound's coming loose," he said. His own voice sounded strange to him, now, as if he couldn't be sure which one was real. "Out of order. It's the gradient, it's so steep here that — that sound's arriving before—"

"*It was the suit*," said Ione's voice.

It came from nowhere. From the air, from the threads. Not her voice now, but her voice from somewhere else — cracked, hoarse, as if she'd been crying, as if she'd been shouting. Close and far at once.

"*Cas, it was the suit, it was never — it was the suit —*"

And gone.

She'd gone white. He could see it in the side of her face, the blood gone out of her cheek, her lips pressed together.

"I didn't say that," she said.

"I know."

"I haven't said that. I don't know what that means." Her hand, her good hand, had come up and closed on his wrist where it crossed her chest. Very tight. "What suit? What does it — what does that *mean*, Cas?"

"I don't know."

"*Cas*—"

"I don't know. I don't know. Ione. We haven't said it yet. We don't know what it means because we haven't said it *yet*." He heard his own voice rising. He made it stop. "Look at the arrows. Please. Look at the arrows."

She looked at the arrows. After a long moment she said, "Straight," and they went on.

* * *

He saw it about halfway.

There was a place where the threads thinned — a gap, a clearing in the forest of light — and the Rim's inner rail was right there beside them, and beyond the rail, the Vault. The drop. Four kilometres of violet air down to the tiny still city. And off to the left, rising up out of that gulf, the black lattice of Spindle Four — the spindle they had climbed — its upper structure hung with the lowest trailing threads of the Lace, so that from here it looked like a tree wrapped in cobwebs of light.

And on it, a long way down, something was moving.

He stopped. Ione stopped with him.

It was a figure. Small, at this distance — half a kilometre down, perhaps more — climbing down the inside of the spindle's lattice. Grey. Hulking. It went down a ladder, and along a walkway, and down again, slowly, carefully, the way you climb when you're tired. And on its back, there was a light. A soft band of light round a rounded housing, the size of a keg.

A keel.

He knew it. He knew it before he could have known it. He'd lived beside it for a month and inside it for the best part of a week. He knew the shape of those shoulder plates, the gouge down the left side of the back where he'd hit a beam in a shaft in the Undercroft. He knew the housing on its back — the band of lights, the white number painted on it by a boy in a lab, *1*, Teo's number, Teo's keel. He knew the harness round the hardpoints, spliced in rigger's strapping, the webbing that Ione had spent three nights making.

His suit.

His suit, coming down Spindle Four. Alone.

Just one. He looked. He looked for a long time. There was nobody else on the spindle. Nobody with it. Nobody on the ladder above it or the walkway below. One grey figure in a keel suit, climbing down, alone, through the cobwebs of light, toward the city.

As he watched, it stopped.

It stood on a walkway, a small grey shape against the black lattice, and it turned. It tipped its head back — the visor, a dark curve, catching the Lamp's light — and looked up. Up at the Rim. At the Lace. At them.

And it raised its hand.

It held it there. A small grey arm, raised, the palm out. For a long moment. Then it lowered it, and turned, and went on down.

He stood with his arms round Ione on the Rim and didn't move.

*It's me*, he thought.

It came into his head whole and finished, the way thoughts had come in the Shear. It's me. That's me, coming down. I come back. I get to the Crown and I do whatever it is and I come back down the spindle, alone, in my suit. And she—

He looked down at the top of her head, under his chin. At the white streak.

*There are only three suits.* One was at the bottom of the Anvil in a booth, in a chair, without its keel. One was in a hall in the Works, full of dust. And one was his. And nobody came down with it.

And she—

"Did you see it?" said Ione.

Her voice was very quiet. She was looking down at the spindle too. At the place where the figure had been. It was gone now, further down, behind the lattice.

"Yes," he said.

"That's what I saw," said Ione. "When I was seventeen. Coming down from the Rim. That's what I saw, from the other spindle. That's exactly what I saw." She was quiet. "It raised its hand then too."

He said nothing.

Neither did she. She stood against him, on his boots, with his arms round her, looking down at Spindle Four. He couldn't see her face. He could feel her breathing — slow, even, very controlled — through the chestplate.

He thought: *she knows what it means.*

He thought: *she's known since she was seventeen. She's known for eleven years. Someone comes down alone.*

He thought: *she's been waiting at the top of the well for eleven years for the man who was going to come back without her.*

She said nothing. He said nothing. He tightened his arms round her, and felt her hand close on his wrist, and after a long time she said, "Left foot," very quietly, and they went on.

And somewhere under his breastbone, where the sketchbook was, a thing that had been small and quiet — the thing he'd found in the Lee watching her sleep, *I want to see what happens next* — turned over, and set its jaw, and became something else.

*No*, it said.

* * *

It started with his hand.

He'd been watching the arrows. Step by step, her feet on his boots, following the chalk. And he looked down at his right hand, where it lay across her waist, and it wasn't there.

It was there. It was. He could feel it — her belt under his gauntlet, the warmth of her through the cloth. But when he looked at it, it was — thin. Faded. The grey plating of the gauntlet was grey, and then it was the colour of fog, and then he could see her belt through it, the buckle, the cloth, as if his hand were a picture of a hand painted on glass. And then it was solid again. And then it wasn't.

"Ione."

"I see it." Her voice was tight. "Mine too."

He looked. Her hand on his wrist — her good hand, the scarred brown rigger's hand — was going in and out. There, and fading, and there. Like a lamp flickering. Like a signal coming and going.

"It's the edge," he said. "We're near the edge. The Tear. It's—" He tried to think. He couldn't think. "The boundary between being and not being is — the vacuum's so stretched here that — things aren't sure. Whether they—"

He looked up from his hand. And the Rim in front of them was flickering too. The steel plate. The rail. There, and fog, and there. The chalk arrows came and went. A whole section of the rail ahead of them simply wasn't there — was a gap, a nothing, violet air — and then was there, solid, black, with rust on it. The threads of the Lace were the only things that didn't flicker. They hung round them, bright and steady and moving slowly, as if they were the only things in the world that were sure.

And then he looked down at the woman in his arms, and didn't know who she was.

It lasted a second. Less. He was holding someone. A short broad woman with dark hair and a white streak in it, standing on his boots, pressed back against his chest, her hand on his wrist. And he didn't know her. He didn't know her name. He didn't know why he was holding her, or where he was, or why, and a terror went through him so absolute, so bottomless, that it was like falling into the thread again, falling down the tunnel of Vaults for ever. *Who is this. Who is this. Who am I holding.* He looked at the white streak. He looked at the scar on her forearm. He *knew* he knew them. He knew there was a word for them, a name, a whole life, and it wasn't there. It was gone. It had flickered out, like the rail.

Then it came back.

*Ione.*

It came back like the rail came back, all at once, solid and rusted and real. Ione. Ione Tally. The top of the well. *What time is it outside.* The loft. The hut. *I'm glad it was you.* It came back, all of it, and he was holding her so hard that she made a sound, and he couldn't loosen his arms, he couldn't make them loosen—

"Cas," she said. "*Cas.* I'm here. I'm here."

"I didn't know you."

"I know."

"For a second. I didn't — I didn't know who you *were*—"

"I know," said Ione. "I know. I didn't know you either. Just now. I looked at your arm and I didn't know whose it was." Her voice was shaking. Her hand had moved from his wrist and was holding his gauntlet — holding it, pressing it against her waist, hard. "It came back. It came back. We're here. Hold on. Hold on to me. Don't let go of me, whatever you see. Don't let go."

"I won't."

"Whatever you see."

"I won't," he said. "I won't."

They stood there, holding on to each other, on a Rim that came and went, in a forest of light that didn't.

Then she said, "Straight," in a voice that was nearly steady, and they went on.

* * *

The worst of it was near the end.

The stair to the Crown was in sight. Twenty metres ahead — a steel stair going up from the Rim, beside the black lattice of Spindle One's top, to a hatch in the underside of the black lens of the Crown. The threads were thinner here. The arrows led straight to it.

And the Lamp was very close.

The Rim curved round it here, at its nearest. The inner rail was right beside them, and beyond it — twenty metres out, thirty, it was impossible to tell — hung the point. The Lesion. The Lamp. The hard white-violet nail driven through the world. He'd never been this close. Nobody had, since the Kindling. It was too bright to look at; he looked anyway; the visor darkened, darkened again, went almost black, and through it, the point burned.

And he saw into it.

He never had the words for it. Afterwards, when he tried — to himself, in the Crown, in the time that was left — the nearest he could come was that it was like looking into the thread, the tunnel of Vaults, only there was no Vault at the bottom. There was no bottom at all. There was the edge, and the edge, and the edge, going in, and in, and under the edge, at the very heart of it, where the vacuum had been stretched so far below nothing that it had split — he saw what nothing was.

It was a ledger.

That was the word that came to him. He didn't choose it. It came up out of him from somewhere very deep, from fifteen years of figures on the backs of envelopes, from the Margin Review, from every sum he'd ever done. A ledger. Two columns. On one side, everything. Every star, every stone, every drop of water, every living thing, every city, every thought; all the positive, all the *something*, all of it, the whole of the universe that had ever been lit. And on the other side, exactly balancing it, the debt. The gravity. The stretched, straining, negative pull of everything on everything, the price of being. And at the bottom of the page, the sum.

Zero.

He'd read about it. As a student. An old idea, older than the Long Heat — a physicist had written a paper asking whether the universe itself might be nothing more than a fluctuation of the vacuum. A loan. Whether, if you added it all up, everything that existed and everything it owed, the total would come to exactly nothing. He'd thought it was a pretty idea. He'd thought it was a clever way to answer the question of where everything came from: *from nowhere, and it all cancels*.

He wasn't thinking it now. He was *seeing* it. He was standing on the Rim with his arms round a woman and looking into the torn place at the heart of the Draw, where the vacuum had been pulled so far below nothing that the bottom had come out of it, and he could see — not with his eyes; with something under his eyes — the whole of everything, balanced, on the edge of nothing. All of it borrowed. All of it owed. The stars on Nell's ceiling. The city. The Commonweal and its six hundred million, and the Draw, and the Lamp, and Holdfast, and the forty-seven. Ben. Teo. Himself. Ione. All of it a loan against nothing, and all of it, in the end, summing to exactly zero.

*None of it's real.*

It came into him the way the cold comes into you in winter water: not all at once, but everywhere.

*None of it's real. None of it matters. It all cancels. Everything I've done and everything I haven't. The pen. The truth in the Commons. The forty-seven. Ben's eleven years. Ruth's fifty. All of it. It all adds up to nothing. It always did. It always will. There's nothing to pay because there was never anything to borrow. There's nothing. There's nothing. There's only the sum, and the sum is—*

He had stopped walking.

He didn't know when he'd stopped. He was standing on the Rim, still, with his arms round her, looking into the Lamp, and he could feel the weight of the zero on him like the weight of the whole Vault, like the weight at the top of the Breach, and his legs were going. His arms were going. He could feel the exo trying to hold him up and not knowing why, and he thought: *let it go. Let it go. What does it matter. It's all zero. Let go—*

"*Cas.*"

Far away.

"*Cas. Casimir. Look at me.*"

He couldn't. He was looking at the Lamp.

Something hit his visor. Hard. The back of her head, slamming back against the glass. Once. Twice. The visor rang like a bell.

"*Look at me.*"

She'd turned round. On his boots, in his arms, inside the bubble, she'd turned round to face him — he felt her do it, felt her body twist against the chestplate — and now her face was in front of his visor, filling it, blotting out the Lamp. Her face, three centimetres away. Her eyes. Grey-green. Not patient. Wild.

"I saw it too," said Ione. "I saw it. Just now. I saw what you saw. I know. I know what it is."

"It's zero," he said. His voice came out of him from very far off. "It's all zero, Ione. All of it. It cancels. None of it—"

"*I know.*"

She had her hands on either side of his helmet. The good one and the splinted one. Holding his head. Making him look at her.

"I watched the light in the gallery for three years," she said. "It was nothing. It was *light*. It wasn't anybody. It was nine minutes going round and round with nobody in it. It cancelled out, every time — Ruth said one-thirty-five and the key fell and she pulled the hammer and it went dark and started again and at the end of every cycle it added up to nothing. Nothing. Nothing had changed. Nothing ever would." Her eyes were bright and wet and fierce. "And I loved them anyway. Do you hear me? I *loved them anyway*. It all cancelled and I loved them. That's all. That's all there is. Zero isn't *nothing*, Cas." She pressed her forehead against his visor. "Zero's *balanced*."

He looked at her.

"Count," said Ione.

"What?"

"You're good at it. You count everything. You counted the hammer all night. You counted the rungs in the conduit. You counted Ben's seconds." She pulled back so that he could see her face. "Count me."

He didn't understand.

"*Count me*," she said. "I'm one. I'm here. I'm one thing, and I'm here, and I'm not zero, I'm *one*. Count me."

He looked at her. At her face in front of his visor, filling it. Her eyes. The white streak. The stitches over her eye. The mole — he'd never noticed it — the tiny mole at the corner of her mouth. Her breath fogging the glass.

"One," he said.

"Again."

"One."

"Again."

"One." His voice was coming back. It was coming back from wherever it had gone, like the rail, like her name, solid and rusted and real. "One. One. You're one. You're here."

"I'm here," said Ione. "And you're one. And that's two. And it doesn't matter what the sum is. It doesn't matter what it all comes to at the bottom of the page. We're not the bottom of the page. We're *here*." She was crying. "Now turn round. Turn round, and look at the stair, and don't look at that thing again, and walk. Please. Please, Cas. I can't carry you. You have to walk."

He turned away from the Lamp.

He looked at the stair. Twenty metres. Steel steps going up to a hatch in the black underside of the Crown. The chalk arrows leading straight to it, fresh and white on the flickering steel.

"Left foot," said Ione, and turned round again in his arms, and stood on his boots.

He walked.

* * *

The hatch was open. Of course it was open; the Crown crew had come down through it the night of the Kindling, running for the lift, and nobody had closed it after. It was a round steel hatch in the black keelstone underside of the Crown, at the top of the stair, and beyond it, darkness.

They climbed the stair. On the last step, before the hatch, Ione stepped off his boots, out of his arms, onto the steel — and the bubble let her go, and she staggered, but the stair was in the lee of the Crown itself, the great keelstone lens overhead pulling against the Lamp, and the weight here was bearable. Just. She caught the rail. She stood there, breathing hard, holding on to it.

He stood beside her. The keel hummed in his spine. It was low now. He could hear exactly how low. It had given them the Lace, and it had cost.

Below them, down the stair, the Rim ran back through the forest of light toward the gallery, and the chalk arrows ran along it.

He looked at them. Then he looked at Ione.

She was looking at them too.

"I'm going to have to draw those," she said. Her voice was very quiet. "Aren't I. On the way back."

"Yes."

"Because I followed them. So I know the way. So I'll draw them. So I can follow them." She was quiet. "They were never anyone's. They never came from anywhere. They're just — true."

"Like the key," said Cas.

"Like the key."

She didn't say anything else. She didn't say *on the way back, alone*. She didn't say *in your suit*. She didn't look at the suit at all.

Neither did he.

He looked instead at the hatch, and the darkness in it, and he thought about a small grey figure on Spindle Four, climbing down alone, raising its hand. He thought: *that's me. That's me, coming back without her.* And the thing under his breastbone that had set its jaw said again, very quietly, very clearly: *No.*

He didn't know yet what the Clause needed. He didn't know what the plate meant, or what the witness was, or what he'd find under the floor. But he knew one thing, standing on the stair at the top of the world with the keel running down in his spine and the Lace moving slowly below him. He knew it the way he'd known the fractal number on the telemetry screen, the way he'd known it at the window in the Collar: *at last*.

Whatever it asked for, he'd be the one who gave it. Whatever stayed up here, he'd be the one who stayed. And then the figure on Spindle Four would be wrong, and it would be her coming down, not him — her, alive — and he'd have beaten it. For once in his life he'd have looked at a projection and said *no* and made it false.

It didn't feel like dying. That was the strange thing. It didn't feel like the window in the Collar at all. It felt like the opposite.

"Come on," he said.

They went up into the Crown.
