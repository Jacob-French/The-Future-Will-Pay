---
title: Stars
---

> They're always there. You just can't see them.
> — Nell Hale, aged ten, on the telephone

The edge of the lee was half a metre from Teo Lisk's boots when it stopped existing.

She'd been watching it come for — she didn't know. A long time. Two minutes, maybe, by the suit clock, which were the longest two minutes of her life, and she'd had some long ones. The shimmer on the floor, a hand's height off the ground, like heat over a road. Crawling. The dust on the far side of it drifting, and the dust on her side lying still, and the line between them coming across the floor of Lattice Station 14-C toward the corner where she sat with her boots flat against the wall. A metre and a half. A metre. She'd stopped moving the chair, because there was nowhere left to move it. She'd folded her hands in her lap. She'd said *come on, Hale* a few more times, not out loud.

Three-quarters of a metre. Half a metre.

And then it wasn't there.

It didn't go away. It didn't retreat. It was there, a shimmer, a line of wrongness crawling toward her boots — and then, between one breath and the next, there was nothing on the floor but floor. Dust, lying flat. All of it. On both sides.

The room settled.

She felt it. She couldn't have said how. It was like the moment when a ship that's been rolling in a swell comes into harbour and the deck goes still under you — not a movement, the end of a movement she hadn't known was there. The air stopped being wrong. The floor stopped being a floor that might tilt. The whole room simply became a room, the way rooms were, and she was a woman sitting in a chair in the corner of it, and she weighed what she weighed.

She sat there without moving.

Then she got up, slowly, and went to the viewport.

The Shear was gone. The trembling curtain across the conduit, forty metres up — gone. The ladder went up the shaft straight as a ruler, and she could see every rung, clean, not jumping, not smearing. It went up and up into the dark. And at the top — where there had always been, since she'd sat down in this chair, a disc of pale hard violet-white light, far up the shaft, too bright to look at for long—

Nothing. Dark.

She stood with her hand flat on the glass. The glass with the names on it in black marker. MATTIAS. JOSS. BEN. SEREN (THE LADY). CAS.

"Oh, Hale," she said. "Oh, you did it. You bloody did it."

And then, because she was alone, and because nobody was ever going to know, she put her forehead against the glass and cried until she couldn't stand up, and sat down on the floor under the viewport with her back against the wall, and cried some more.

* * *

The hardline rang eleven minutes later.

"Major Lisk." Gaunt. But not Gaunt's voice. Or Gaunt's voice with something gone out of it — the softness, the patience, the lent-you-a-book. "Major. Are you — are you there?"

"I'm here."

"Something's happened." A pause. She heard him breathe. "The shimmer over the Pit. It's gone. It was there, and it's — gone, just now, all at once, the birds went straight over it, a whole flock of them — and the clocks at the cordon have stopped. Not stopped running. Stopped running *fast*. They're — they match. For the first time since two in the morning yesterday, they match." Another breath. "And the output's gone to zero. Not the fake zero. Real zero. The Draw's — there's nothing coming out of it. Nothing at all. It's as if it isn't there." He stopped. "Major, what's happened?"

She looked at the dark disc at the top of the shaft.

"He did it," she said.

"Did — reseated Six?"

"No." She found she didn't care any more. She found she'd never cared. "No, Colonel. He never reseated anything. There was nothing to reseat. He went up to the Crown and he shut it. The Lamp. The Lesion. The whole thing. He found a woman with a key and they went up and shut it for good." She wiped her face with the back of her hand. "It's not coming back. There's no more Draw. It's done. The debt's done. You'll want to tell the Minister. You'll want to tell her he defaulted."

The line was silent.

"The grid," said Gaunt at last. "Kovač was at half. Without anything from the Draw at all—"

"Then it'll go dark."

"Six hundred million people."

"Then it'll go dark, Colonel." She leaned her head back against the wall. "It'll go dark and they'll be alive in it. That's the trade. That's the whole trade. Tell them to light a candle."

Another silence. And then, to her enormous surprise, a sound down the line that might have been a laugh. A small, broken, astonished sound.

"Is Dr Hale—" Gaunt began.

"I don't know," said Teo. "I don't know yet. I'm waiting."

* * *

The Civil Defence teams came four hours later.

She heard them long before she saw them: voices echoing down the galleries of the Lattice, boots on steel, the crackle of radios, somebody swearing at a door. They came in through the booth's outer door with their lamps and their ropes and their stretchers — a sergeant she knew from the Kovač fire, Amara Kade, and eight others — and found her sitting in the chair by the viewport with her boots up on the dead monitors, eating the last of the ration paste out of the suit's tube with her finger.

"Major," said Kade. She was staring. "Major, Christ. We've been — they said you'd — the Works is *walkable*, Major, there's no weather, there's nothing, it's just a building now, we came straight in down Radial Nine like it was a Tuesday. We found the stairhead. And the — the algae hall. There's writing on the glass—"

"I know."

"We've got a stretcher. We'll have you out in two hours. There's water, there's—"

"I'm not going."

Kade stopped.

"I'm waiting for someone," said Teo. "Leave me some water. And a lamp. And a blanket; I gave mine away. And then go back up to the Collar and tell them to send everything they've got down here. Everything. Every team, every stretcher, every lamp, every blanket in Averna."

"Major, the Colonel said—"

"The Colonel," said Teo Lisk, "can come down here and carry me out himself." She finished the paste. "Go on. Go. And Kade — leave the hardline to me."

They went, in the end. Kade stayed, and two others, sitting on the floor of the booth by the door with their lamps, not asking. Teo sat in the chair.

She went down through the hatch once, and sat beside the lady in the foil blanket for a while. Not talking. Just sitting. The Bell came up through the floor — the hammer, the old Dead Man's Hammer, still going, once a second, the same as it had been going since two in the morning yesterday, the same as it had gone through the car and the canteen and the Collar. Only here, now, at the bottom of the conduit, it came up through the floor exactly as it did everywhere else. No longer stretched. No longer a groan. A knock. Once a second.

*Ordinary*, she thought.

She dozed, in the chair, at some point. She dreamed of the flood in the Tessarine underpass. She dreamed of toast.

She woke to singing.

* * *

It came down the shaft.

Faint, at first. So faint that she thought she was still dreaming. A deep, slow, rhythmic sound, far up the conduit, echoing down the round black walls. One voice, calling. Then many voices, answering. A word. The same word, over and over.

*Haul.*

She was at the viewport before she knew she'd moved. Kade and the others were on their feet behind her.

Far up the shaft, in the dark — lights. Little lights. Yellow, warm, flickering. Not a torch. Flames. A line of them, coming down the shaft, one below another, strung down the side of the conduit like beads on a thread. Lamps. Somebody was hanging lamps down the shaft, one every few metres, all the way down. And beside the lamps, on the ladder—

Something grey.

Her heart went. She saw it and her whole body went toward the glass, her hands flat on it. A grey shape, hulking, heavy, coming down the shaft. The suit. His suit. Grey plates, the housing on its back. It was coming down. It was coming down the shaft toward her and she was already moving to the hatch, she was already saying his name—

It turned on its rope.

It was on a rope. It hung from a rope, and it was being lowered, slowly, beside the ladder, turning gently as it came, like a coat on a hanger. Its visor was dark. Its arms hung. There was nobody in it.

She stopped with her hand on the hatch.

Beside it, on the ladder, climbing down, was a woman.

Teo watched her come. She watched her all the way down — through the place where the Shear had been, and past it, and on down, rung after rung, fast and neat and steady, a lamp swinging from her harness and a dented steel box on her back. Short. Broad. A rope on her shoulder. At the bottom she stepped off the ladder and stood for a moment in her lamp's light, and then turned and knelt by the lady in the foil blanket.

Teo couldn't hear what she said. She saw her lift the edge of the foil, at the neck. She saw her put her hand flat on the blanket, over the heart. She saw her stay like that for a long time.

She saw the white streak in her hair.

She knew. She'd known since the first chirp. *There's a woman.* After that, in nearly every one. A rigger. A key. A way of looking at you as if she'd read the end of the book. Teo had pictured her so often, sitting in this chair, that she almost didn't need to look.

She opened the hatch and went down the ladder.

The woman stood up and turned round. Teo put the torch on her. She was younger than Teo had thought. Or older. It was hard to tell. She had a cut over one eye with black stitches in it, and a splinted wrist, and a face that had been crying and had stopped, and grey-green eyes that looked back at Teo — at her suit, at her face, at her hands — without any hurry at all. As if she'd seen her before. As if she'd been expecting her.

"You must be Ione," said Teo. "He never shut up about you."

The woman looked at her for a long moment.

"And you're Teo," she said. Her accent was strange — flat, clipped. "You're the one in the chair."

"I'm the one in the chair."

"You covered my mother."

Teo looked down at the foil blanket. At the little yellow lamp she'd tucked under it, all those hours ago, that had been a few minutes ago, that had been — she didn't know. She looked back at the woman.

"I didn't know who she was," she said.

"I know," said Ione Tally. "You did it anyway."

They stood there, in the conduit, in the lamplight.

"He's not coming," said Teo. It wasn't a question.

"No." Ione didn't look away. "He's up there. In the Crown. He's the witness. It needed someone to hold it until it closed, and whoever held it would be — in it. When it closed. For ever. Once." She said it very plainly. "He's in it. It's a single instant. He's in one instant, and it doesn't end."

Teo stood without moving.

She thought about the booth. About a man kneeling in front of her chair with his broken keel whining and a look on his face she'd seen on men on bridges. *It seemed tidy.*

"Did he want to?" she said.

Ione looked at her. And something in her face changed — the patience went out of it, and something younger came in, and her eyes filled.

"Yes," she said. "He said — he said it was the only thing he'd ever wanted that he was sure of. He said he wasn't paying anything. He said he wanted the moment." She drew a breath. "He said you'd ask. He said, *Teo'll ask if I wanted to*. He said to tell you yes. He said to tell you it wasn't guilt."

Teo Lisk put her hand over her mouth.

She stood there, in the conduit, with the lamps hanging down the shaft above them and the singing coming down, *haul, haul, haul*, and the Bell coming up through the floor, and she held her hand over her mouth for a long time. When she took it away, she was crying, and she didn't care.

"Then that's all right," she said. "Then that's all right. The daft sod. Then it's all right."

And the woman from the top of the world stepped forward and put her arms round her, suit and all, and held on. Like a rigger. Like somebody who hadn't been touched for a long time and had recently remembered how.

* * *

Teo took the hardline off its hook with a hand that wasn't quite steady.

"Collar. This is Lisk. Fourteen-C. Get me Colonel Gaunt." A pause. "Colonel. Listen to me very carefully, because I'm only going to say this once and then I'm going to be busy. I want every team you've got. I want every stretcher and every lamp and every length of rope in the Commonweal sent down Radial Nine. I want water. Tankers of it. I want food, anything, whatever you've got. I want doctors. And blankets. Bring blankets. Bring a *lot* of blankets."

A pause on the line.

"For how many?" said Gaunt.

Teo looked up the shaft. At the lamps, strung down it like beads on a thread. At the ladder. At the first of them coming down it now, behind Ione — a huge man in a long grey coat and a battered hard hat, carrying a little boy on his back; and behind him a woman with a baby in a sling; and behind her an old man in a rope harness, being lowered, three short broad figures guiding his ropes from above; and behind them, more, and more, faces in the lamplight, short and pale and broad and heavy-boned, all the way up the shaft, as far as she could see, singing.

"Thirty-one thousand," said Teo Lisk. "Give or take a rat."

The line was silent for a very long time.

* * *

They walked out through the Works in the dark.

It took until dawn. Longer — it took all the next day, and into the night after, for the last of them — but the first of them walked out before dawn, and Ione walked with them.

The Works was a building now. That was the thing that astonished the Civil Defence crews most, the ones who'd been waiting at the Collar for two days listening to the hammer: the Works was just a building. No weather. No gusts, no shears, no walls becoming floors. The filaments were gone from the halls. The corridors were long and dark and dripping and smelled of algae and ozone and old oil, and they went on for kilometres, and you walked down them, and that was all. Civil Defence had strung lamps along Radial Nine all the way from the Lattice to Gate Nine, and roped the stairs, and posted people at every turning, and Holdfast walked down it in a long, long line with its lamps and its bundles and its children and its rats, singing, and the hammer coming up through the floor once a second.

Ione walked beside Teo.

Teo told her things, as they walked. About the car and the Collar and the crisis room. About Ben, on the flatbed, hugging his cradle. About the canteen and the two-percent napkin. About the phone call to a gym on Tier Thirty. Ione listened. She didn't say much. She looked at everything.

At the stairhead where the four corridors met, the wall was hung with cloths: a Civil Defence jacket, a tarpaulin, high-vis vests, a dust sheet. People looked at them as they went by and didn't ask. Teo didn't explain.

At the algae hall, Ione stopped.

The gallery was on Radial Nine; the line of Holdfast walked right past its stair, and she went up it, and Teo went with her, and they stood at the long window looking down into B-7. It was a ruin. The tanks were dry and cracked and grey. The dust lay over everything like snow. On the catwalk, right against the glass, there was a heap of it, and a grey rag, and a scatter of small bones, and a stubby yellow grease pencil lying in the dust.

And on the glass, faded, cracked, chalky, the writing.

:: THANK YOU FOR STAYING
:: IT WAS LONG ENOUGH

Ione read it twice.

"That's him," she said. "Ben."

"That's Ben."

"He found the seam," said Ione. "In the Carousel. His map. Cas said. We'd have been in there for ever without him." She put her hand flat on the glass, beside the words. On the other side of it, a centimetre away, in the dust, the grease pencil. "He got us out."

Teo couldn't answer.

"*It was long enough*," said Ione. And stood there, with her hand on the glass, reading it again.

* * *

Gate Nine stood open.

The great hazard-striped door, five metres high, rolled all the way back. And beyond it, the Collar — the long evacuated corridors, every door open, every screen dark now, the MARGIN IRREGULARITY: REMAIN CALM gone with the power. Lamps along the floors. Civil Defence at every junction, with water and blankets and quiet voices, and doctors in masks at tables by the doors, and somewhere at the back, in charcoal overcoats, the Calm, standing very still and watching and not doing anything, because there was nothing, any more, that they could do.

And beyond the Collar, the outer doors. And beyond those—

She walked out of the Draw at twenty minutes past four in the morning, two days after Continuance Day, into the Pit.

Wind.

It hit her in the face the moment she came through the doors. She stopped dead. It was cold — colder than anything had ever been in Holdfast — and it *moved*. It came at her from nowhere, from the dark, and pushed at her, and went round her, and tugged at her clothes and her hair and the rope on her shoulder, and went on, and came back. It smelled of — she didn't know. Of nothing she had a word for. Of water, and stone, and distance.

"That's wind," said Teo beside her.

"I know," said Ione. She didn't know. He'd told her about it. It wasn't like being told.

Around her, Holdfast was coming out of the doors, and stopping, the way she'd stopped. Short broad people in patchwork clothes, holding their lamps, standing on the concrete apron in front of the Collar with their faces turned into the wind, their hair blowing, their mouths open. A child laughed — a high, startled, delighted laugh — and then another, and she looked, and they were jumping. The children. Jumping up and down on the concrete, because in the ordinary weight their Holdfast legs threw them into the air so high, higher than they'd ever jumped in their lives, and they were shrieking with it. Pim was jumping. Pim, with a cage of rats on his back, was leaping into the air like a flea and coming down and leaping again and screaming with laughter.

And there were people on the rim.

All round the Pit, on the rim road, high above them, she could see lights. Hundreds of lights. Torches, lanterns, the headlamps of vehicles. People. Standing along the edge of the crater in the dark, thousands of them, looking down. At the Draw. At the doors. At the long line of little flames coming out of it.

Because the city behind them was dark.

She looked past them. Past the rim, past the lights. At Averna. At the Outside. At the thing she'd been told about all her life and never once believed was there.

It was a shape. A vast black shape against a slightly lesser black — the Stacks, rising in their terraced ranks, a hundred and forty levels, tier on tier on tier, going up and up and away into the distance on every side like mountains. And they were dark. Every one. Not a light in them. Here and there a tiny flicker — a candle in a window, a torch on a terrace, a fire — and otherwise, nothing. The great lanterns of the Stacks, that Cas had told her about, that had never gone out in ninety-one years, were out. The Canopy was out. The Draw was gone, and Averna was dark, the whole of it, all six hundred million, standing on their terraces and their balconies and their rooftops in the cold, in the wind, in the dark.

Looking up.

She looked up.

* * *

She didn't have words for it. She'd have them later — she'd find some, the way you find words for anything, eventually, by living with it — but she didn't have them then, standing on the concrete apron of the Pit at twenty past four in the morning with the wind in her face.

There were so many.

That was the first thing. He'd told her. *Thousands of them.* She'd thought she understood. She'd thought: a thousand is the market on a busy round; a thousand is a few rows of the Tollers' column. She hadn't understood. The sky was full of them. The whole sky. It went up from the black edge of the Stacks on every side, black, blacker than the dark in the Vault after the Lamp went out, a black that went on and on and didn't stop — and it was full of lights. Small ones. Tiny. Points. Some so bright they seemed to shiver. Some so faint she could only see them when she looked beside them instead of at them. White, mostly. Some blue. Some — yes — a little red, the way he'd said. Scattered across the whole of the black in no pattern she could see and every pattern she could imagine. Crowded, in places, so thick they ran together into a sort of mist, a pale river of light going right across the sky from one edge of the dark to the other.

They didn't move. They didn't sing. They didn't hum. They didn't reach down toward her in long bright threads. They didn't do anything. They were just there.

Her knees went.

She sat down on the concrete. She didn't decide to. She just found she was sitting, with her lamp beside her and her hands flat on the cold ground and her head tipped back, looking up. And around her, she saw, Holdfast was doing the same. All along the apron, people were sitting down. Lying down. Lying on their backs on the concrete with their lamps beside them, looking up, the children who'd been jumping lying flat now with their arms spread out, silent. Osk was sitting with his hard hat in his lap and his mouth open. Pim had lain down on his back beside her with the cage of rats on his chest and was staring up with his eyes so wide they shone.

Nobody spoke. Thirty-one thousand people came out of the Draw into the wind, one after another, all that night and all the next day, and every one of them, when they came out of the doors and looked up, stopped. And a lot of them sat down.

And up on the rim — she saw, when she could look at anything but the sky — the people of Averna were doing it too. Standing along the edge of the Pit with their torches, their heads tipped back. Six hundred million of them, on their terraces and their balconies and their rooftops, in the dark, in the cold, looking up at a sky that no one in the city under sixty had ever seen. The same sky. At the same time. All of them together.

She looked for one.

She sat on the concrete and looked up at the thousands of them, and looked, and looked. A bright one. Not the brightest; there was one very bright one, low down, white and fierce and shivering, and she thought that must be the one he'd called the Dog Star, and it wasn't his. She wanted one that was a little on its own. One that didn't have anything else bright near it. She looked for a long time.

There. Low in the south, over the black edge of the Stacks. A single star, quite bright, a little golden, sitting by itself in a dark patch of sky with nothing near it at all. Alone. Steady. Just there.

*Fomalhaut*, he'd said, in the gallery, while she sat against the algae trough with her head on her knees. *Which is lonely. Which sits by itself.*

She didn't know if that was it. She'd ask Nell. She'd find out. It didn't matter.

"That one," she said, out loud, to him, in case. "That one's yours."

* * *

The Calm came, eventually. Of course they did. They came across the apron in their charcoal overcoats with their tablets, which didn't work, and their lists, which were paper now, and a tall silver-haired woman in a dark suit walking in front of them, very straight. And they began to talk about processing, and registration, and screening, and quarantine — which was fair, Teo said afterwards; these people had been sealed off from every germ in the world for a hundred and sixty-eight years, and a cold would kill half of them — and relocation for comfort.

The silver-haired woman stopped in front of Ione, where she sat on the concrete.

"I'm the Minister of Continuance," she said. "Ondine Rask. I'm told you — came out first. That you were with Dr Hale."

Ione looked up at her from the ground. At her very straight back and her crease-less suit and her face, which was grey with exhaustion and entirely closed, like a door.

She thought of a man with silver hair, fifteen storeys high on the side of a scaffold tower, holding up a key.

"Your deputy," she said, "ordered the test."

The Minister went quite still.

"Aldous Crane," said Ione. "He came up to the Crown with a camera crew and a key round his neck, and he told Ruth Tally to take the interlocks off, because you wanted a round number for Continuance Day. She said no. He said her daughter's name. She said yes." Her voice was very calm. "Then Six went, and she asked him for the key, and he wouldn't give it to her. He held it. And it fell. And nineteen hundred people spent a hundred and sixty-eight years in there because of it." She looked up at Rask with her grey-green eyes. "He's been dead a hundred and thirty years. He wrote it down, before he died. I can show you. And Ruth Tally's log. It's in this box."

"How—" said Rask. Her voice had gone thin. "How can you *possibly*—"

"I watched him do it," said Ione Tally. "A hundred and seventy-five thousand times."

Rask stared at her.

"Who *are* you?" she said.

Ione thought about it. She thought about it seriously, the way she thought about every question, turning it over. She looked at the dark city, and the thousands of lamps coming out of the doors behind her, and the sky.

"I'm the night shift," she said.

Behind Rask, a little apart from the overcoats, a soft, older man in a brown cardigan buttoned wrong had stopped. He had a paper bag in his hand. He was looking at Ione, and at Teo, sitting beside her on the concrete, and at the dented steel box on the ground between them. His face in the lamplight was very tired, and very strange — as if something had broken in it, and something else, underneath, were trying to come out.

"Major Lisk," said Ansel Gaunt quietly. "Is Dr Hale—"

"He's in the Crown," said Teo. "He's staying."

Gaunt looked at her. Then he looked at the box.

"I think," he said at last, slowly, like a man trying out words in a language he hasn't spoken since childhood, "that I should like to hear about it. All of it. Properly." He paused. "On the record."

* * *

The sky began to change a little after six.

It went grey at the edge first, low in the east, behind the dark Stacks. Just a little. A paleness. And then more — a grey going to blue, going up the sky, pushing the black back. And the stars in that part of the sky began, one by one, to go out.

Ione saw it happen. She was still sitting on the concrete, wrapped in a Civil Defence blanket, with Teo beside her in another and Pim asleep across both their laps with his rats. She'd been watching the lonely one in the south. And she looked up and saw the stars in the east going — fading, thinning, gone — and she sat up so fast that Pim woke with a yelp.

"They're going out," she said.

Her voice was high. She heard it and couldn't stop it. The stars over the Stacks were going out, one after another, like the lamps in the lanes when the oil runs down, and the sky was going pale, and she was on her knees on the concrete with the blanket falling off her shoulders, and she couldn't breathe.

"They're going out, Teo — they're *going out* —"

"Hey," said Teo. "Hey. Hey. No. Look at me."

She looked at her.

"It's the sun," said Teo Lisk. "It's just the sun coming up. It's morning. That's all." She put her hand on Ione's arm. "They don't go out. They're still there. You just can't see them in the day. They come back."

Ione stared at her.

"They come back," said Teo again. "Every night. When it gets dark. They come back."

"Every night?"

"Every night."

Ione looked at the sky. At the grey turning to blue, and the stars fading out of it, and the lonely one, low in the south, still there, just barely, going faint, going. Every night. They'd go, and they'd come back. And go. And come back. For ever. The same, and never the same. The good kind.

She sat back down on the concrete. She pulled the blanket back round her shoulders. She watched the lonely star go out, and the sky go blue, and the first light come up over the black shoulders of the Stacks and touch the rim of the Pit, and the faces of the people standing on it, and the long line of lamps still coming out of the doors of the Draw behind her.

"Every night," said Ione Tally, to him. "Did you hear that? They come back."
