---
title: Blister
---

> If one of us ends up in fast time, the others'll sound like whales.
> — Benedek Farr

From the gallery they watched him come out of the lock into the hall.

He went quickly, the way he did everything, the keel's hum rising and falling in their radios as he moved, his helmet light bobbing between the great glowing columns of the tanks. The control box for the bulkhead's manual cycle was on a pillar ten metres inside the hall. Ben reached it. They saw him open its cover. They heard him say, "Bleed valve first, you horrible thing," and heard the squeal of a valve wheel, and then a long, thin hiss.

"Ninety seconds for the pressure to come off," he said. "Then crank. Told you. I know all its secrets."

Teo let out her breath. Cas watched the wall.

He was watching it when the filament moved.

Far off at the other end of the hall, the great tree of light under the wall's skin didn't move so much as *grow*, all at once — a flicker of new branches running out from its tips like frost racing across a pond, the jump he'd watched in the storeroom, only bigger. And one of them didn't stop at the wall's face. A single fine thread came out through the concrete as if the concrete were smoke, and ran across the ceiling of the hall faster than he could follow, fine as spider silk, and went down into the floor about forty metres from where Ben was standing.

Nothing slammed. Nothing needed to. Every door in the hall was already shut. That had been the point of the lock.

The glass in front of Teo's face rippled.

It was the locker-room window, made huge. The whole long pane of the gallery, from end to end, took on that slow, trembling shimmer, like heat over a road, and held it. Along the window frame the paint crazed in a band a finger wide, with a tiny, glassy crackle like ice forming on a pond.

And Ben—

Ben flickered.

That was what it looked like. One moment he was standing at the pillar with his hand on the valve wheel, and the next he was standing somewhere else — a step to the left, turned round — and the next he was at the lock, and the next he was back at the pillar, and none of it was motion. It was like watching a film with most of the frames cut out. He jumped from place to place in stutters. His helmet light flared and moved and flared and moved.

In their ears, the radio made a noise.

It was a squeal. A high, thin, rising chirp, like a bird, like a bat, like a whistle blown in a dream, so brief it was almost not there, and then another, and another.

"Ben!" Teo was at the glass, her gauntlets flat on it. "Ben! *Farr!*"

The suit's radio chimed. A little amber light came on at the bottom of Cas's visor: RECEIVING — BUFFERING — RETIMING. Ben's code. It had caught the chirps and was stretching them back out.

And then, suddenly, Ben's voice, perfectly clear, perfectly normal, in their ears:

"—what happened, what happened, the glass — Cas? Teo? The glass is all — oh no. Oh no, no, no, no. I can see you, I can see you up there, you're not moving, why aren't you — oh no. Oh, no. Okay. Okay. Okay."

It stopped. The light went off.

Down on the floor of the hall, Ben was at the inner door of the lock, both gauntlets flat against it. Not opening it. Just leaning there. Then he was gone. Then he was at the pillar. Then he was standing still — for a whole second, perfectly still — in the middle of the hall, looking up at them.

The radio chirped.

RECEIVING — BUFFERING — RETIMING.

"Okay." His voice again. Different. Steadier, as though he had had time to steady it, as though he had sat down somewhere and breathed for a while. "Okay. I've got the pole out. I'm reading forty-eight thousand. Forty-eight thousand to one. It came out of the wall forty metres from me, and the whole hall's come up to its speed. It's a blister. It's the locker room. The drop's all in the walls and the doors — it's in your glass, I can see it from here, the window's gone all wavy." A breath. "Don't open the door. Listen to me. Don't open the door. You know why. It all goes through the doorway, and then down the corridor and up the stair and through you. The keels won't hold forty-eight thousand. Nothing will. And I can't open it from in here, either, not the lock, not the big one — it'd go through me on the way out." A pause. "The lock's right there. I'm looking at it. It's about ten metres away. That's a stupid thing to be able to see." Another pause. "I'm okay. I'm okay. I'm just going to think."

The light went off.

The radio never spoke again.

* * *

"Forty-eight thousand," said Teo.

She said it very quietly. She was still at the glass.

"A minute for us," said Cas, "is a month for him."

He heard himself say it. He heard how calm it sounded.

"Then we open the—"

"He said no."

"I heard what he said. We've got keels. We've got—" She turned on him. "What do you mean, *no*? That's a *boy*, down there—"

"It's in the glass. Two to forty-eight thousand, across a centimetre. If we open that door, it's across the doorway instead, and then it comes down the corridor like a wave, and whoever it passes through has one half of their body living twenty-four thousand times faster than the other half. The keel averages over a metre. It isn't enough. Nothing's enough." He made himself go on. "He knows the keels better than anyone alive. He said no."

Teo stared at him. Then she looked back down through the glass. Below them, in the emerald light, Ben was moving in stutters from tank to tank.

"There's got to be a way," she said.

"Maybe. If there is, he'll find it before we do. He's got more time."

He didn't say *one in twenty*. He would say it later, to himself, for a long time.

It was Ben who worked out how to talk.

They didn't see him do it. They saw him vanish from the floor, and then — perhaps a minute later — he was there on the other side of the glass, suddenly, right in front of them. The algae tanks stood in rows below the gallery, and along their tops ran a maintenance catwalk that came right up against the gallery window, and he was standing on it, a foot away from them through the glass. He had taken his helmet off.

His face was thinner. That was the first thing. It had been perhaps a minute, for them — and in that minute he had found a razor from somewhere and shaved, and cut his own hair, badly, very short, and gone dark under the eyes and hollow in the cheeks. He looked at them. His eyes moved from Teo to Cas and back again. Then he held up a hand, and in it was a stubby yellow grease pencil, the kind engineers used to mark pipes.

He wrote on the glass. He wrote fast; they couldn't see his hand move. The letters simply appeared, backwards to him, forwards to them.

:: HOW LONG

Cas looked round. There was a supervisor's desk in the gallery, and in its drawer, a marker. He took his right gauntlet off — the gallery was calm, a lee of sorts; the suit let him — and pulled the cap off the marker with his teeth and wrote, carefully, in capitals, backwards so Ben would read them the right way round:

:: 3 MIN

It took him perhaps fifteen seconds.

When he stepped back, Ben had written underneath:

:: 3 MONTHS

And then, a flicker, and more:

:: YOUR LETTERS TAKE A WEEK
:: WROTE HOW LONG. WAITED 8 DAYS TO SEE 3 MIN
:: KEEP IT SHORT

* * *

There was a moment — Cas would think about it a great deal, afterwards — when they could have gone.

The route through the hall was closed. But the long way round was still there. Three hours, the map said. Three hours to go round B-7 and rejoin Radial Nine on the far side, and then another kilometre and a half to the Lattice, and whatever was past the Lattice, and whatever was past that. Every minute they stood on this gallery was a minute of the city's, and the city didn't have many. The debt was coming due. Somewhere out there a pulse was gathering itself at the core, and there was a lock in the Crown and perhaps a key and certainly six hundred million people, and none of it was going to wait.

Teo looked at him. He looked at her.

Neither of them said it. Teo took off her own right gauntlet, and sat down on the floor of the gallery with her back against the desk, and after a moment Cas sat down beside her. The glass was in front of them. On the other side of it, in the green light, a man they had known for a day began to live the rest of his life.

* * *

They didn't see most of it. They saw it the way you'd see a life through a keyhole — in flashes, in fragments, the long stretches between cut out. When Ben stood still for a while, they saw him. When he moved, he was a flicker, a smear, a sense of someone having just been there. He'd be at the glass, and then the catwalk would be empty, and then — a minute later, a month later — he'd be back.

He came back with a beard.

It happened in no time at all. One flicker, he was clean-shaven and hollow-eyed. The next, there was a dark scruff along his jaw. The next, it was a beard: short, rough, reddish-brown, which neither of them had known his hair would grow. He'd taken the suit off entirely. He was wearing the grey liner and, over it, a pair of stained orange overalls from some locker, with the sleeves rolled up. He looked at them through the glass — they could see him breathing — and then wrote:

:: I'M OK
:: AIR GOOD (ALGAE)
:: WATER GOOD (PIPES)
:: FOOD: ALGAE
:: :(

Teo laughed. It came out of her like a sob.

Ben saw it. Or saw something — saw her face change, very slowly, over days, perhaps. He grinned. It was so quick they barely saw it, but it was there, a flash of teeth in the beard, and then he was gone again.

* * *

:: TELL JONAH

Four minutes later, it was still there on the glass. Underneath it, in smaller letters:

:: TELL HIM I SAID YES TO THE DOG

Cas didn't know what it meant. Teo did. She'd been across the hangar the night before, and she'd heard. She turned her face away, into the shoulder of her suit, and stayed that way for a while.

* * *

Twenty minutes in — nearly two years, for Ben — the catwalk against the glass had become a place.

He'd dragged things up there. A pallet, to sleep on, with a foam mat from a first-aid station and an emergency blanket, silver on one side. A crate for a table. A row of algae-paste tins, some full, some empty and scrubbed and used for things — a pencil pot, a cup, a tiny pot with a green shoot in it. He'd rigged a grow-lamp on a cable to hang over it all. He'd rigged a sort of curtain, from a tarpaulin, that he could draw across the glass. He didn't draw it often.

And there was a rat.

They noticed it one flicker when Ben was sitting cross-legged on the pallet, eating something out of a tin with his fingers. It was sitting on his knee. A big, sleek, brown-grey Works rat, with a long pink tail curled round it and its paws folded, looking at them through the glass with its black-bead eyes as though it too were waiting to see what happened. Ben pointed at it and wrote an arrow on the glass, and a word:

:: MINISTER →

Teo laughed again. This time it was nearly a real laugh.

* * *

And then, for a while, there was nothing good.

They'd known it would come. You couldn't be alone in a room for that long and not have it come. Cas had lived alone for fifteen years and he knew. But he had had a city outside his window, and a phone, even a phone that never rang, and the possibility — even if he never took it — of walking down a street and buying a newspaper and speaking to a stranger. Ben had a rat, and algae, and two statues behind a pane of glass who took a week to write four letters.

He came to the glass one flicker with his hair grown out wild, and his beard wild, and his eyes red, and wrote in huge letters, slanting, tearing the grease pencil:

:: I CAN'T
:: I CAN'T DO THIS
:: I CAN'T DO THIS I CAN'T I CAN'T

And then he hit the glass. They didn't see his fist move. They just saw the glass shake, very slightly, and a starburst of cracks appear in the outer pane, in front of Teo's face, and Ben's hand on the glass on the other side with blood smeared from the knuckles, and his forehead pressed against it, and his shoulders shaking.

For one long second Cas thought of the locker-room door, and the doorway, and what a hole in that glass would mean for all three of them. The cracks stayed where they were. They were in the outer skin. The inner pane held.

Teo put her bare hand flat on the glass over his.

She held it there. She held it there for a long time — two minutes, three — and on the other side of the glass, Ben stood with his forehead against it, perfectly still. And they understood, both of them at the same moment, what he was seeing: a hand, very slowly, over days and days, rising to the glass and coming to rest there. A hand that stayed. That didn't go away. That was still there when he woke up, and still there when he went to sleep, for as long as he needed it to be there.

Then he was gone. The glass was empty, except for the smears, and the cracks, and the huge slanting letters.

He didn't come back for a long time. Seven minutes. Eight months.

* * *

When he came back he was different.

He was thinner — much thinner — and his beard had been cut short with something, scissors or a knife, and his hair was tied back with a piece of wire. He was clean. He'd found clothes: a Works jumpsuit, too big, belted with cable. He moved — when he moved slowly enough for them to see — carefully, deliberately, like someone who has made a decision about how to live and is living it. He looked at them through the glass for a long moment. Then he wiped the old words off with his sleeve, and wrote:

:: SORRY

And under it, after a pause:

:: I'M STUDYING IT

And an arrow, pointing down the length of the hall, at the far wall, where the great tree of light was still spreading slowly into the tanks.

* * *

That was when it started to fill the glass.

For the next hour — the next five years — Ben Farr studied the thing that was killing him, and wrote down what he learned where they could see it.

He wrote fast, and they saw it arrive in bursts. A diagram would appear: the filament's tree, sketched in grease pencil in quick sure lines, branches labelled. A column of numbers. An arrow. A question mark that was later crossed out, and replaced with an answer. The glass became a page, and then a notebook, and then a blackboard in a lecture hall. He ran out of room and wiped whole sections off and started again, and Cas — sitting cross-legged on the gallery floor with his black sketchbook open on his knee and a pencil in his bare hand — copied everything, frantically, as fast as it came, because he knew that anything he didn't copy in the minute it was there might be wiped away the next.

It was very good work.

Ben had measured the filament. With the pole, with a tape, with a stopwatch and his own eyes, month after month, year after year, standing as close as he dared. He'd watched how it grew — how every branch split into branches with the same pattern, the same angles, at every scale. He'd counted. He'd worked out the branching ratio. He'd worked out that the pattern was self-similar, and computed, from his own measurements, the number that described how rough the thing was — how much edge it had.

:: D = 2.73

Cas stopped drawing. He sat for a moment, looking at the number. The same number that had been at the bottom of the last telemetry burst from the Crown. Ben had found it with a tape measure. He'd found it alone, in a room, with a rat, from first principles.

"What?" said Teo.

"He's right," said Cas. "That's all. He's right."

There was more. Ben had noticed that the filament didn't grow at random; it grew along lines. He'd mapped where the hall's structure ran — the pillars, the beams, the old coolant lines that had once connected this hall to the Lattice — and found that the filament's branches followed them, followed the lines where the Lattice had once pulled hardest on the station's frame. Stress lines. The Tear was growing outward along the ghosts of the forces that had held it in. And where two branches forked, there was always, between them, a narrow gap — a throat — where the gradient was gentlest. A seam.

He drew the Lattice. From memory, from the hall's own schematics which he'd found in a cabinet, from reasoning. He drew the inner face of it, where Cas had said there would be a shear. He drew where the stress lines would run through it. He drew where the throats would be.

:: IF THERE'S A SHEAR
:: IT'LL HAVE SEAMS
:: HERE HERE HERE
:: GO THROUGH THE THROATS

And arrows. And numbers. And a map.

Cas copied it until his hand cramped. Teo held the sketchbook steady on his knee when his hand shook. At one point, without either of them saying anything, she took the pencil from him and did a whole section herself, the list of numbers, in her small square hand, while he flexed his fingers.

On the other side of the glass Ben was thirty-two, and then thirty-three, and then thirty-four. His hair had started to go grey at the temples. He had lines around his eyes from squinting at the light. Minister had died — they saw the day he came to the glass with the rat lying in his cupped hands, and wrote MINISTER. OLD AGE. 3. GOOD INNINGS — and a year later there was another rat, smaller and darker, and he wrote MINISTER II, and pointed at it, and grinned in the beard.

:: I HAVE HAD 8 BIRTHDAYS
:: NOBODY CAME

Teo wrote back:

:: WE'RE HERE

It took her fifteen seconds to write it. It took him, on the other side, more than a week to watch it arrive. When it was finished, he stood looking at it. Then he wrote:

:: I KNOW

* * *

Near the end — though they didn't know it was near the end — he came to the glass and sat on the pallet with his arms around his knees and looked at them, not writing anything.

He was thirty-seven. He was greying at the temples and in the beard, and there were deep lines at the corners of his mouth, and he had the look of a man who has been very unhappy and then, over a long time, has found his way to something that was not happiness but was near it, and could live there. He had been alone in that hall for eleven years. He was older now than Cas had been when he signed the revised Margin Review.

He wrote:

:: IS IT STILL TODAY FOR YOU

Cas wrote back:

:: SAME DAY

Ben read it — slowly, over days — and they saw him nod. Then he wrote, carefully, smaller than before, so they had to lean close:

:: STRANGE
:: YOU'VE BEEN SITTING THERE MY WHOLE LIFE
:: FEELS LIKE YOU'RE THE ONES WHO ARE STUCK

Cas looked at those words. He found that his eyes were stinging. He found that he could not remember when that had started.

And then Ben wrote:

:: GO

Teo made a small sound.

:: YOU HAVE TO GO
:: I'M FINE
:: HAD A GOOD LIFE
:: MOSTLY ALGAE

"No," said Teo, to the glass.

:: THE WORLD'S ENDING OUT THERE
:: GO AND STOP IT
:: YOU'VE GOT THE MAP

Cas looked down at the sketchbook in his lap. Thirty pages of it, dense with grease-pencil diagrams in his own pencil and Teo's. Eleven years of a man's life.

He looked at Teo. She was crying, openly, silently, her face wet in the visor-light, her jaw set.

"Two more minutes," she said. "Two more minutes and then we go. I'm not leaving him the minute he tells us to. I'm not having him think—" She didn't finish.

"Two more minutes," said Cas.

He picked up the marker. He wrote:

:: 2 MORE MIN

And then, after a moment, because it seemed important and because there was no one else to say it:

:: THANK YOU BEN

* * *

He never got to read the second line.

They saw it happen at the far end of the hall first. The tree of light on the far wall flared — the whole of it, every branch, at once, bright enough that the gallery windows went white and Cas's visor darkened automatically — and then it grew. Not slowly now. Not with the patient frost-crawl they'd been watching for two hours. It *surged*. New branches ran out of it in every direction, racing across the ceiling and down between the tanks, fork after fork after fork, and the fine thread that had come down in the middle of the hall two hours ago thickened as they watched, from a hair to a finger to a rope of light as thick as an arm.

On the other side of the glass, Ben stood up.

He was already writing. The letters came so fast now that they seemed to appear all at once — whole lines at a time — and Cas understood, with a lurch that went all the way down into his stomach, what that meant.

:: IT'S SPEEDING UP

And, under it, instantly:

:: RATIO CLIMBING
:: 100K
:: 400K
:: 3M
:: POLE CAN'T READ IT
:: IT'S OK

And under that, all at once, the whole bottom of the glass:

:: THANK YOU FOR STAYING
:: IT WAS LONG ENOUGH

He put the pencil down.

He put his hand flat on the glass. His palm, his long engineer's fingers, dirty at the knuckles, with the white scar across two of them where he'd hit the glass all those years ago.

Cas put his own bare hand flat against it on the other side.

For a moment — less than a second — they were like that. Two palms, a centimetre of glass between them.

Then Ben aged.

He was thirty-seven, and then there was grey all through his beard, and his face had fallen in around the eyes, and he was fifty. He was sixty. The hand on the glass thinned under Cas's palm, the knuckles swelling, the skin spotting and loosening over the bones. His back curved. His hair went white and thinned and was gone. He was an old man — a very old man, eighty, ninety — standing with his hand on the glass and his eyes, still his eyes, looking out through a face that Cas didn't know at all. He was smiling. Cas was almost sure he was smiling.

Then he wasn't standing. He was sitting, on the pallet. He was lying on it. He was still.

And then he was not a man. The overalls sagged. The shape under the silver blanket collapsed, and yellowed, and was bones, and the bones went grey and dry and cracked, and the blanket went brittle and split. The pallet rotted to splinters under it. The tins rusted to brown lace. The little pot with the green shoot in it burst with a sudden crown of leaves that browned and fell. Out in the hall the tanks bloomed — all of them, at once, a fantastic eruption of green, thick and rank and clotting against the glass — and then darkened, and went black, and drained, and cracked, and stood empty, with the grey slime dried to crusts up their sides. Dust fell from the ceiling in a slow endless snow. The grow-lamps flickered and died one by one. The paint peeled off the pillars and lay on the floor in drifts, and the drifts went to dust, and the dust settled over everything, deep and grey, like a snowfall on a ruin a thousand years old.

It took about thirty seconds.

Then the light at the far end of the hall dimmed back to its ordinary glow. The rope of light in the middle of the hall thinned, and thinned, and was a hair again, and drifted a little on the air, and was gone, into the floor, somewhere else.

Cas was still standing with his hand on the glass.

On the other side of it, very close, there was a heap of grey dust under a brittle grey rag, and a scatter of small bones that had been a rat, and, lying in the dust where it had been put down, a stubby yellow grease pencil.

Above it, on the glass, the writing was still there. The grease had outlasted him. It was faded now, cracked and chalky, but you could still read it.

:: THANK YOU FOR STAYING
:: IT WAS LONG ENOUGH

* * *

Teo was sick in her helmet vent.

She did it without any fuss, turning her head, and the suit's scrubbers whined, and she knelt there afterwards on the gallery floor with her head down. Then she got up.

Cas hadn't moved. He was looking at the pencil.

"Hale." Her voice was hoarse.

He didn't answer.

"*Hale.*"

He looked at her.

"We have to go now," said Teo. "We said two minutes. That was two minutes."

He looked back at the glass. At IT WAS LONG ENOUGH. At the dust. At a centimetre of glass in front of his bare palm, and a smear on its other side where, a minute ago and sixty years ago, another palm had been.

He took his hand away.

He put his gauntlet back on. He closed the sketchbook — thirty pages, dense with numbers and diagrams, a map of a thing no one had ever seen, drawn by a man who had spent his whole life looking at it — and put it inside the chest of his suit, next to his heart, under the neck-ring, where it would be safe.

They took the long way round. Three hours. For the first hour of it, neither of them said anything at all. They walked through the Works in the pearly light with the hammer coming up through the floor, once a second, once a second, and every so often Cas found that his lips were moving, and that he was counting.

He wasn't counting the hammer. He was counting how many seconds it had been since he'd put his hand on the glass.

He knew what he would do if he ever reached a number that was long enough. He just didn't know what the number was.
