---
title: The Shear
---

> Guilt's a lousy reason, Hale. Die for something useful or don't bother.
> — Major Teodora Lisk, Civil Defence

It was Teo who talked first, in the end. They were going down a long ramp between two walls of cable trunking, with the hammer coming up through the soles of their boots, and she said, out of nowhere, "He was a good kid."

"Yes."

"I only knew him a day."

"Yes."

"No. I knew him eleven years." She was quiet for a while. "Does that count? It feels like it counts."

Cas thought about it. He thought about a man with a beard and a rat on his knee, standing at a pane of glass for more than a week to watch two words arrive, and writing *I KNOW*.

"It counts," he said.

They walked. The ramp went down and turned and went down.

"I'll tell Jonah," said Teo. "If I get back. About the dog."

"I don't know what it means."

"It means they'd been arguing about getting a dog. He wanted one and Ben kept saying they didn't have room." She laughed, a short, raw sound. "Eleven years in a room full of algae and that's the thing he wanted us to get right."

Cas didn't answer. He found that he couldn't.

He found that grief, for him, had a peculiar shape. It came as arithmetic. He kept doing sums. A minute for them, a month for Ben. The hundred and twenty minutes they'd sat on the gallery floor. Eleven years, four months, give or take. The thirty seconds at the end, and how many years in them — sixty? A hundred? Ben's pole hadn't been able to read it. He did the sums over and over, the way you run your tongue over a broken tooth, and every time they came out the same, and every time it was unbearable, and he did them again.

And under all of them, one more. The one he did most. *One in twenty. Less.*

"I said yes," he said.

Teo didn't stop walking. "You did the sum," she said. "I watched you do it. You had the face." A few more steps. "It was a good sum. I'd have done the same one. It came out wrong." She was quiet for a while. "That's what odds are, Hale. If they never came out wrong, they'd be called something else."

He didn't answer. She didn't make him.

* * *

The Lattice lay below and inward, and to reach it they had to go down.

This was the part of the plan Cas had drawn on the back of a Ministry briefing sheet in the crisis room, a hundred years ago, yesterday: the Vault was a sphere, its floor was the Anvil, and the Anvil was pierced from below by fourteen great conduits — cooling and power trunks that ran down through the keelstone plate into the Works beneath. The Crown was at the top of the Vault, four kilometres up Spindle One. There was no way into the Vault from the side; the Lattice was solid keelstone all around. The only way in was up through the floor.

So they went down, into the Works under the Vault, through a region the old engineers had called the Undercroft, where everything was older and heavier and the Lattice's pull came from above as well as from the side and the gravity weather was worst of all.

It was in a shaft that the Undercroft nearly killed him.

It was an old lift shaft, the cage long gone, with a steel ladder running down one wall and a gridwork of beams crossing it every ten metres. They were climbing down it, Teo first, Cas above, the keels humming loud in the narrow space, and Teo was calling out the washers — dropping one every few rungs and watching it fall straight down the middle of the shaft, glinting in her helmet light, until it vanished — and then she dropped one and it went sideways.

"*Squall—*"

It hit. Down went sideways and then *up* and then sideways again, a great slow roll like a ship broaching, and Cas's hands were on the ladder and then they were not. He didn't let go. The ladder moved. Or he moved; there wasn't a way to tell. One moment he was clinging to steel rungs with the shaft below him, and the next the shaft was beside him and he was falling across it, and a beam came at him out of the dark, and he hit it back-first with his whole weight and the weight of the suit.

He heard the keel. Over everything — over Teo shouting his name, over the scream of the beam as the suit's plating ground along it, over his own breath — he heard the keel's note change. It went from a clean high hum to something rough, something with a rattle in it, like a coin in a spinning drum.

Then down swung back, and he was lying across the beam with his legs hanging over the shaft, and the note in his spine was wrong.

"Hale! Cas! Talk to me—"

"I'm here." He didn't move. He was afraid to. "I'm here. I'm all right."

"Your keel—"

"I know."

Teo was beside him on the beam — she'd climbed across, somehow, fast, in the suit — and had her light on his back. He heard her breath go in and not come out.

"The housing's cracked," she said. "There's a — there's a split down the side, about the length of my hand. There's light coming out of it. It's — is it supposed to do that? Hale, is it supposed to—"

"No."

He reached back with his gauntlet and touched it. Through the gauntlet, through the plating, he could feel the rattle — a tiny eccentric wobble in something that had been perfectly balanced. The bearing. The crack had let the housing flex, and the rotor had kissed its bearing, just once, just slightly, and now it was running out of true.

*A bad day*, Ben had said. *A very, very bad day.*

He looked at the band of indicator lights around the housing's waist, reflected in Teo's visor. Green. Green. One amber.

"How long?" said Teo.

"It'll run down faster. The bubble'll shrink as it slows. Hours, instead of days. Maybe."

"And if it goes?"

"If the bearing goes—" He didn't finish. "It won't go. It'll just run down."

Teo looked at him through two visors.

"You're lying," she said.

"A bit."

"Right." She got her shoulder under his arm. "Up."

* * *

He walked. He kept walking. For the first hour after the shaft he could hear the rattle all the time, faint and patient in the keel's note, like a clock ticking in another room; after that he stopped hearing it, the way you stop hearing a clock, and only noticed it when the corridor went quiet and it was suddenly there, ticking, ticking. Teo heard it too. He could tell because she stopped talking whenever it was loud. She walked a little closer to him than she had before.

They came to the Lattice at the end of the twelfth hour.

He had seen it before, from inside, as a student — but only in pieces, through inspection ports, in schematics. He had never seen it like this. They came out of a tunnel onto a long gallery, a sort of balcony running along the outside of the Lattice's outermost ring, and the Lattice was simply there, filling the world. A wall. A black wall so high that its top was lost above them in the pearly dark and so long that it curved away on both sides into nothing, smooth and seamless and utterly lightless, as if someone had cut a slice out of the universe. It hummed. You didn't hear it; you felt it, in your chest, a vast deep tone below the hammer. It was keelstone, all of it — every cubic metre of that wall weighed as much as a locomotive — and they could feel it pulling at them, a steady sideways tug toward it, as if the wall were very gently trying to draw them in.

"God," said Teo.

Cas said nothing. He had found an inspection port — a round window of thick crystal set into the gallery's floor, looking down and in through a gap between two of the outer rings. He knelt by it and looked.

It went down a long way. Ring behind ring behind ring, black on black, each one a curved cliff, the gaps between them lit by that sourceless pearly light. And far down, far in, he could see one of the great tensioner rotors — the enormous spinning keelstone drums set into the rings that held the Lattice's shape. It was turning slowly. It was perfectly true.

And beyond it, deeper, where there should have been another—

"That's Six," he said.

Teo knelt beside him.

It was hard to see at first. Tensioner Six was the size of a building: a drum of black keelstone two hundred metres across, set on a magnetic bearing in a cradle in the Lattice. Only it wasn't in its cradle. It was lying tilted in the gap between two rings, like a coin that has fallen on its edge and leaned against a wall, one rim jammed against the ring above and the other sunk in the cradle below, and it was not turning. Around it, the rings themselves were wrong. They weren't curved properly. They sagged toward Six and bulged away from it, as though the whole Lattice around it had been squeezed in a fist and then let go. And through the gaps around it, very faintly, threads of that white-violet light came branching outward from somewhere deeper in.

"Can it be—" said Teo.

"No."

He said it without thinking. He'd known it since the crisis room, since the number at the bottom of the screen. He'd known it since the car. But he hadn't *seen* it, and now he had, and he found that seeing it made it into a thing that could be said out loud without any effort at all.

"It can't be reseated. Not from the Crown, not from anywhere. There's nothing to seat it in. The Lattice has lost its shape. Even if you stood it back up it would just fall over again." He sat back on his heels. "Every engineer in that room knew it. Rask probably knows it. They all said it anyway."

"You said it."

"I said it."

Teo got up. She stood looking out at the black wall, her arms folded over the chest of her suit.

"It's funny," she said. "I've been in Civil Defence twenty-nine years. Floods, fires, the Kovač stack. Every time, the Ministry says it's under control and we go in and it isn't. Every time. And you know what? Every time, I believed them a bit. Right up until I saw it. Every time." She shook her head. "I used to think I was an idiot for that. I've decided it's worse than that. I've decided it's the whole bloody country."

* * *

Lattice Station 14-C was a control booth for Conduit 14, and they found it an hour later by following Ben's map.

That was the thing. Cas had not been sure, until they found it, whether the map was real — whether a man in a room, however clever, however many years he'd had, could have reasoned his way from the branching of a filament to the shape of a thing he'd never seen. But Ben had drawn the Lattice's stress lines, and where they ran, and where they crossed the conduits, and on page twenty-six of the sketchbook, in Cas's pencil copying Ben's grease-pencil, there was a circle around one conduit and three words: *14 — THROAT HERE*. And when they came round the curve of the Lattice's inner gallery, there was the door. LATTICE STATION 14-C. CONDUIT 14 ACCESS. AUTHORISED PERSONNEL ONLY.

And there was a lee. They felt it as they came through the door: the pull of the black wall let go of them, and the gravity weather, which had been gusting all through the Undercroft, simply stopped. The booth was a small, bare room with a bank of dead monitors along one wall and a big thick viewport in the other, and a hatch in the floor by the viewport, and a chair, and a water cooler, and a first-aid cabinet, and it was as calm and still and ordinary as an office on a Sunday afternoon.

Teo dropped a washer. It fell straight down and bounced.

"Station air," said Cas, checking the panel by the door. "Pressure's good. You could take the helmet off in here."

"Could I," said Teo. She had gone to the viewport. "Hale. Come and look at this."

He went.

The viewport looked into the bottom of Conduit 14. It was a vertical shaft, round, maybe six metres across, lined with black keelstone and ribbed with the brackets of old cooling pipes that had been stripped out long ago. A steel ladder went up one side of it. The shaft went up and up, lit by the pearly glow, and at the top of it, very far up, there was a disc of light that was brighter than anything they'd seen, a pale, hard, violet-white.

And about forty metres up the shaft, something was happening to the air.

It trembled. That was the only word. It was like the shimmer over the Pit, the column of moving air they'd seen from the rim; but here it was close, and dense, and you could see it for what it was. A layer. A horizontal curtain across the shaft, a few metres thick, where the air was — *wrong*. Where things seen through it jumped and smeared and doubled. Where the ladder, going up through it, seemed to bend and break and join again, as though you were looking at it through moving water, or through the heat of a fire, or through tears.

"The Shear," said Teo.

"A hole in the dam," said Cas.

He could see it now, the whole shape of it, the way he'd seen it in the locker-room glass. The Lattice was holding the entire difference between the core and the Works, sitting in its keelstone the way the drop had sat in that little square window. And here, where the conduit went through, the difference had nowhere to be but in the air.

*The whole Vault's a blister*, he thought. *And we're standing at the door.*

Cas looked at his visor display. He'd dropped a relay puck at the booth door; the suit was comparing its own clock with the puck's, with the one before, with the Collar's. LOCAL RATIO: 1.9.

He looked up at the trembling curtain. If Ben's map was right — if everything he'd guessed in the crisis room was right — then on the far side of it the ratio wasn't two. It was hundreds. It might be thousands.

"Forty metres," he said. "From two to — I don't know. Seven hundred? Across forty metres."

"And the keel spreads it over one."

"Yes."

"And yours is broken."

He didn't answer.

"Hale," said Teo. "Look down."

He looked down.

At the bottom of the shaft, on the floor of the conduit just beyond the viewport, lying in a heap against the foot of the ladder, was a body.

* * *

They opened the floor hatch and went down to it, and Teo knelt beside it with her light, and for a long time neither of them said anything at all.

It had fallen. That much was clear. It lay crumpled at the foot of the ladder, the way a body lies when it's come down a long way. It was very small — shorter than Teo — and very broad; even in death, even ruined, you could see the width of the shoulders, the thickness of the bones.

And it was ruined in a way that made no sense.

The legs were bone. Clean bone, grey and dry and old, the clothes over them rotted to threads. The pelvis was bone. But from the waist up, the body was *leather* — dark brown, mummified, shrunken tight on the frame, like the bog bodies Cas had seen as a boy in the Museum of the Long Heat — and the face was worse, because the face was not quite either. The left side of it was dry skin over a skull. The right side, from the cheekbone to the chin, was — Cas made himself look — was still almost a face. Wasted, sunken, grey. But skin. Hair. An eyelid.

"It went through," Cas said. His own voice sounded strange to him. "Through the Shear. Coming down. Different parts of it spent different amounts of time in there. It was on the ladder, it — no." He stopped. He made himself think. He looked at the legs, and then at the face. "It fell. It came off the ladder and turned over, and it came through head first. The head came *out* first. Into the slow side, down here. The legs were still up in the fast side. For a second, maybe. Maybe less. The legs lived — years? Longer? — while the head lived a second." He heard himself. "It was dead before it got here. It was dead before it finished falling."

Teo said nothing.

"But that's not—" He stopped again.

"That's not what's wrong," said Teo. "Is it."

"No."

She reached out with her gauntlet and, very gently, touched the clothes.

They weren't station clothes. That was what was wrong. They weren't a jumpsuit or overalls or a lab coat or anything issued by the Ministry to anyone who had ever worked in the Draw. They were a sort of loose tunic, belted, and trousers, and they had been made. By hand. You could see the stitching — big, careful, uneven stitches, the kind you'd make with a needle you'd filed yourself, and the thread was something coarse, twisted from fibres. And the cloth itself was a patchwork. Squares and strips of a dozen different fabrics: orange high-vis, grey jumpsuit cotton, a white that might once have been a lab coat, a blue that might once have been a curtain, all sewn together, and patched, and re-patched, and patched again over the patches, as if the garment had been worn and mended for years. For decades. By more than one person.

Around the neck, on a cord of the same twisted fibre, hung a little ornament.

Teo lifted it with her gauntlet. It was yellow plastic — the thick, glossy plastic of an old-style hard hat — carved into a shape: a tiny, flat, stylised flame, or a lamp, with rays around it. Somebody had carved it very carefully with a knife, and then it had been handled — held, rubbed, worried between finger and thumb — for so long that all the edges were worn smooth and round and the yellow had gone pale and soft as old soap.

"Hale," said Teo, very quietly. "Who is this?"

He had been doing arithmetic since they came through the hatch. He'd been doing it without meaning to, the way he always did, the way he'd done it in the crisis room with Rask telling him to stop. The body had fallen down the conduit. So it had come from above. From inside. From the Vault. It had come through the Shear. It wore clothes that had been sewn by hand, from station fabric, and worn and mended for longer than the station had been broken. It wore a lamp carved from a hard hat. Its bones were thick. Its bones were very, very thick, and it was very small and very broad, the way a person might grow up — the way a *child* might grow up — somewhere the gravity was high.

*If anyone survived in there*, he had said to Rask, *they could have been alive in there longer than the Commonweal has existed.*

"They lived," he said.

"Who did?"

"The night shift. Some of them. In the Vault. They lived. They had air — the Vault's got air, it's a sealed volume, it's got scrubbers. Water. Light. They lived." He looked at the patched sleeve, the fine careful stitches of someone who had had all the time in the world to learn to sew. "And they had children. And the children had children. And somebody — decades on, for them — got dressed in the clothes their grandmother mended and hung this round their neck and came down a ladder to find out what was at the bottom."

Teo was very still.

"Hale," she said. "What's in there?"

He looked up the shaft, at the trembling curtain of the Shear, and above it, the far bright disc of light.

"People," he said. "Or there were."

* * *

It took her about ten minutes to decide. It took him most of those ten minutes to realise she'd decided.

He was checking his keel. He'd found a way to see into the crack in the housing with his helmet light turned down, and through it he could see the rim of the rotor going round, a black blur edged with a thin bright line where it was catching the light, and every time it came round there was the tiniest flicker — a wobble, a stutter, the bright line jumping. He counted the stutters. He timed them against the keel's note. The note had dropped since the shaft. He could hear it had dropped.

He did the arithmetic. He didn't like it.

"How long," said Teo behind him.

"Four hours. Five. Before the bubble's too small to matter."

"And in the Shear?"

He didn't answer.

"In the Shear, Hale. Forty metres of it. Through the throat, on the map, where it's gentlest. With a keel with a crack down the side and a wobble in the bearing. Going up."

"The throat's gentler. Ben said—"

"Ben said *gentler*. Ben didn't say gentle." Her voice was very calm. "If that bearing goes in there, what happens?"

He closed his eyes.

"The rotor comes loose," he said. "It's eleven kilos of keelstone at two hundred thousand revs. It goes through the housing and the suit and me and the conduit wall and probably the Lattice. And the bubble goes with it. So the Shear gets what's left." He opened his eyes. "Either way, I'd be dead before I noticed."

"Right," said Teo. "So you're taking mine."

He turned round.

She was standing by the viewport with her helmet off. He hadn't heard her take it off. Her face was grey with tiredness and streaked where she'd been crying at the glass, and her cropped hair was flattened with sweat, and she looked older than she had at the gate and very much more certain.

"No," he said.

"The housings come off. Farr showed me on the rig. They're built to hot-swap, that's what the spin rigs were for, you spin them on the rig and clip them on. Four clamps and a coupling. We take yours off and put mine on. Five minutes."

"And you've got a suit with no keel, in the Lattice, four kilometres from the gate."

"I've got a suit with a broken keel, in a lee, with station air and a water cooler and a first-aid kit and a chair." She jerked her head at the room. "I'll stay here. It's calm here. I'll sit in the chair. I'll be the most comfortable person in the whole bloody Draw."

"And when the lee moves?"

"Then I'll move with it." She shrugged. "I'm good at moving. I've been doing it all day."

"Teo—"

"Listen to me." She came across the room. She had to look up at him. "Listen. You're the one who knows what's up there. You're the one who's read the paper. You're the one who knows what the Clause is and what the plate means—"

"I *don't* know what the plate—"

"You know more than me. Which is everything. I don't know how to turn a key in a Keystone. I don't know what a Keystone *is*. I don't know how to talk to people who wear their grandmother's curtains. I can carry you. That's what I'm for. I carry people out of things. And I can't carry you through that." She pointed at the viewport. At the trembling air. "So I'm giving you my keel instead. And you're going to take it. Because if you don't, you're going to try to go through on yours, and you'll die in there, and I'll have to sit in this bloody chair and watch it, like I watched that boy. And I'm not doing that twice in one day, Hale. I'm not."

He looked at her.

He wanted to say: *it should be me who stays*. He wanted to say: *I'm the one who signed it. I'm the one who owes*. He had come in here — he knew it, he had known it at the window in the Collar, *at last*, *almost a relief* — he had come in here half hoping that something would happen in the dark that would settle it. He had been thinking, since the shaft, since the rattle in the keel, that perhaps this was how it would be settled. Quietly. In the Shear. Tidily. And he'd been thinking, underneath that, very quietly, that it would be fair.

She saw it. He watched her see it. Something changed in her face, and she stepped in closer, and her voice dropped.

"Oh, no you don't," she said. "No. I read your file. I sat next to you all day. I know that look. I've seen it on men on bridges." She put her bare hand flat on the chestplate of his suit, where Petra Imre had put hers at the gate. "Guilt's a lousy reason, Hale. It's the worst reason there is. It makes you stupid and it makes you selfish and it makes you think dying is a thing you *do*, instead of a thing that happens. Die for something useful or don't bother. You hear me? If you're going to die in there, you do it because it saves somebody. Not because it makes you feel square. Nobody's keeping score. Nobody ever was."

He didn't say anything. He couldn't.

"Now turn round," said Teo, "and let me get that bloody thing off your back."

* * *

It took eleven minutes, not five.

They did it in the middle of the booth, in the lee, with Cas on his knees and Teo behind him. She had to take her own suit's back plate off first to get at the clamps — she did it by feel, swearing, with her helmet under her arm — and then his. The keels had to come off at full spin; there was no way to stop them. When the first clamp let go on his housing the whole thing *lurched* in her hands, gyroscopic, fighting her, and for a second it swung sideways and he felt the bubble around him go lopsided and the room went strange — the far wall stretching, the floor tilting, the light doing something he didn't have words for — and Teo said *hold still hold still hold still*, and got the second clamp, and the third, and then it was off, and his back was cold and light and the world was just a room.

She lowered it to the floor. It sat there, humming its wrong, rattling note. Through the crack in its side a thin bright line of light went round and round.

Then she took off her own.

She did it the same way, slower, more carefully, and when it was off she was standing in the middle of the booth with no keel at all, and for the first time Cas saw what that meant. The lee was calm — but not perfectly calm. Nothing in here was. Without the bubble around her, he could see her sway, very slightly, as if standing on the deck of a ship at anchor. He saw her put a hand out to the back of the chair.

"Right," she said. "Right. Hold still."

She clipped it onto him. Four clamps. The coupling. The suit took it with a thunk, and the note came into his spine — clean, high, true, without a rattle, the note from the hangar — and the room went steady around him like a held breath.

He stood up. Teo stepped back.

"Good," she said. "That's good. How's that feel?"

"Like yours."

She laughed. She put her back plate back on. She put her helmet on the chair, and sat down in it, heavily, and looked at him.

"You'll drop pucks," she said. "One at the bottom, one at the top."

"Yes."

"And you'll talk to me."

"If the radios—"

"You'll *talk* to me, Hale. You'll send me news. I'll be sitting here in this chair with nothing to do, I'll be bored out of my skull." She tipped her head back and looked at the ceiling. "Farr said the — the ratio thing. If you're up there in the fast bit and I'm down here in the slow, what does that do?"

"Anything I send you arrives squashed. Ten minutes of me in a couple of seconds. You'll hear it as a chirp. The suit'll stretch it back out."

"Birds," said Teo. "Farr said whales. So I get birds."

"And anything you send me arrives stretched. A minute of you would take — hours. Six, maybe. More."

"So you'll be up there for weeks, and I'll be down here for an hour or two."

"Yes."

She was quiet for a moment.

"That's all right," she said. "I've waited longer than that for a relief crew."

He knelt down beside the chair so he could see her face. He didn't know what to say. He had not had a friend in fifteen years, and he'd known her for a day and a half, and she had just given him the only thing she had that could keep her alive, and he did not know what you said to that.

"Teo," he said.

"Don't," she said. "I'll cry, and then I'll have to kill you." She put her hand on the side of his helmet and held it there. "Go on, then. Go and find your people in their curtains. And when you've found them, you tell them there's a woman down here in a chair who wants to hear all about it."

He put his arms around her.

It was absurd. It was two people in armoured suits, one of them kneeling, trying to hold each other through a centimetre of plating and a helmet ring, clumsy as two statues embracing. She put her arms around him and patted his back plate twice, hard, the way you'd pat a horse. Then she pushed him away.

"Go on, then," said Teo Lisk.

* * *

He went down through the hatch and dropped a puck at the foot of the ladder, beside the body in its patchwork clothes. He looked at the little yellow lamp for a moment. Then he started to climb.

It was a ladder. That was all it was, for the first thirty metres. Rungs in his gauntlets, the keel humming clean and high in his spine, the black curved wall of the conduit sliding past, the hammer coming up through the rails. He climbed on the back side of the ladder, pressed close to the wall, because Ben's map said that was where the throat was — a narrow column of gentler gradient running up the conduit's rear face where two stress lines forked apart. He could see the trembling curtain above him, coming closer. Through it the ladder seemed to kink sideways and back. Above it, very far up, the pale bright disc.

"Talking to you," said Teo's voice in his ear. "Just so you know. I'll keep talking till you're through. Tell me when you're there."

"Almost."

"How's the view?"

"Ladder."

"Very good. Very descriptive. They'll put you on the radio."

He reached the curtain.

He'd known it would be bad. He had done the arithmetic. He had not understood — because arithmetic can't make you understand it — what it would be like to have a body in two places in time.

It started with his hands. He reached up for the next rung and his right hand was *there* before he'd reached — he saw it, in his helmet light, already closed on the rung, already gripping, while he could still feel the motion of reaching in his shoulder. He stared at it. It was his hand. It was ahead of him. It was living a little faster than the rest of him and it had got to the future first. And then his arm caught up, and his hand was his again, and the next rung was above him and his hand was already on it.

He climbed. There was nothing else to do. He pressed himself against the wall where Ben had said and climbed, and the keel's note went higher and higher in his spine as it fought, and the world came apart.

Sound went first. He heard his boot hit the rung below him, and then he felt his foot move to put it there. He heard his own breath in the helmet and then felt his chest rise. He heard Teo's voice in his ear saying *— still with me? —* and it was wrong, it was *slow*, it was sliding down like a record played at the wrong speed, *— stiiill wiiith meee —*, and then he heard his own voice answering *— yes —* and didn't remember saying it, and then felt his mouth make the word.

His heart. He could hear his heart. He could hear it the way you'd hear footsteps in the room above: first the beat, then, a moment later, the feeling of it in his chest. An echo, but the wrong way round. His blood was arriving at his head before it left his heart. Something in him understood that this could not go on for very long, that there was a limit to how much of this a body could be asked to do, and that he was coming up to it.

The ladder went up through the curtain. The curtain was forty metres thick. He was halfway.

His thoughts came finished. That was the strangest part. He'd be climbing, and a thought would be there in his head, whole and complete — *the rung is loose* — and only afterwards would he feel himself think it, feel the words assemble, feel himself notice the rung. It was as though some part of him up ahead in time were leaving notes for the rest of him to find.

He put his hand on the loose rung. He'd known it was loose. He moved his hand to the next one.

Teo's voice was a groan now. It came in his ear like the lowing of some enormous animal a long way off, a vast, slow, falling note with no words in it, and he knew it was her, still talking, still saying *still with me* and *how's the view* and *nearly there*, and he could not understand any of it, and he held on to the sound of it anyway, the way you'd hold on to a rope in the dark. It went down and down. It went below anything he could hear. It became a pressure in his ears. Then it was gone.

And then — like coming up out of deep water — he was through.

It was over as suddenly as it had begun. His hand closed on a rung and he felt it close at the same time. His breath went in and his chest went up together. His heart beat, and he heard it beat, at once, one thing. He hung on the ladder, his helmet pressed against the black wall, shaking so hard the exo was fighting to hold him, and breathed, and breathed, and breathed.

He looked down. Below him, the Shear. Through it, the ladder going down, jumping and smearing. And far down at the bottom of the conduit, very small, the round lit window of Lattice Station 14-C, and in it, a figure in a chair.

She wasn't moving. She was sitting perfectly still, like a photograph. Like a statue. He watched her for a long time and she did not move at all.

He took an orange puck from his belt and pressed it to the wall. It stuck.

"Teo," he said. "I'm through."

There was no answer. He hadn't expected one. She'd hear him almost at once; to her, his whole climb would be a blur and a squeak. But even if she answered the moment she heard, her few words would have to crawl up to him — he did the arithmetic — over the better part of an hour.

He looked up.

* * *

The conduit went on. Above the Shear it went up through the Anvil itself, through a plate of keelstone three hundred metres thick, and the climbing was different. There was no weather. There was only weight. It came on gradually and then it didn't stop — a steady, crushing, *reasonable* weight, as if the whole world were pressing on him from above, patient and enormous. The exo groaned. His arms shook. Every rung was a decision.

He climbed. He counted rungs, because counting was what he did. They were thirty centimetres apart; three hundred metres was a thousand of them. At a hundred, he stopped and rested with his arms looped through the ladder and the keel singing in his spine and his heart going like a fist on a door. At two hundred. At three hundred. At six hundred his arms were not his arms any more, they were two things he had borrowed, and he was using them anyway.

The light above got brighter. It was not the pearly glow of the Works. It was something harder, whiter, bluer — a light like a welding arc, like the Lesion in the Crown's window in the old footage, like the nail of light Iris Hollowell had driven through the world. It came down the shaft and lit the rungs white. He could not look straight up into it.

And there was sound. He noticed it gradually, the way you notice rain. Coming down the shaft, faint, under the hammer — which was not a hammer any more, he realised, not in here; it had stopped being a once-a-second blow somewhere back in the Shear and become something else, a long, slow tremor that came up through the rails every so often and lasted and lasted — under that, from above, there was a sound he knew and could not place. A murmur. A noise with a texture to it, rising and falling. Voices? No. Too many for voices. Water? No.

He climbed. Eight hundred. Nine hundred. The disc of light was very close now; he could see the rim of the conduit against it, a black circle, and something moving across it, some shadow, some shape.

At nine hundred and sixty rungs his right hand missed.

It just — didn't close. He reached, and the arm that he'd borrowed didn't have anything left in it, and his fingers brushed the rung and slid off, and for a moment he hung by his left hand alone with the weight of the whole world pulling on him and three hundred metres of keelstone and forty metres of Shear and two kilometres of Undercroft below him, and he thought, very clearly, the way thoughts had come in the Shear, whole and finished: *oh. Here, then.*

And a hand came down out of the light and took hold of his wrist.

It was a bare hand. Small, broad, very strong, the knuckles scarred. It closed on his wrist above the gauntlet with a grip like a vice. And a voice above him said, perfectly calmly, in an accent he'd never heard, all the vowels flattened and the consonants clipped:

"Don't let go. I've got you. Pim — get his other arm."
